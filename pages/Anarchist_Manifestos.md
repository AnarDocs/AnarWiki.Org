# Anarchist Manifestos

## Overview

**Anarchist manifestos** are public declarations of anarchist
principles, demands, and intentions directed at broad audiences — fellow
workers, oppressed peoples, or society at large. They differ from
<a href="Anarchist_Programmes" class="wikilink"
title="anarchist programmes">anarchist programmes</a> in both scope and
purpose: where a programme specifies the theoretical foundations,
organisational methods, and transitional arrangements of a particular
tendency or group, a manifesto announces a political position and calls
for action. Programmes are internal-facing documents for movement
building; manifestos are addressed outward, intended to persuade,
agitate, and recruit.

The distinction is not absolute. Several documents straddle the line —
<a href="Nestor_Makhno" class="wikilink" title="Nestor Makhno">Nestor
Makhno</a>'s 1918 manifesto functions partly as a programme for the
Makhnovist movement, and the 1884
<a href="La_Questione_Sociale" class="wikilink"
title="La Questione Sociale">La Questione Sociale</a> programme was also
a public-facing declaration. But the generic difference holds:
manifestos tend toward the rhetorical and polemical, programmes toward
the strategic and organisational.

The tradition stretches back at least to
<a href="Josiah_Warren" class="wikilink" title="Josiah Warren">Josiah
Warren</a>'s 1841 *Manifesto* and
<a href="Anselme_Bellegarrigue" class="wikilink"
title="Anselme Bellegarrigue">Anselme Bellegarrigue</a>'s better-known
1850 text, which is often cited as the first explicitly anarchist
manifesto, though Bellegarrigue was probably unaware of Warren's earlier
effort. Since then, anarchist groups and individuals across every
tendency — communist, syndicalist, individualist, feminist, green,
insurrectionary — have used the manifesto form to announce their
positions and differentiate themselves from other currents.

## Manifestos and Programmes

The manifesto form has its origins in bourgeois politics — most famously
<a href="Karl_Marx" class="wikilink" title="Marx">Marx</a> and
<a href="Friedrich_Engels" class="wikilink" title="Engels">Engels</a>'
1848 *Communist Manifesto* — but anarchists adopted and transformed it
early on. Where Marxist manifestos typically address a vanguard party
and outline a path to state power, anarchist manifestos generally reject
both. They address workers, the dispossessed, or humanity broadly, and
they call for the abolition of state and capital rather than their
seizure.

Programmes are more technical documents. They set out what a movement
believes, how it is organised, and what it proposes to do in concrete
terms — including in a revolutionary situation or transitional period. A
manifesto may contain programmatic elements, but its primary register is
declarative and agitational rather than strategic. The
<a href="Organisational_Platform_of_the_Libertarian_Communists"
class="wikilink"
title="Organisational Platform of the Libertarian Communists">Organisational
Platform of the Libertarian Communists</a> (1926) is a programme; the
London Anarchist Communist Alliance's 1895 *An Anarchist Manifesto* is a
manifesto. The difference lies less in length or specificity than in
intended audience and rhetorical function.

### 21st Century Concerns

21st century anarchist manifestos inherit the classical concerns —
abolition of state and capital,
<a href="workers&#39;_self-management" class="wikilink"
title="workers&#39; self-management">workers' self-management</a>,
<a href="federalism" class="wikilink" title="federalism">federalism</a>,
<a href="mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="mutual aid">mutual aid</a>
— but apply them to a substantially altered terrain. Several new
problems recur across recent texts.

The ecological crisis occupies a central place that it simply did not in
earlier periods. Where 19th and early 20th century manifestos
occasionally touched on land and resource questions, contemporary
documents treat climate breakdown as a defining condition of struggle.
The <a href="Green_Anticapitalist_Front" class="wikilink"
title="Green Anticapitalist Front">Green Anticapitalist Front</a>'s 2019
manifesto argues that ecological catastrophe is inseparable from
capitalist accumulation and state power, and cannot be addressed through
the same institutional frameworks that produced it. The
<a href="Organização_Socialista_Libertária" class="wikilink"
title="Organização Socialista Libertária">Organização Socialista
Libertária</a>'s founding documents similarly treat the environmental
question as structurally linked to labour exploitation and colonial
extraction rather than as a separate issue.

<a href="Intersectionality" class="wikilink"
title="Intersectionality">Intersectionality</a> — the recognition that
race, gender, sexuality, disability, and class produce overlapping and
mutually reinforcing structures of domination — runs through much recent
manifesto writing in ways absent from classical texts. The 2019
<a href="Chiara_Bottici" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchafeminism Manifesto">Anarchafeminism Manifesto</a> and the
1971 <a href="Chicago_Anarcho-Feminists" class="wikilink"
title="Chicago Anarcho-Feminists">Chicago Anarcho-Feminists</a>' earlier
founding text both insist that anarchism cannot limit itself to economic
and state critique: patriarchal structures require their own analysis
and their own forms of resistance.
<a href="Relationship_anarchy" class="wikilink"
title="Relationship anarchy">Relationship anarchy</a>, explored in
<a href="Mariia_Karhu" class="wikilink" title="Mariia Karhu">Mariia
Karhu</a>'s manifesto, extends this critique into intimate life,
rejecting hierarchies of affection and obligation as continuous with
political hierarchy.

Digital technology and surveillance capitalism present problems the
classical tradition could not anticipate.
<a href="McKenzie_Wark" class="wikilink" title="McKenzie Wark">McKenzie
Wark</a>'s *A Hacker's Manifesto* (2013) addresses the emergence of a
new class relation organised around information and intellectual
property rather than physical means of production. The
<a href="Anarchist_Federation_of_Cyber_Communes_(AF2C)" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist Federation of Cyber Communes (AF2C)">Anarchist
Federation of Cyber Communes (AF2C)</a>'s 2023 *Cyber-Anarchist
Manifesto* directly confronts state and corporate surveillance,
algorithmic control, and the concentration of digital infrastructure in
the hands of a small number of corporations and governments. The
<a href="Tangpingist_Manifesto" class="wikilink"
title="Tangpingist Manifesto">Tangpingist Manifesto</a> (2021), emerging
from China, applies an anarchist-adjacent framework of refusal and
withdrawal to the specific conditions of platform capitalism and
totalitarian surveillance.

Anti-colonialism and decolonisation feature more explicitly than in
earlier periods. The 2013 Tunisian anarchist text and the
<a href="Haraket_A&#39;ssyann" class="wikilink"
title="Haraket A&#39;ssyann">Haraket A'ssyann</a> *Manifesto of
Disobedience* (2012) both write from contexts of postcolonial state
formation and address forms of domination that European classical
anarchism often failed to theorise adequately.

The question of insurrectionary form — how anarchist resistance should
be organised, whether formal organisations are necessary, what
relationship exists between everyday refusal and open revolt — remains
contested. The 2020 *A New Anarchic Manifesto* reflects on contemporary
informal, decentralised, and international anarchist practice without
attempting to resolve these questions, treating the tension between
organisation and spontaneity as productive rather than a problem to be
eliminated.

### Manifestos in Fiction

Anarchist and anti-authoritarian manifestos have appeared as narrative
devices in fiction, most often as texts-within-texts that characters
write, conceal, or pass between themselves — mirroring the actual
circulation of clandestine political literature.

The most culturally significant example is
<a href="V_for_Vendetta" class="wikilink" title="V for Vendetta">V for
Vendetta</a>,
<a href="Alan_Moore" class="wikilink" title="Alan Moore">Alan Moore</a>'s
graphic novel (1982–85), subsequently adapted as a film by the
Wachowskis (2005). V's televised address to the people of Britain
functions as a de facto manifesto — a public declaration arguing that
the Norsefire state rules only through the compliance of the governed,
and that this compliance can be withdrawn. Moore, himself an anarchist,
was explicit that the work was written as anarchist propaganda against
Thatcherism.

#### V's Televised Address

From pages 112–18 of *V For Vendetta*, Alan Moore, 1982.

> Good evening, London. I thought it time we had a little talk. Are you
> sitting comfortably? Then I'll begin…
>
> I suppose you're wondering why I've called you here this evening.
> Well, you see, I'm not entirely satisfied with your performance
> lately… I'm afraid your work's been slipping, and… well, I'm afraid
> we've been thinking about letting you go.
>
> Oh, I know, I know. You've been with the company a long time now.
> Almost… let me see. Almost ten thousand years! My word, doesn't time
> fly? It seems like only yesterday…
>
> I remember the day you commenced your employment, swinging down from
> the trees, fresh-faced and nervous, a bone clasped in your bristling
> fist… "Where do I start, sir?" you asked, plaintively. I recall my
> exact words: "There's a pile of dinosaur eggs over there, youngster,"
> I said, smiling paternally the while. "Get sucking."
>
> Well, we've certainly come a long way since then, haven't we? And yes,
> you're right, in all that time you haven't missed a day. Well done,
> thou good and faithful servant.
>
> Also, please don't think I've forgotten about your outstanding service
> record, or about all the invaluable contributions that you've made to
> the company… fire, the wheel, agriculture… it's an impressive list,
> old-timer. A jolly impressive list. Don't get me wrong.
>
> But… well, to be frank, we've had our problems, too. There's no
> getting away from it.
>
> Do you know what I think it stems from? I'll tell you: it's your basic
> unwillingness to get on within the company. You don't seem to want to
> face up to any real responsibility, or to be your own boss. Lord
> knows, you've been given plenty of opportunities… We've offered you
> promotion time and time again, and each time you've turned us down. "I
> couldn't handle the work, Guv'nor," you wheedled. "I know my place."
>
> To be frank, you're not trying, are you? You've been standing still
> for far too long, and it's starting to show in your work… and, I might
> add, in your general standard of behaviour.
>
> The constant bickering on the factory floor has not escaped my
> attention, nor the recent bouts of rowdiness in the staff canteen.
>
> Then of course there's… hmm. Well, I didn't really want to have to
> bring this up, but… well, you see, I've been hearing some disturbing
> rumours about your personal life. No, never you mind who told me. No
> names, no pack drill… I understand that you are unable to get it on
> with your spouse. I hear that you argue. I am told that you shout.
> Violence has been mentioned. I am reliably informed that you always
> hurt the one you love, the one you shouldn't hurt at all.
>
> And what about the children? It's always the children who suffer, as
> you're well aware. Poor little mites. What are they to make of it?
> What are they to make of your bullying, your despair, your cowardice
> and all your fondly nurtured bigotries?
>
> Really, it's not enough, is it?
>
> And it's no good blaming the drop in work standards upon bad
> management, either… though, to be sure, the management is very bad. In
> fact, let us not mince words… the management is terrible! We've had a
> string of embezzlers, frauds, liars, and lunatics making a string of
> catastrophic decisions. This is plain fact.
>
> But who elected them?
>
> It was you! You who appointed these people! You who gave them the
> power to make decisions for you! While I'll admit that anyone can make
> a mistake once, to go on making the same lethal errors century after
> century seems to me nothing short of deliberate.
>
> You have encouraged these malicious incompetents, who have made your
> working life a shambles. You have accepted without question their
> senseless orders. You have allowed them to fill your workspace with
> dangerous and unproven machines. You could have stopped them. All you
> had to say was "no."
>
> You have no spine. You have no pride. You are no longer an asset to
> the company.
>
> I will, however, be generous. You will be granted two years to show
> some improvement in your work. If at the end of that time you are
> still unwilling to make a go of it… you're fired.
>
> That will be all. You may return to your labours. Normal service will
> be resumed as soon as possible.

In television, the character Karis Nemik in the
*<a href="Andor" class="wikilink" title="Andor">Andor</a>* series (2022)
writes an explicitly anti-authoritarian manifesto throughout the
narrative. After his death, the completed text is passed to
<a href="Cassian_Andor" class="wikilink" title="Cassian Andor">Cassian
Andor</a>, and excerpts are read aloud in the finale — arguing that
imperial power rests ultimately on the compliance of the oppressed, and
that this compliance is always revocable. The device of a manifesto
written in secret, completed posthumously, and passed hand to hand
closely mirrors the actual circulation of clandestine anarchist
literature, and the text has attracted genuine interest from anarchist
readers and commentators.[^1]

#### Nemiks' Manifesto

> There will be times when the struggle seems impossible. I know this
> already. Alone, unsure, dwarfed by the scale of the enemy.
>
> Remember this. Freedom is a pure idea. It occurs spontaneously and
> without instruction. Random acts of insurrection are occurring
> constantly throughout the galaxy. There are whole armies, battalions
> that have no idea that they've already enlisted in the cause.’
>
> Remember that the frontier of the Rebellion is everywhere. And even
> the smallest act of insurrection pushes our lines forward.
>
> And then remember this: the Imperial need for control is so desperate
> because it is so unnatural. Tyranny requires constant effort. It
> breaks, it leaks. Authority is brittle. Oppression is the mask of
> fear.
>
> Remember that. And know this, the day will come when all these
> skirmishes and battles, these moments of defiance, will have flooded
> the banks of the Empire's authority and then there will be one too
> many. One single thing will break the siege.
>
> Remember this. Try.

A further curiosity is the emergence of AI-generated anarchist
manifestos — texts produced by language models rather than political
actors. These raise genuine questions about the genre's core function as
an act of political commitment: a manifesto is in part a declaration by
a subject who accepts responsibility for it, and it is unclear what it
means for that declaration to have no such subject.[^2]
