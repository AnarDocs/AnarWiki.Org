# Campism

## Overview

**Campism** is the political tendency to divide the world into two
opposing geopolitical blocs — an "imperialist camp" led by the United
States and the West, and an "anti-imperialist camp" of states opposing
them — and to demand that the left support the latter regardless of
their internal character. Campists hold that the primary axis of global
conflict is between these blocs, and that this overrides questions of
class, democracy, or human rights within the states of the supposedly
anti-imperialist side.

The term is associated with the
<a href="Cold_War" class="wikilink" title="Cold War">Cold War</a>
division of the world into competing superpower blocs, but it persists
today in sections of the Western left that offer uncritical backing to
states such as Russia, China, Iran, Syria, Venezuela, and North Korea —
not on the basis of any socialist or libertarian content in these
states, but solely because they stand in opposition to Washington.

From an
<a href="anarchism" class="wikilink" title="anarchist">anarchist</a>
perspective, campism is not anti-imperialism at all. It is a statist
ideology that relocates the centre of political analysis from class
struggle and popular self-determination to the manoeuvring of rival
state powers. It reproduces the same logic it claims to oppose: rather
than dismantling imperialism as a system, it merely picks a side within
that system. As Syrian-British anarchist
<a href="Leila_Al-Shami" class="wikilink" title="Leila Al-Shami">Leila
Al-Shami</a> has put it, campism represents "the anti-imperialism of
idiots."[^1]

## Origins

### The Soviet Camp and Cold War Logic

The roots of campism lie in the Stalinist transformation of the
Communist International during the 1930s. After
<a href="Joseph_Stalin" class="wikilink" title="Joseph Stalin">Joseph
Stalin</a> consolidated control over the Soviet state and the
international communist movement, affiliated parties around the world
were expected to subordinate their politics to the foreign policy needs
of the USSR. Where Moscow pursued the
<a href="popular_front" class="wikilink" title="popular front">popular
front</a> against fascism, communist parties followed; where Moscow
signed the <a href="Molotov–Ribbentrop_Pact" class="wikilink"
title="Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact">Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact</a> with Nazi
Germany in 1939, Western communists fell silent or offered tortured
justifications. Support for "the camp" took precedence over independent
political analysis.

After the Second World War, the binary logic hardened. The world was
divided into a capitalist first camp led by the US and a "socialist"
second camp led by the USSR. Communist parties in Western countries
aligned with Moscow even when this meant defending the Soviet crushing
of workers' uprisings in East Germany (1953), Hungary (1956), and
Czechoslovakia (1968). The
<a href="tankie" class="wikilink" title="tankie">tankie</a> tendency —
named after those who supported the Soviet tanks sent into Budapest —
was the clearest expression of second campism: the view that whatever
the socialist camp did was, by definition, preferable to Western
imperialism.[^2]

The
<a href="third_camp" class="wikilink" title="third camp">third camp</a>
tradition — associated with
<a href="Max_Shachtman" class="wikilink" title="Max Shachtman">Max
Shachtman</a>,
<a href="Hal_Draper" class="wikilink" title="Hal Draper">Hal Draper</a>,
<a href="CLR_James" class="wikilink" title="CLR James">CLR James</a>,
and
<a href="Tony_Cliff" class="wikilink" title="Tony Cliff">Tony Cliff</a>
— argued against this logic from within the Trotskyist movement. Third
campists held that genuine socialist politics required independence from
both imperialist blocs, and that the Soviet Union under Stalin was
itself a form of class society whose crimes could not be excused by
anti-Western politics. Their central principle, drawn from Karl
Liebknecht, was: "The main enemy is at home."[^3]

### Fascist Roots of the "Proletarian Nation"

Campism's logic has uncomfortable parallels with the nationalist right.
Italian thinker Enrico Corradini developed the concept of the
"proletarian nation" in the early twentieth century — the idea that
rather than class struggle between workers and capitalists, the real
conflict was between oppressed nations and wealthy imperialist ones.
Mussolini built on this framework to argue that national solidarity must
replace class struggle. The same structure reappears in much campist
thinking: oppressed nations (however governed) stand against imperialist
nations, and the left must back the former unconditionally.

This framework shaped not only Italian fascism but also Maoist
<a href="Three_Worlds_Theory" class="wikilink"
title="Three Worlds Theory">Three Worlds Theory</a>, various
<a href="Marxist-Leninist" class="wikilink"
title="Marxist-Leninist">Marxist-Leninist</a> nationalisms, and — in a
contemporary form — the geopolitics of
<a href="Aleksandr_Dugin" class="wikilink"
title="Aleksandr Dugin">Aleksandr Dugin</a>, whose concept of
"multipolarity" maps almost exactly onto campist anti-imperialism while
drawing explicitly on fascist philosophical traditions. The overlap
between far-left campists and the far right on questions like Ukraine,
Syria, and Iran is not accidental — it follows from the shared statist
logic.[^4]

### Post-Cold War Mutation

After the Soviet collapse, campism did not disappear — it mutated.
Without a USSR to rally around, second campists shifted their allegiance
to whatever states antagonised Washington: Russia under Putin, Iran
under Khamenei, Assad's Syria, China under Xi, and Venezuela under
Maduro. The defining criterion was no longer socialist content but
anti-Western orientation.

Gilbert Achcar described this shift from Cold War campism to its modern
form as moving from "the enemy of my friend is my enemy" to "the enemy
of my enemy is my friend."[^5] Where the old campism at least maintained
the pretence of identifying with a socialist project, the new variety
simply inverts US foreign policy: whatever Washington opposes, campists
support.

## Tendencies

### The Selective Anti-Imperialist

The most common form of modern campism applies the label "imperialism"
exclusively or primarily to the United States and its allies. Other
imperial powers — Russia's wars in Chechnya and Ukraine, Iran's
projection of force through proxy militias across the Middle East,
China's suppression of Tibetans and Uyghurs, India's occupation of
Kashmir — receive little attention, are explained away as defensive
responses to Western provocation, or are actively denied.

This selectivity was stark in the Western left's response to the Syrian
civil war after 2011. While the Assad regime killed the vast majority of
Syrian civilians using barrel bombs, chemical weapons, mass torture, and
starvation sieges, large sections of the Western "anti-war" left
organised primarily against the possibility of Western intervention, not
against the ongoing slaughter. Russia's direct military intervention
from 2015 onward received little protest. Leila Al-Shami documented the
outcome: "The slogan 'Hands off Syria' really means 'Hands off
Assad'."[^6]

When the US bombed Raqqa in its campaign against ISIS — killing over a
thousand civilians and leaving 80% of the city uninhabitable by UN
estimates — no major anti-war organisation mounted significant protests.
The same organisations that would mobilise in the thousands against US
strikes on Assad's military facilities were silent when Washington
destroyed a Syrian city.[^7]

### The Axis of Resistance Myth

A particularly concentrated form of campism is the uncritical
glorification of the "Axis of Resistance" — the loose alignment of Iran,
the Assad regime, Hezbollah, Hamas, and allied groups — as a coherent
anti-imperialist force.

The problems with this framing are immediate on inspection. The Assad
regime participated in the US rendition programme, allowing the CIA to
send prisoners to Syria for torture. It has historically killed more
Palestinians than the Israeli state. It has never made serious military
efforts to reclaim the Israeli-occupied Golan Heights, preferring
instead to deploy its armed forces against internal dissent.[^8] Iran's
Islamic Republic — upheld by campists as a bastion of resistance —
executed at least 700 people in 2023, tortured and raped protesters
during the 2022 "Jin Jiyan Azadî" uprising, and suppresses Baloch,
Kurdish, and other minority populations through systematic state
violence.[^9]

For campists, none of this disqualifies these states from left
solidarity. The sole criterion is anti-Western rhetoric. The Iranian
state can execute hundreds of protesters and still be championed; the
Syrian revolution can mobilise millions of ordinary people against a
dictatorship and be dismissed as a Western-engineered colour revolution.

### Denialism and Conspiracy

Campist politics regularly produce — and depend on — the denial of
atrocities committed by the backed camp. When evidence of the Syrian
government's mass torture emerged through the Caesar photographs,
campist outlets dismissed them as fabrications. When chemical weapons
attacks on civilian areas were documented, campists insisted these were
false flags. When Iran's killings of protesters were reported, they were
attributed to Western propaganda.

This denialist pattern mirrors the conspiracy logic of the far right,
which is why the two frequently converge. During the 2018 US-UK-French
strikes on Syrian chemical weapons facilities, figures from the white
nationalist right and the campist left shared identical talking points,
promoted the same memes, and drew on the same Russian state media
sources.[^10] The political spectrum collapses when the only criterion
is opposition to Washington.

### Support for Popular Struggles Conditional on Geopolitics

Where popular uprisings occur in states aligned with the West, campists
tend to offer support. Where they occur in states of the
"anti-imperialist camp," campists suppress, undermine, or deny them.

The 2011 Syrian revolution was one of the most significant mass popular
movements of the twenty-first century, involving democratic
self-organisation, workers' councils, and civil society networks
operating under bombardment. Campists either ignored it or reframed it
as a foreign plot. The 2019 Lebanese uprising, which directly challenged
Hezbollah's grip on the sectarian state, was undermined by Hezbollah and
dismissed by Western campists as Western-managed instability. The 2022
Iranian uprising was met with silence or scepticism from many who would
readily platform anti-US struggles.[^11]

As the SWANA (Southwest Asian and North African) anarchist collective
Decolonize Anarchism put it, within the campist framework "it is
frequently sufficient for a cause to be rhetorically 'supported' by the
United States in order for it to be immediately discredited" —
regardless of whether the cause is a genuine mass movement against
oppression.[^12]

## Criticism

### From Anarchism

Anarchists reject campism at the root because it is a statist framework.
Campism does not challenge imperialism as a system — it treats the world
as a contest between rival state powers and demands that people take
sides between them. Rather than building international solidarity among
workers, oppressed peoples, and liberation movements, it subordinates
all of this to the diplomatic and military interests of particular
states.

The anarchist tradition has consistently argued for a "three-way fight"
analysis — recognising that the US empire, authoritarian rival powers,
and domestic fascism are all distinct enemies, none of which can be
treated as an ally against the others. Campism collapses this analysis
into a simple binary that serves nobody except the ruling classes of the
"anti-imperialist" states.[^13]

As William Gillis of the
<a href="Center_for_a_Stateless_Society" class="wikilink"
title="Center for a Stateless Society">Center for a Stateless
Society</a> put it, campism "doesn't just treat states as merely the
largest loosely congealing patterns in a wider and deeper ecology of
power relations, but centers them in a way that slices away all other
considerations." The result is that political analysis becomes
geopolitical analysis, solidarity becomes alliance with states, and the
people who suffer under those states become invisible.[^14]

### The Double Standard for State Violence

A consistent theme in criticism of campism is its radically unequal
application of political standards. Dan La Botz and Jason Schulman,
writing from a socialist internationalist perspective, argue that
campism "seldom addresses the internal class character of the nations of
the 'anti-imperialist camp'" and "almost never criticizes the
'anti-imperialist nations.'" The tests that campists apply to the US —
Is it democratic? Does it allow independent unions? Does it suppress
minorities? Does it wage aggressive wars? — are not applied to Russia,
China, Iran, or Syria.[^15]

This is not an inconsistency to be corrected — it is the logic of the
position. Once the world is divided into camps and one camp is
designated anti-imperialist by definition, no evidence of that camp's
behaviour can dislodge the designation. The Stalinist roots of this
approach are clear: the Soviet Union was defended as socialist
regardless of what it did, just as Russia, China, or Iran are defended
as anti-imperialist regardless of what they do.

### Erasure of Affected Peoples

Perhaps the most damaging practical consequence of campism is the
erasure of the populations actually living under the states being
backed. As Leila Al-Shami observed of the Syrian case, campism requires
"the complete dehumanization of Syrians" — treating them not as people
with their own political agency, struggles, and desires, but as pawns in
a geopolitical game whose real actors are Washington and Moscow.[^16]

The same logic applies wherever campism operates. Iranian feminists
fighting the Islamic Republic's mandatory hijab laws and execution of
protesters become assets of US regime-change strategy. Belarusian
workers striking against Lukashenko become NATO proxies. Myanmar's
Spring Revolution becomes a colour revolution funded by the West. In
every case, the actual people resisting actual oppression are written
out of the picture so that the geopolitical map can remain legible.[^17]

Ayça Çubukçu, writing from the perspective of socialist feminism, noted
that Iranian feminists like Sima Shakhsari have developed a politics
that simultaneously opposes the Islamic Republic's repression and
rejects Western sanctions and military intervention — a position that
campism cannot accommodate, since it can only recognise states as
actors, not movements.[^18]

### The Anarchist Alternative

Against campism, anarchists argue for a consistent anti-authoritarianism
that opposes all state violence, all imperialism, and all forms of
oppression regardless of which state or bloc perpetrates them. This
means supporting popular uprisings, workers' movements, and liberation
struggles in every country — including those whose governments are in
conflict with the US — while refusing to extend solidarity to the states
themselves.

This position requires more analytical work than campism. It means
grappling with difficult questions: how to support Palestinian
resistance without backing the Iranian state; how to oppose Russian
imperialism in Ukraine without aligning with NATO; how to oppose US
sanctions on Venezuela without pretending Maduro's government serves the
Venezuelan working class. These are genuine difficulties, and anarchists
do not pretend otherwise.

What anarchists insist is that these difficulties cannot be resolved by
collapsing into campist binary thinking. The answer to "which camp
should we back?" is: neither. The task is to build international
solidarity with people, not with states.
