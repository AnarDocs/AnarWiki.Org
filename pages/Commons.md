# Commons

**Commons** (also known as **Common**, **Collective** or **Community
Ownership**) refers to cultural and natural resources accessible to all
members of a community, including natural materials such as air, water,
land, and forests. These resources are managed collectively by
communities through democratic self-governance, rather than by the
<a href="state" class="wikilink" title="state">state</a> or through
<a href="Private_Ownership" class="wikilink"
title="private property">private property</a>. The commons represents a
fundamental alternative to both capitalist private ownership and state
control, embodying principles central to
<a href="anarchism" class="wikilink" title="anarchism">anarchism</a> and
<a href="libertarian_socialism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian socialism">libertarian socialism</a>.

## Definition

The commons can be understood as both a resource and a social practice.
As resources, commons include natural materials like grazing lands,
fisheries, and water sources, as well as cultural resources like
knowledge, software, and public spaces. As a practice, commoning refers
to the collective management of these resources through
community-created institutions and democratic processes.[^1]

Unlike <a href="open_access" class="wikilink" title="open access">open
access</a> resources (which anyone can use without restriction), commons
have a defined community of users who collectively establish rules for
access and use. This community self-governance distinguishes commons
from both unregulated open access and top-down state management.

The term derives from traditional English
<a href="common_land" class="wikilink" title="common land">common
land</a>, but the practice of commons management extends across cultures
and throughout human history. The Roman legal concept of *res communis*
(things common to all) recognised resources meant to be used and enjoyed
by everyone, distinct from *res publica* (public property managed by
government).[^2]

## Historical Commons

### Common Land Systems

Contrary to capitalist propaganda such as the concept of the
'<a href="Tragedy_Of_The_Commons" class="wikilink"
title="Tragedy of the Commons">Tragedy of the Commons</a>', historical
commons were highly successful systems of collective resource management
that functioned for centuries. In medieval England, common lands were
integral to manor systems, with communities developing sophisticated
customary laws to prevent overuse. Manorial courts enforced rules about
grazing limits, harvest timing, and resource allocation.[^3]

These systems were **not** examples of unregulated access leading to
depletion. They demonstrated that communities could effectively
self-govern shared resources without private ownership or state control,
directly refuting claims that collective ownership inevitably fails.

### Enclosure and Dispossession

The destruction of the commons came not through internal failure but
through violent
<a href="enclosure" class="wikilink" title="enclosure">enclosure</a> —
the privatisation of common lands by force. Beginning in the 16th
century and accelerating through the 18th and 19th centuries, European
governments (particularly in
<a href="British_Commons_History" class="wikilink"
title="Britain">Britain</a>) seized common lands and granted them to
private owners, dispossessing rural communities of their traditional
rights.

<a href="Karl_Marx" class="wikilink" title="Karl Marx">Karl Marx</a>
identified enclosure as a form of 'primitive accumulation' — the violent
separation of people from their means of subsistence necessary for
capitalism to develop. He noted that enclosure merely displaced problems
while putting increasing numbers in precarious conditions.[^4]

This historical pattern continues today as corporations and governments
use the false narrative of the
'<a href="tragedy_of_the_commons" class="wikilink"
title="tragedy of the commons">tragedy of the commons</a>' to justify
seizing indigenous lands, privatising water sources, and enclosing
digital resources.

## Successful Commons Management

### Elinor Ostrom's Research

Political scientist
<a href="Elinor_Ostrom" class="wikilink" title="Elinor Ostrom">Elinor
Ostrom</a> won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics for demonstrating that
commons frequently succeed where theory predicted they would fail. Her
extensive research documented hundreds of examples of communities
successfully managing shared resources for generations.[^5]

Ostrom identified eight principles for successful commons:

1.  Clearly defined boundaries — clear definition of the resource and
    who has rights to it
2.  Proportional equivalence — appropriation and provision rules adapted
    to local conditions
3.  Collective decision-making — most appropriators can participate in
    rule-making
4.  Monitoring — monitors are accountable to or are the appropriators
    themselves
5.  Graduated sanctions — rule violators face escalating consequences
6.  Conflict resolution — accessible, low-cost mechanisms for resolving
    disputes
7.  Recognition of rights — external authorities recognise the
    community's right to self-organise
8.  Nested enterprises — for larger resources, organisation in multiple
    layers with local control at the base[^6]

These principles align closely with anarchist organisational structures
emphasising decentralised decision-making, community accountability, and
federation.

### Contemporary Examples

**Swiss Alpine Commons** — Grazing lands in the Swiss Alps have been
collectively managed by farmer communities since 1517, demonstrating
centuries of successful commons governance.[^7]

**Mongolian Grasslands** — Research comparing land degradation in
Mongolia, Russia, and China found that Mongolian grasslands (managed
communally with seasonal movement) had only 9% degradation, while
Russian state-owned pastures had 75% degradation and Chinese
privatised/state pastures had 33% degradation. Collective management
proved far more sustainable.[^8]

**Maine Lobster Fishery** — The thriving Maine lobster industry operates
through community-created regulations, including harbour territories,
informal trap limits, and self-policing. Local fishers collectively
manage the resource with minimal government intervention.[^9]

**Acequia Systems** — In New Mexico, community-run Acequia Associations
manage irrigation water through collective responsibility, preserving
water as a common resource while reinforcing agricultural traditions and
democratic participation.[^10]

**Community Forests in Nepal** — Since the 1980s, Nepal has transferred
forest management to local communities. Community forest user groups
regulate harvesting, generate income for community development, and have
increased forest cover in many areas while advancing democratic
practices at the grassroots level.[^11]

**James Bay Beaver Hunting** — Indigenous communities in James Bay,
Quebec maintain extensive customary systems for regulating beaver
hunting territories, demonstrating effective resource sharing through
traditional commons management.[^12]

## Types of Commons

### Natural Resource Commons

Natural commons — land, forests, water sources, fisheries, grazing
areas, and the atmosphere — are the traditional commons that sustained
human communities for millennia before enclosure and privatisation. Key
natural commons today include water (rivers, aquifers, irrigation
systems), forests (timber, non-timber products, biodiversity),
fisheries, grazing lands, seeds and biodiversity (genetic resources for
agriculture), and the atmosphere (air quality and climate stability).

### Urban Commons

Urban commons reframe city resources around use value and community need
rather than market value.
<a href="David_Harvey" class="wikilink" title="David Harvey">David
Harvey</a> distinguishes between mere public spaces and true urban
commons — the latter emerge when citizens take collective political
action to assert control.[^13] Examples include occupied public squares
— <a href="Syntagma_Square" class="wikilink"
title="Syntagma Square">Syntagma Square</a> (Athens),
<a href="Tahrir_Square" class="wikilink" title="Tahrir Square">Tahrir
Square</a> (Cairo), <a href="Plaza_de_Catalunya" class="wikilink"
title="Plaza de Catalunya">Plaza de Catalunya</a> (Barcelona) —
transformed from public spaces to commons through revolutionary protest;
community gardens; self-managed social centres; streets reclaimed
through direct action; and squatted housing managed communally.

### Digital Commons

Digital commons encompass information and knowledge resources
collectively created and shared within communities, representing new
frontiers for commons-based organising that challenge hierarchical
production and enable <a href="horizontal_organization" class="wikilink"
title="horizontal organisation">horizontal organisation</a>.[^14]

Digital resources are non-rivalrous — they can be shared at minimal cost
without depletion — which enables peer-to-peer collaboration without
bosses or markets. Key examples include
<a href="Wikipedia" class="wikilink" title="Wikipedia">Wikipedia</a>
(collectively maintained encyclopedia representing what scholar
<a href="Yochai_Benkler" class="wikilink" title="Yochai Benkler">Yochai
Benkler</a> calls 'commons-based peer production'[^15]),
<a href="Free_and_open_source_software" class="wikilink"
title="Free and open source software">Free and open source software</a>
such as Linux and Mozilla,
<a href="open-source_hardware" class="wikilink"
title="open-source hardware">open-source hardware</a> like RepRap 3D
printers, open access publishing, and Creative Commons licensing.

### Cultural, Knowledge, and Global Commons

Cultural commons include literature, music, arts, film, and heritage
sites, resisting the commodification of culture and asserting collective
ownership of cultural production. Knowledge commons encompass research,
education, traditional ecological knowledge, and shared information,
challenging intellectual property regimes that privatise knowledge and
restrict access.

Global commons include resources that transcend national boundaries: the
oceans, Antarctica, outer space, and the atmosphere. These pose
particular challenges for commons governance given their scale, but also
represent areas where commons-based approaches offer alternatives to
both state sovereignty and corporate exploitation.

## Commoning as Practice

### Process Over Property

Contemporary theorists emphasise *commoning* as a verb — an active
practice rather than just a type of property.
<a href="David_Harvey" class="wikilink" title="David Harvey">David
Harvey</a> and others use this framing to highlight the commons as an
ongoing social process of collective governance rather than a static
entity.[^16]

Commoning involves creating institutions through democratic rule-making,
governing resources collectively, practising mutual aid and reciprocity,
resisting enclosure, and what Federici calls 'subjectivization' —
producing ourselves as common subjects through collective practice.[^17]
This process-oriented understanding connects commons to anarchist
principles of
<a href="direct_action" class="wikilink" title="direct action">direct
action</a>,
<a href="mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="mutual aid">mutual aid</a>,
and <a href="prefigurative_politics" class="wikilink"
title="prefigurative politics">prefigurative politics</a>.

### Social Norms and Governance

Commons governance relies on informal norms and social practice rather
than state enforcement or market mechanisms. Communities develop and
transmit customary knowledge about sustainable use, conflict resolution,
and collective responsibility. Monitoring and enforcement come from
within the community rather than from external authorities — a core
anarchist insight about the possibilities of self-governance.

## Feminist Perspectives

### Women and Commons

<a href="Silvia_Federici" class="wikilink"
title="Silvia Federici">Silvia Federici</a> articulates the centrality
of women to commons struggles in her essay 'Feminism and the Politics of
the Commons'. Women have historically been at the forefront of defending
commons as primary subjects of reproductive work — the labour of
sustaining life and community — making them both most vulnerable to
privatisation and most steadfast in resistance.[^18] This has taken many
forms: subsistence farming on common lands, defending communal water
sources, foraging for food and medicines, rotating savings and credit
associations (tontines), and collectivised childcare, cooking, and
eldercare.

### Enclosure and Patriarchy

Federici connects the enclosure of commons to the intensification of
patriarchal control. As common lands were privatised, women lost
traditional autonomy and economic independence — the destruction of
commons coincided with witch hunts and increased restrictions on women's
lives. Any effective commons movement, she argues, must centre women's
leadership in organising collective alternatives, particularly around
reproductive labour and the means of subsistence.

### Reproductive Commons

Feminist analysis highlights reproductive commons — the collective
resources and practices that sustain life: childcare, cooking,
healthcare, education, emotional labour. Federici calls for
collectivising this work as a commons rather than privatising it within
nuclear families or commodifying it through markets. This connects to
broader anarchist visions of communal living and
<a href="mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="mutual aid">mutual aid</a>
networks that socialise reproductive labour.

## Anarchist Analysis

### Commons and Anarchism

The commons embody anarchist principles of self-management, voluntary
association, horizontal organisation, direct democracy, federation, and
mutual aid. Communities govern resources directly without state control,
people freely organise to manage shared resources, decision-making power
is distributed among participants, and larger commons are organised
through nested layers maintaining local autonomy. Scholar Kevin Carson
identifies extensive anarchist themes in Ostrom's work, noting the
alignment between successful commons governance and anarchist organising
principles.[^19]

### Contemporary Anarchist Commons

**<a href="Rebel_Zapatista_Autonomous_Municipalities" class="wikilink"
title="Rebel Zapatista Autonomous Municipalities">Rebel Zapatista
Autonomous Municipalities</a>** — The Zapatista communities in Chiapas,
Mexico operate on commons principles, with land held communally and
decisions made through participatory assemblies. Their motto 'everything
for everyone, nothing for us' reflects commons values.

**<a href="Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria"
class="wikilink"
title="Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria">Autonomous
Administration of North and East Syria</a>** (Rojava) — The
revolutionary society in northern Syria practises democratic
confederalism with communal land ownership, cooperatives, and
commons-based resource management influenced by
<a href="libertarian_socialism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian socialism">libertarian socialism</a> and
<a href="ecosocialism" class="wikilink"
title="ecosocialism">ecosocialism</a>.

Both demonstrate that commons-based organising can function at
significant scale as real alternatives to state and capitalist systems.

### Squatting and Land Liberation

Movements practising direct action to reclaim land as commons include
<a href="Abahlali_baseMjondolo" class="wikilink"
title="Abahlali baseMjondolo">Abahlali baseMjondolo</a> (shack dwellers'
movement in South Africa fighting for housing as a commons), the
<a href="Landless_Workers&#39;_Movement" class="wikilink"
title="Landless Workers&#39; Movement">Landless Workers' Movement</a>
(MST, occupying unused land in Brazil and establishing collective
farms), <a href="Take_Back_the_Land" class="wikilink"
title="Take Back the Land">Take Back the Land</a> (liberating vacant
housing in the United States),
<a href="The_Land_is_Ours" class="wikilink" title="The Land is Ours">The
Land is Ours</a> (occupying unused land in the UK for community use),
and the <a href="Occupy_movement" class="wikilink"
title="Occupy movement">Occupy movement</a> (asserting public space as
commons through direct action). These movements prefigure anarchist
society by creating commons through collective action rather than
petitioning authorities.

## Defending the Commons

### Against Enclosure

The ongoing enclosure of commons continues through land grabs
(corporations and states seizing indigenous territories), privatisation
(selling public resources to private companies), intellectual property
regimes (copyright and patents enclosing knowledge), financialisation
(turning resources into tradeable assets), and development schemes
displacing communities in the name of progress. Defending commons
requires active resistance through direct action, legal challenges, and
building alternative institutions.

### The 'Tragedy' Narrative

The '<a href="tragedy_of_the_commons" class="wikilink"
title="tragedy of the commons">tragedy of the commons</a>' functions as
propaganda justifying enclosure. As detailed in that article, this
narrative is historically false and empirically disproven. It is wielded
to dispossess indigenous peoples, privatise resources, and undermine
collective alternatives to capitalism. Commons advocates must actively
counter it by highlighting successful commons and explaining how
privatisation and state control — not collective management — cause
resource depletion and environmental destruction.

### Strategies for Defence

Effective defence of commons draws on direct action (occupying lands,
blocking privatisation), legal recognition of community rights,
documentation of commons management practices and outcomes, building
alliances across movements and regions, creating new commons as
prefigurative projects, and education about successful commons history.

## Commons and Ecosocialism

Commons-based resource management offers solutions to ecological crisis
that neither state environmentalism nor green capitalism can provide.
Communities with direct relationships to resources practise sustainable
management because they depend on the long-term health of those
resources.

### Indigenous Commons

Indigenous peoples worldwide maintain commons-based resource management
systems developed over millennia, embodying sophisticated ecological
knowledge and sustainable relationships between humans and nature.
Douglas L. Johnson's research on nomadic pastoralists shows they
'balanced local stocking ratios against seasonal rangeland conditions in
ways that were ecologically sound' — yet 'the nomad was blamed for
problems that were not of his own making and were a product of alien
forces.'[^20] Defending indigenous commons means supporting indigenous
sovereignty and self-determination against state and corporate
enclosure.

### Climate Commons

The atmosphere represents a global commons threatened by capitalist
overproduction. Addressing climate change requires transitioning from
private and state control of resources to commons-based management that
prioritises ecological sustainability over profit. Peter Barnes proposes
'sky trusts' — commons-based approaches to atmospheric resources that
charge polluters and distribute proceeds to all, creating incentives for
reducing emissions.[^21]

## Building Commons

### Creating New Commons

Anarchists can create commons through land occupations (liberating
unused land for collective use), housing cooperatives (removing housing
from the market through collective ownership), worker cooperatives
(managing workplaces as commons), community centres, tool libraries,
skill sharing outside market systems, free schools, and free stores and
food banks distributing resources based on need. These projects
prefigure anarchist society and provide material support for resistance
struggles.

### Digital Commons Building

Creating digital commons involves free and open source software
development, open access publishing and education, platform
cooperativism (worker-owned alternatives to corporate platforms),
decentralised peer-to-peer infrastructure, Creative Commons licensing,
and open-source hardware and local manufacturing. Digital commons
connect traditional commons organising with contemporary technology,
enabling global collaboration while maintaining local autonomy.

### Coalition Building

Successful commons movements connect diverse struggles: land and housing
(rural and urban commons defenders), digital rights (free software and
knowledge advocates), labour movements (workers seeking collective
control), indigenous movements (defending traditional territories),
environmental justice (communities resisting pollution and extraction),
and feminist movements (collectivising reproductive labour). These
coalitions strengthen resistance to enclosure while building alternative
institutions.

## Challenges and Limitations

### Scale Questions

Critics question whether commons can function at the scale of modern
societies. Ostrom's research shows that nested, federated commons can
manage large resources through coordinated local control — a model
consistent with <a href="anarchist_federalism" class="wikilink"
title="anarchist federalism">anarchist federalism</a>. Large-scale
historical commons and contemporary examples like the Zapatistas and
Rojava demonstrate viability beyond small communities.

### External Threats

Commons face constant threats from state violence (police and military
suppression), corporate enclosure (privatisation schemes), market
pressures (economic incentives to commodify resources), legal systems
favouring private property over collective rights, and ideological
attacks portraying commons as failures. Defending commons requires
militant resistance and solidarity networks.

### Internal Challenges

Commons communities must address inequality (preventing hierarchies from
emerging within commons), inclusion (ensuring participation across
differences), conflict resolution (managing disputes without state
authority), free riders (members who benefit without contributing), and
adaptation to changing environmental and social conditions. These are
navigable through direct democracy, consensus decision-making, and
accountability structures.

## Vision

A society based on commons would feature workers collectively managing
workplaces, agriculture and housing organised communally, information
and culture freely accessible, democratic governance by those affected
by decisions, communities with direct stakes in local ecosystems, needs
met through cooperation rather than markets, and relationships based on
mutual aid rather than competition. This vision builds on centuries of
successful commons management and contemporary movements — it is not a
speculation but a generalisation from practice.
