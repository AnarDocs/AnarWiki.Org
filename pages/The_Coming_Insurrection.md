# The Coming Insurrection

## Overview

*The Coming Insurrection* (French: *L'insurrection qui vient*) is a
political tract written by
<a href="The_Invisible_Committee" class="wikilink"
title="The Invisible Committee">The Invisible Committee</a>, an
anonymous collective associated with the
<a href="Tiqqun" class="wikilink" title="Tiqqun">Tiqqun</a> journal.
First published in France in 2007 by Editions La Fabrique, it was
translated into English and published by
<a href="Semiotext(e)" class="wikilink"
title="Semiotext(e)">Semiotext(e)</a> in 2009. The book argues that
capitalist civilisation is in terminal crisis and that the task facing
radicals is not to reform or negotiate with it but to accelerate its
collapse through the formation of autonomous communes and targeted
sabotage of infrastructure.

The text became widely known in France following the arrest of
<a href="the_Tarnac_Nine" class="wikilink" title="the Tarnac Nine">the
Tarnac Nine</a> — nine individuals, including
<a href="Julien_Coupat" class="wikilink" title="Julien Coupat">Julien
Coupat</a>, detained in the rural village of Tarnac on 11 November 2008
and accused of sabotaging overhead electrical lines on the national rail
network. The French Interior Minister described the book as a "manual
for terrorism" and cited it as evidence against those arrested. In the
United States, right-wing television host Glenn Beck repeatedly promoted
it to his audience as a dangerous revolutionary manual, inadvertently
boosting its sales and broadening its readership considerably.[^1]

*The Coming Insurrection* is the first in a series of texts by the
Invisible Committee, followed by *To Our Friends* (2015) and *Now*
(2017).

## Summary

The book is structured in two halves. The first moves through what the
authors call the "seven circles" of alienation produced by capitalism
and the state: the self, social relations, work, the urban environment,
the economy, ecology, and civilisation itself. The diagnosis is total.
Work has colonised every other sphere of life even as workers become
increasingly superfluous to production. Social bonds have been reduced
to transactional arrangements. The city — or rather the *metropolis* —
is presented not as a place but as the spatial form of capitalist power
itself. The state sustains itself through violence it refuses to name as
such.

The second half turns prescriptive. Against all forms of conventional
political organisation — parties, unions, NGOs, protest movements — the
authors propose the *commune* as the basic unit of revolutionary life.
Communes are not ideological groupings but simply people who find each
other, share a place and a set of commitments, and begin to take care of
themselves outside and against the institutions of capitalist society.
They are roughly analogous to what the broader anarchist tradition calls
<a href="affinity_groups" class="wikilink"
title="affinity groups">affinity groups</a>.

From this base, the text moves toward a theory of insurrection: blocking
the economy, rendering police operations irrelevant, seizing local power
in moments of crisis. The authors point to the
<a href="2005_riots_in_France" class="wikilink"
title="2005 riots in France">2005 riots in France</a>, the
<a href="2006_Oaxaca_uprising" class="wikilink"
title="2006 Oaxaca uprising">2006 Oaxaca uprising</a>, the
<a href="piquetero" class="wikilink" title="piquetero">piquetero</a>
movement in Argentina, and the grassroots relief efforts in New Orleans
following Hurricane Katrina as partial examples of the kind of
spontaneous collective capacity they have in mind. The text closes with
the injunction: "All power to the communes!"[^2]

The book's intellectual debts are primarily to
<a href="Tiqqun" class="wikilink" title="Tiqqun">Tiqqun</a> and the
French radical philosophy tradition:
<a href="Guy_Debord" class="wikilink" title="Guy Debord">Guy Debord</a>
and the <a href="Situationist_International" class="wikilink"
title="Situationist International">Situationist International</a>,
<a href="Michel_Foucault" class="wikilink"
title="Michel Foucault">Michel Foucault</a>,
<a href="Giorgio_Agamben" class="wikilink"
title="Giorgio Agamben">Giorgio Agamben</a>,
<a href="Gilles_Deleuze" class="wikilink" title="Gilles Deleuze">Gilles
Deleuze</a>, and
<a href="Félix_Guattari" class="wikilink" title="Félix Guattari">Félix
Guattari</a>. Its analysis of
<a href="biopower" class="wikilink" title="biopower">biopower</a> and
the spectacle is visible throughout, though the writing favours
provocation over systematic argument.

## Critiques

*The Coming Insurrection* has attracted criticism from within the
anarchist and libertarian socialist traditions on both strategic and
analytical grounds.

### Class struggle anarchist critique

Wayne Price, writing from a
<a href="class_struggle_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="class struggle anarchist">class struggle anarchist</a>
perspective, identifies the book's most serious weakness as its
dismissal of the working class and its organisations.[^3] The Invisible
Committee describes unions as "the first obstacle every social movement
faces" — a formulation that collapses together the workers, their
organisations, and the bureaucracies that dominate those organisations
into a single hostile bloc.[^4] Price notes that the book's claim that
living labour has been "almost reduced to zero" by automation is a
considerable exaggeration, and that this error conveniently licenses the
authors to abandon any strategic engagement with workplaces or workers'
struggles.[^5]

Price also challenges the book's hostility to popular democratic
organisation. The Invisible Committee dismisses general assemblies and
mandated delegates, proposing instead a mystical account of collective
decision-making in which "the decision will occur to us rather than
being made by us." Price observes that this is not only vague but
authoritarian in tendency — easily captured by cliques or charismatic
individuals.[^6]

### Anarcho's critique

Iain McKay (writing as Anarcho) raises similar concerns in a review for
libcom.org, noting a recurring tension between the book's
ultra-revolutionary rhetoric and its quietly reformist proposals.[^7]
The practical recommendations — tending allotments, studying plankton
biology, fiddling welfare — sit uneasily with the grand calls for
infrastructure sabotage. McKay argues that the book offers no coherent
account of how a riot becomes a revolution, and that the emphasis on
external crisis as the motor of liberation reproduces the old Marxist
reliance on capitalist breakdown rather than developing working-class
power from within.[^8]

McKay also notes a contradiction at the heart of the book's
organisational theory: the Invisible Committee denounces all milieus and
organisations while urging readers to form communes — which are, on any
reasonable reading, organisations. The distinction the authors draw
(communes degenerate into milieus only when they "lose contact with the
truth on which they are founded") is too vague to do analytical
work.[^9]

Both critics acknowledge that the book correctly identifies the depth of
capitalism's crisis and shares the fundamental goals of revolutionary
anarchism: a classless, stateless, ecologically balanced, self-managed
world. The disagreement is strategic. As Price puts it, what is needed
is not insurrectionism but revolution — and revolution requires building
organised working-class power, not waiting for collapse.[^10]

### The Tarnac case

The association with the
<a href="Tarnac_Nine" class="wikilink" title="Tarnac Nine">Tarnac
Nine</a> raises its own questions about the book's politics. The Tarnac
group were, in practice, doing something quite different from what *The
Coming Insurrection* advocates in its more militant passages: they were
living quietly in a rural village, running a cooperative grocery and
helping local people. This is arguably closer to the prefigurative,
community-building tradition within anarchism than to the
insurrectionary sabotage the text calls for. Whether the rail sabotages
attributed to the group (charges later dropped or reduced) represent the
text's theory made practice or simply an isolated action remains
contested.
