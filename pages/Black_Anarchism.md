## Overview

**Black anarchism** — sometimes called **New Afrikan anarchism**,
**Panther anarchism**, or more broadly **Black anarchic radicalism** —
is an anti-authoritarian current that fuses anarchist opposition to
hierarchy and the state with an uncompromising analysis of white
supremacy, colonial dispossession, and the intersecting oppressions that
shape Black life. It rejects both the authoritarian socialism that has
repeatedly betrayed liberation movements and the Eurocentric blind spots
of mainstream anarchism, insisting that neither can adequately address
the specific conditions of Black people under capitalism and the settler
state.

The tendency draws on a long history of anti-authoritarian organisation
in Africa and the African diaspora, from stateless and egalitarian
pre-colonial societies to the Maroon communities of the Americas. As a
distinct political current in the United States, it emerged from the
radicalization of rank-and-file members of the
<a href="Black_Panther_Party" class="wikilink"
title="Black Panther Party">Black Panther Party</a> who grew
disillusioned with that organization's Marxist-Leninist structure, and
has since developed into a range of overlapping traditions including
<a href="Anarkata" class="wikilink" title="Anarkata">Anarkata</a>,
<a href="Anarchist_People_of_Color" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist People of Color">Anarchist People of Color</a>, and
various African anarchist formations.

## Origins

### Pre-colonial foundations

Long before anarchism was named as a philosophy in 19th-century Europe,
many African societies practised forms of non-hierarchical organisation
that prefigure its core commitments. Nomadic gatherer-hunter peoples,
including the Ju/wasi of southern Africa, organised without private
property, formal hierarchy, or division of labour for centuries. Settled
agricultural societies across the continent also developed egalitarian
and communal structures — some numbering in the millions — with
collective decision-making, common access to land, and economies based
on gift and mutual aid rather than accumulation. Elders held social
respect, but not coercive power; their role was one of consensus and
shared wisdom rather than command.

These societies are not romantic exceptions to human nature, nor a
blueprint to be copied wholesale. They are evidence that stateless,
non-hierarchical organisation is neither utopian nor foreign — it is
part of the actual history of African peoples, suppressed and distorted
by colonialism and the slave trade but never fully extinguished.

### Lucy Parsons and early Black anarchism

<a href="Lucy_Parsons" class="wikilink" title="Lucy Parsons">Lucy
Parsons</a>, a formerly enslaved woman who became a central figure in
the late 19th-century labour movement, is one of the earliest
identifiable Black anarchists. Writing in *The Alarm*, the paper of the
<a href="International_Working_People&#39;s_Association"
class="wikilink"
title="International Working People&#39;s Association">International
Working People's Association</a>, she documented the lynching of
thirteen African Americans in Carrollton, Mississippi, and connected the
violence of racism directly to the violence of class exploitation. Her
article "The Negro" called on Black people to resist through racial
solidarity, self-determination, and armed self-defence — an analysis
that placed race and class as co-constitutive rather than separate
concerns, decades before "intersectionality" became a theoretical term.

Parsons saw the dissolution of capitalism and the state as inseparable
from the creation of any genuinely anti-racist society. The Chicago
Police Department reportedly described her as "more dangerous than a
thousand rioters."

### The civil rights movement and anti-authoritarian organisation

After the Second World War, links formed between Black activists and
anarcho-pacifist currents in the United States.
<a href="Martin_Luther_King" class="wikilink"
title="Martin Luther King">Martin Luther King</a> contributed to David
Dellinger's magazine *Liberation*;
<a href="Bayard_Rustin" class="wikilink" title="Bayard Rustin">Bayard
Rustin</a> worked with the
<a href="War_Resisters_League" class="wikilink"
title="War Resisters League">War Resisters League</a>. Under the
influence of
<a href="Ella_Baker" class="wikilink" title="Ella Baker">Ella Baker</a>
— who insisted that "strong people don't need strong leaders" — the
<a href="Student_Nonviolent_Coordinating_Committee" class="wikilink"
title="Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee">Student Nonviolent
Coordinating Committee</a> (SNCC) organised along decentralised,
grassroots lines and maintained independence from the major civil rights
organisations. Some observers described it as operating on essentially
anarchist principles.

As the 1960s wore on, rank-and-file activists grew increasingly critical
of the civil rights movement's leadership — the so-called Big Six — whom
they accused of opportunism and accommodation to the liberal
establishment.
<a href="Ojore_Lutalo" class="wikilink" title="Ojore Lutalo">Ojore
Lutalo</a> would later characterise the mainstream civil rights movement
as "corrupt", with leaders "open for a price." The
<a href="Black_Power_movement" class="wikilink"
title="Black Power movement">Black Power movement</a> emerged from this
disillusionment, shifting emphasis from integration to autonomy, from
reform to confrontation with institutional white supremacy.

## Tendencies

### From the Black Panther Party to anarchism

The <a href="Black_Panther_Party" class="wikilink"
title="Black Panther Party">Black Panther Party</a> (BPP) represented a
serious attempt to address the intersections of white supremacy,
economic exploitation, and state repression. Its Ten Point Program, its
free breakfast programmes, its Copwatch networks — these were
experiments in community self-organisation and mutual aid that
anarchists would recognise as prefigurative in character. The Panthers
also published <a href="Sergey_Nechayev" class="wikilink"
title="Sergey Nechayev">Sergey Nechayev</a>'s *Catechism of a
Revolutionary* and drew inspiration from the
<a href="Diggers" class="wikilink" title="Diggers">Diggers</a>' free
food distribution model, which later influenced
<a href="Food_Not_Bombs" class="wikilink" title="Food Not Bombs">Food
Not Bombs</a>.

But the party carried internal contradictions that would prove fatal. As
the 1970s progressed, state repression via
<a href="COINTELPRO" class="wikilink" title="COINTELPRO">COINTELPRO</a>
devastated its membership, while the Oakland-based central committee
under
<a href="Huey_P._Newton" class="wikilink" title="Huey P. Newton">Huey P.
Newton</a> consolidated power, reorienting the party from decentralised
revolutionary organising toward electoralism and fundraising.
Rank-and-file members across the country were left without meaningful
internal democracy, subject to commands from a leadership increasingly
divorced from their conditions.

<a href="Kuwasi_Balagoon" class="wikilink"
title="Kuwasi Balagoon">Kuwasi Balagoon</a> described the result as "a
hierarchy that had undeserved pretensions of grandeur" that "turned away
from its purposes of liberation of the Black colony to fundraising."
<a href="Lorenzo_Kom&#39;boa_Ervin" class="wikilink"
title="Lorenzo Kom&#39;boa Ervin">Lorenzo Kom'boa Ervin</a> argued the
party "partially failed because of the authoritarian leadership style"
of Newton, Seale and others on the central committee — leaders who
resolved contradictions for the membership rather than with them.
<a href="Ashanti_Alston" class="wikilink" title="Ashanti Alston">Ashanti
Alston</a> reflected on his own "uncritical acceptance" of the
leadership, asking: "what does it say about you, if you allow someone to
set themselves up as your leader and make all your decisions for you?"

These critiques — worked out largely in prison, at a geographic and
institutional distance from the movement's centre — led a group of
incarcerated Panthers and their allies toward anarchism.

### The post-Panther milieu

**Martin Sostre** may have been the first Black anarchist of the
post-1960s wave. Growing up in Harlem during the Great Depression, he
was imprisoned on trumped-up drug charges in 1952, turned to the Nation
of Islam, and became a jailhouse lawyer. After his release in 1964, he
opened a radical bookstore in Buffalo that served as a community centre
for resistance. When revolt hit Buffalo, Sostre was there — teaching,
distributing literature, contextualising the uprising — and was arrested
and imprisoned again.

In prison, Sostre almost single-handedly won democratic rights for
prisoners: the right to receive revolutionary literature, to worship
outside mainstream faiths, to challenge indefinite solitary confinement,
to access legal representation at disciplinary proceedings. He
encountered anarchist ideas and developed a deep critique of
Marxist-Leninist "party-line" politics, which he saw as replacing one
set of ruling elites with another without advancing human freedom. He
was released in 1971 after the witness against him recanted, and
continued to develop anarchist ideas until his death in 2015.

**Lorenzo Kom'boa Ervin** was introduced to anarchism by Sostre during a
shared period of incarceration. A former SNCC and Panther activist, he
had fled to Cuba after attempting to kill a Ku Klux Klan member, only to
be jailed by Cuban authorities, deported, captured in East Germany,
tortured, and handed two life sentences by an all-white jury. His
experience of actually-existing "socialism" in Cuba and Eastern Europe
confirmed his developing critique of authoritarian statism. From prison,
he began writing what became *Anarchism and the Black Revolution*
(1979), one of the most widely read works in the Black anarchist
tradition. The Anarchist Black Cross and international supporters
campaigned for his release; he was freed in 1983.

Ervin consistently argued that Black people and other people of colour
would form the backbone of any serious anarchist movement in the United
States — but only if that movement overcame its Eurocentric limitations
and confronted white supremacy as a structural reality, not an
afterthought. His emphasis on intersectional analysis — race, class,
gender, and state power as co-constitutive — proved influential in
shifting American anarchism away from class-exclusive frameworks.

**Kuwasi Balagoon** joined the Panthers in New York in 1967 after three
years in the US army, where experience of racism in Germany and exposure
to the African diaspora in London had moved him toward Afrocentrism. He
was arrested in 1969 as part of the Panther 21 and later sentenced to
23–29 years. Openly bisexual — a fact often erased from his legacy —
Balagoon developed what he called New Afrikan Anarchism, writing: "Of
all ideologies, anarchy is the one that addresses liberty and
equalitarian relations in a realistic and ultimate fashion... the goals
of anarchy don't include replacing one ruling class with another,
neither in the guise of a fairer boss or as a party." He died in prison
in 1986 of an AIDS-related illness.

**Ojore Lutalo** was not a Panther but was involved in revolutionary
struggle from 1970. Imprisoned after a shootout with police, he
encountered anarchism through Balagoon, becoming a New Afrikan anarchist
in 1975. He spent years in the Maximum Control Unit — sensory
deprivation, shackles, guards carrying clubs they called "nigger
beaters" — facing false charges and denial of parole for his political
influence on other prisoners. In a 2005 prison interview he said: "I
just believe in the consensus process, I believe in the autonomous
process. I believe that people are intelligent enough to govern their
own lives and make their own decisions without somebody collecting
untold billions of dollars of taxes and telling you what should and
shouldn't be." He was released in 2009.

**Ashanti Alston** joined the Panthers and the Black Liberation Army in
1971 and was imprisoned in 1974. In solitary confinement, he finally
read anarchist texts introduced to him by fellow Panther Frankie Ziths —
and was surprised to find, not chaos theory, but serious analyses of
peoples' struggles, cultures, and organisational forms. He found
European anarchism incomplete, however: it did not speak to the specific
experience of Black people under white supremacy. He looked to the
anti-authoritarian practices of non-European societies — from
pre-colonial Africa to the anti-colonial struggles of Angola,
Guinea-Bissau, Mozambique, and Zimbabwe — to develop an anarchism
adequate to Black liberation. Released in 1985, Alston has continued to
organise and write, arguing that Black nationalism, despite its
historical problems with sexism and hierarchy, remains a force for unity
and social change when practised in an anti-authoritarian way. "Power to
the people where it stays with the people," he wrote — a formulation he
traces to his anarchist politics.

### Anarchist People of Color

Throughout the 1990s, Black anarchists established their own
organisations: the
<a href="Black_Autonomy_Network_of_Community_Organizers"
class="wikilink"
title="Black Autonomy Network of Community Organizers">Black Autonomy
Network of Community Organizers</a> (BANCO), the
<a href="Federation_of_Black_Community_Partisans" class="wikilink"
title="Federation of Black Community Partisans">Federation of Black
Community Partisans</a> (FBCP), and the newspaper *Black Autonomy*,
edited by
<a href="Greg_Jackson" class="wikilink" title="Greg Jackson">Greg
Jackson</a>, which over four years introduced readers to Black anarchist
ideas and documented police brutality and urban insurrections.

The decade also saw the emergence of the
<a href="Anarchist_People_of_Color" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist People of Color">Anarchist People of Color</a> (APOC)
movement, which brought together African, Asian, and Latin American
anarchists across the United States. In 2003, Ernesto Aguilar organised
the first APOC conference in Detroit, attended by around 300 people. The
conference gave anarchists of colour space to see each other, recognise
commonalities, and articulate a vision independent of the white-majority
anarchist movement. As Alston described it, the conference allowed
participants to "share their experience and articulate their vision to
fellow anarchists of colour, advocate for a stronger analysis of race
and ethnicity within the anarchist movement, and develop a conscious
project of self-determination for people of colour."

APOC collectives provided what Pedro Ribeiro described as a *quilombo* —
a refuge and space of collective resistance — where members could
organise around anti-racism without the condescension and
colour-blindness that had characterised much of the white anarchist
movement.

### Anarkata

**Anarkata** emerged in 2019 as a distinct political tendency defined by
the Afrofuturist Abolitionists of the Americas. The term reclaims the
Yoruba word *akata* — sometimes used as a slur — meaning roughly "wild
animal" or "housecat", as a marker of defiant Black freedom. It is an
in-house term, not to be applied by non-Black people to others.

Anarkata draws on Black anarchism, Black Marxism, Pan-Africanism, Black
feminism, and queer liberation, opposing not only the Western capitalist
forces that oppress Black people but all axes of domination that bear on
Black lives. It traces a lineage from the communal nomads of
pre-colonial Africa through the Maroons of the Americas, slave
uprisings, resistance to European colonialism, Black feminist struggle,
trans defiance of colonial gender binaries, and the contemporary prison
abolition movement. As the Anarkata statement puts it: "Through
countless moments of defiance and flexibility, our ancestors made a way
for us to imagine an anarchic radicalism that is unmistakably Black."

### African anarchism

Black anarchic politics are not confined to the diaspora. In South
Africa, the
<a href="Zabalaza_Anarchist_Communist_Front" class="wikilink"
title="Zabalaza Anarchist Communist Front">Zabalaza Anarchist Communist
Front</a> (ZACF), founded in 2003, is an anarchist communist
organisation active in Johannesburg. Its name is drawn from the Xhosa
word for "struggle". The ZACF works within mass social movements to
spread anarchist principles — mutual aid, horizontalism, direct action,
and independence from electoral politics — without necessarily being
recognised as such. Its Black members have faced death threats,
repression, and arrest.

In the Horn of Africa, the Horn Anarchists collective, founded in 2020,
organises against the deeply hierarchical political and religious
structures of the region — including the expansionist Ethiopian empire
and its project of assimilating diverse identities into a single
Orthodox Christian Ethiopian identity — and has worked with refugees
displaced by the Tigray genocide.

In Nigeria, the Awareness League flourished in the 1990s in resistance
to military rule, drawing on the collapse of state socialism in Europe
to develop a popular anarchist politics. It largely dissipated after
civilian rule returned in 1999, but Nigerian anarchist and co-author of
*African Anarchism*, Sam Mbah — who died in 2014 — argued that anarchism
in Africa was not dead, only developing. The work of the ZACF and Horn
Anarchists continues to prove him right.

### Contemporary politics

Black anarchism refuses any politics that can conceive of liberation
only through the nation-state. As William C. Anderson has written, those
most marginalised — poor people, stateless people, migrants, the
oppressed of all kinds — are rendered disposable by state power, not
protected by it. Genuine transformation means power in the hands of
people themselves, without the homogenising force of national
citizenship and borders.

In *As Black As Resistance: Finding the Conditions of Liberation*,
Anderson, <a href="Zoé_Samudzi" class="wikilink" title="Zoé Samudzi">Zoé
Samudzi</a>, and
<a href="Mariame_Kaba" class="wikilink" title="Mariame Kaba">Mariame
Kaba</a> write:

> "Black Americans are residents of a settler colony, not truly citizens
> of the United States. Despite a constitution laden with European
> Enlightenment values and a document of independence declaring certain
> inalienable rights, Black existence was legally that of private
> property until postbellum emancipation. The Black American condition
> today is an evolved condition directly connected to this history of
> slavery, and that will continue to be the case as long as the United
> States remains as an ongoing settler project. Nothing short of a
> complete dismantling of the American state as it presently exists can
> or will disrupt this."[^1]

Ervin's strategy for practice centres survival programmes, mutual aid,
housing cooperatives, rent strikes, labour strikes, community councils,
and the seizure of food systems, workplaces, and educational
institutions. The goal is not to rename or repurpose the machinery of
oppression but to dismantle it.

## Criticism

### Of Eurocentric anarchism

Black anarchists have consistently criticised the white, middle-class
character of mainstream anarchism in the United States. Ervin found the
movement "overwhelmingly White, middle-class, and for the most part,
pacifist" — and encountered active resistance when he attempted to
establish autonomous working groups for people of colour within
organisations like the
<a href="Industrial_Workers_of_the_World" class="wikilink"
title="Industrial Workers of the World">Industrial Workers of the
World</a> and the Love and Rage Revolutionary Anarchist Federation,
whose members accused him of "separatism." Balagoon criticised White
anarchists for failing to understand the deep structures of white
supremacy and their opposition to national liberation movements. Alston
argued that this ignorance actively damaged the movement's capacity for
effective anti-racist organising.

Pedro Ribeiro defined the problem as a deformed "white, petty-bourgeois
anarchism that cannot relate to the people" — one that refuses to engage
with race except "in that very abstract sense of
we-are-all-equal-let's-sing-kumbayas-and-pretend-the-colour-of-our-skin-does-not-matter
anti-racism."[^2]

Ernesto Aguilar extended this critique inward: "In essence, equal power
is talked about, but many white people aren't actually prepared to share
it with the world majority. Why should they? Giving up the intoxicating
power and influence over others and history is not easy."

### Of Marxism-Leninism

Black anarchists reject Marxist-Leninist politics not as a theoretical
abstraction but as a lesson drawn from experience. Ervin's time in Cuba
and East Germany taught him that "actually existing socialism" was
dictatorship — not some transitional stage toward freedom. Sostre
rejected "the wooden party line" and "some abstract political line or
ideology" in favour of struggling for "human beings with lives to
fulfil." Balagoon argued that Marxism was ineffective for organising
Black communities and that its tendency toward bureaucracy and political
repression reproduced the structures it claimed to oppose.

Both Sostre and Alston concluded that the BPP's Marxist-Leninist
orientation made it structurally inclined toward internal repression and
intolerant of spontaneity and bottom-up decision-making.
