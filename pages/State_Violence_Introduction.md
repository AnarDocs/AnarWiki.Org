# Problems with Hierarchical Capitalist Justice Systems

Anarchists argue that the current system for handling violence and
anti-social behaviour under hierarchical capitalism does not protect
people — it protects property and power.

## Creation and Maintenance of Crime

Modern police forces did not emerge from social necessity. Historically,
they arose when crime was already diminishing, alongside the deliberate
creation of new criminal categories through the criminalisation of
predominantly working-class behaviours that had previously been
tolerated: vagrancy, gambling, public drunkenness. Those in authority
define 'criminal activity' according to their own needs, then present
their definitions as neutral and timeless. Far more people are killed by
pollution and work-related accidents than by drugs, but drug dealers are
branded a threat to society while factory owners face little scrutiny.

## Disproportionate Impact on Marginalized Communities

The criminal justice system functions as the executive arm of racist and
classist oppression. In the US, one third of African American men are
either in prison, on probation, or on parole. In the UK, black people
are seven times more likely to be stopped and searched, and make up 15%
of the prison population despite representing just 2.2% of the overall
population. Over two-thirds of US prisoners are locked up for nonviolent
offences, and the majority are poor — reflecting both the
criminalisation of poverty-related behaviour and the greater likelihood
of poor defendants being convicted or sentenced more harshly.

## Failure to Address Root Causes

The current system punishes rather than addresses the social conditions
that drive harmful behaviour. Economic inequality, poverty,
homelessness, and alienation are well-established contributors to crime,
yet they remain untouched by criminal justice policy. Prisons function
as what critics have called 'universities of crime': first-time
offenders learn new techniques and are absorbed into criminal
subcultures. Meanwhile, the authoritarian child-rearing practices and
social conditioning that normalise violence and anti-social behaviour
continue largely unchallenged.

## Removal of Agency from Those Affected

When the state assumes responsibility for dealing with offences, it
removes power and agency from victims, communities, and defendants
alike. The process is frequently traumatic for all parties, often leaves
victims unsatisfied, and brands defendants with a 'criminal' status that
makes reintegration into society genuinely difficult. Genuine
accountability and healing are not what the system is designed to
produce.

## Serving Elite Interests

The justice system preserves existing power structures rather than
challenging them. It generates fear and division useful to the powerful.
Criminalising property damage creates stable environments for investors.
The 'War on Drugs' justifies police occupation of poor neighbourhoods
and mass imprisonment. Thousands of workers killed annually by employer
negligence face no criminal consequences for their employers, while poor
people stealing food face imprisonment.

## Prison as Industry

The prison system has become a profitable industry. It channels public
money into institutions that strengthen state control, relies on
prisoner labour paid at a fraction of minimum wage, creates economic
incentives for maintaining high prison populations, and operates company
stores where prisoners buy necessities at inflated prices.

## Perpetuating Violence

The system does not reduce violence; it reproduces it. Police and
prisons are themselves systems of extreme violence directed at the most
oppressed communities. The presence of militarised police in poor
neighbourhoods correlates with high crime rates rather than low ones.
Prison conditions encourage gang organisation and produce more dangerous
individuals upon release. The adversarial model pits people against each
other rather than seeking resolution.

## Suppression of Community Solutions

The state's monopoly on justice prevents communities from developing
their own conflict resolution methods. Rather than building collective
capacity to address harm, it creates dependence on a system oriented
toward elite interests.
