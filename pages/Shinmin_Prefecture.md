## Shinmin Prefecture

From 1929 to 1931, two million Korean migrants ran an autonomous,
<a href="Anarchist" class="wikilink" title="Anarchist">Anarchist</a>
zone in Shinmin, a rural province in Manchuria (in China) across the
border from Korea. Residents made decisions at village assemblies, which
confederated into district councils and area councils.[^1][^2] The
confederation, known as the Korean People's Association in Manchuria
(KPAM), had administrative departments that assisted with agriculture,
education, finance, military affairs, and health. Staff in these
departments received no more than the province's average wage. The KPAM
described itself as "an independent self-governing cooperative system of
the Korean people who assembled their full power to save our nation by
struggling against Japan".[^3]

Many of Shinmin's residents had been active in Korea's March 1 Movement
against Japanese colonialism in 1919 and fled Japanese suppression by
migrating to China. There they became active in an anarchist movement
that included the Korean Anarchist Federation in Manchuria (KAF-M), the
Korean Anarcho-Communist Federation (KACF), and a section of the Korean
Independence Association (KIA) associated with
<a href="Kim_Jwa-Jin" class="wikilink" title="Kim Jwa-Jin">Kim
Jwa-Jin</a>, an anarchist military strategist sometimes compared to
<a href="Nestor_Makhno" class="wikilink" title="Nestor Makhno">Nestor
Makhno</a>. These three groups collaborated to launch the KPAM in
Shinmin by August of 1929.[^4][^5]

## Background

The Korean anarchist movement in Manchuria grew out of decades of
Japanese encroachment on the Korean peninsula. Following the Donghak
Peasant Revolution of 1894, the
<a href="Empire_of_Japan" class="wikilink"
title="Empire of Japan">Empire of Japan</a> intervened in Korea and
brought it under Japanese influence, formally annexing the country in
1910. Many Korean dissidents fled into exile in Manchuria, where they
established study groups and military schools to prepare for
resistance.[^6] After the violent repression of the March 1st Movement
in 1919, a fresh wave of refugees crossed the border, and it was among
these exiles that an organised anarchist movement took root.[^7][^8]

The political situation in Manchuria itself was unstable. The region had
been controlled by the warlord Zhang Zuolin, who had collaborated in
repressing the Korean resistance with Japanese backing. After Zhang was
assassinated in 1928 by Japanese imperial agents, his son Zhang Xueliang
took a sharply anti-Japanese stance, opening political space for Korean
exile organising to resume in the open.[^9]

Korean anarchists in Manchuria first organised through a youth body
called *Ja yu cheong nyeon hoe*, which propagated anarchist ideas across
the region. Kim Jong-jin, Yi Dal and Kim Ya-bong gathered its members
into *Heug u yeon maeng*, and further youth groupings converged into
*Bung man han in cheong nyeon yeon maeng*, which combined the study of
anarchism with popular education work. Kim Jong-jin and Yi Eul-gyu then
used this network as the foundation for the Korean Anarchist Federation
in Manchuria, established at Hailin on 21 July 1929.[^10][^11]

By this point Korean nationalists in Manchuria were losing ground. Their
three rival prefecture authorities had failed to unify, they had
alienated the population by expropriating resources, and many ordinary
Koreans were drifting toward <a href="Marxism-Leninism" class="wikilink"
title="Marxist-Leninist">Marxist-Leninist</a> organisations. Sensing the
danger of being eclipsed, the nationalists associated with Kim Jwa-jin's
New People's Government joined forces with the anarchists, and in August
1929 the Korean People's Association in Manchuria was established as the
result.[^12][^13]

## Organisation

The Shinmin uprising had top-down origins that it tried to transcend.
The KPAM initially appointed its own staff, but it also sent teams to
organise village assemblies in order to make the structure more
horizontal.[^14] The new structure "quickly gained grassroots support
because it was based on worker and community self-organisation".[^15]
The KPAM organised mutual aid banks, a primary and secondary school
system, and a militia.[^16]

The association defined itself as an autonomous, self-governing
cooperative organisation. Its representative system was designed, in
line with the anarchist principle of self-governance, to be "a
government without \[compulsory\] government".[^17] Decisions were
largely made in village assemblies, with a
<a href="federation" class="wikilink" title="federated">federated</a>
structure of councils at the village, district and regional levels
handling larger matters.[^18]

According to the rules of the KPAM, members were revolutionary Koreans
who had lived in the region for longer than three months; they carried
rights and obligations including donating funds, enlisting in the
militia, and voting. The central body was made up of representative,
executive and conference agencies, with committees for military affairs,
farming, education and the economy. The representative agency, the
highest decision-making body, met every January. The executive agency of
fifteen to twenty-one members carried out its resolutions, while the
conference agency handled coordination between committees and
propaganda.[^19]

At the regional level, each district had an agriculture association that
served as the local administration, taking responsibility for matters
ranging from finance to education to security, with between five and
nine elected members. Separate education and security associations
handled those tasks in detail.[^20]

Delegates at every level were ordinary workers and peasants who earned a
minimum wage, held no special privileges, and remained subject to recall
by the bodies that mandated them.[^21] Organisation and propaganda teams
travelled the region to encourage farmers to form their own village
assemblies and committees, and were reportedly welcomed almost
everywhere they went without serious incident.[^22]

## Economy

The KPAM's economic programme drew on the traditions of
<a href="libertarian_socialism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian socialism">libertarian socialism</a>: mutual aid
banks, <a href="worker_cooperatives" class="wikilink"
title="worker cooperatives">worker cooperatives</a> and a comprehensive
system of free education throughout the territory.[^23] Its priority was
to meet the immediate material needs of Korean migrant workers and to
shield them from exploitation by Chinese landlords and Korean
nationalist authorities alike. The association introduced
<a href="collective_farming" class="wikilink"
title="collective farming">collective farming</a>, which placed the
production and sale of agricultural produce under common control of the
peasants who worked the land.[^24]

Although Korean nationalists cooperated within the KPAM, the nationalist
project of independence was deliberately subordinated to the survival of
Korean migrant workers, framed through the anarchist principle of mutual
aid. Agitation for an independent or anarchist Korea was postponed in
favour of sustaining the economic structures keeping the region
alive.[^25] The KPAM also built rice mills, in part to break the hold of
Chinese merchants who had been exploiting Korean peasants in the grain
trade.[^26]

The programme adopted by the federation set out an explicit commitment
to "free development via mutual aid and free association, work according
to one's ability and consumption based on one's need" — an
anarcho-communist formulation that situated Shinmin within the broader
tradition of <a href="anarchist_communism" class="wikilink"
title="anarchist communism">anarchist communism</a>.[^27]

## Defence

The <a href="Korean_Independence_Army" class="wikilink"
title="Korean Independence Army">Korean Independence Army</a>, commanded
by General
<a href="Kim_Jwa-Jin" class="wikilink" title="Kim Jwa-Jin">Kim
Jwa-Jin</a>, formed the military of Shinmin Prefecture.[^28] Effectively
a peasant militia, its experienced soldiers were supplemented by
guerrillas trained in the prefecture's own military academy, and it
waged <a href="guerrilla_warfare" class="wikilink"
title="guerrilla warfare">guerrilla warfare</a> against both the Empire
of Japan and the
<a href="Soviet_Union" class="wikilink" title="Soviet Union">Soviet
Union</a>.[^29] The KPAM also established a Safety Unit (*Chiandae*) and
an Anti-Japanese Guerrilla Unit, the former to protect Korean
communities from local bandits, the latter to confront Imperial Japanese
forces directly.[^30]

The militia defended the province from Japanese and Stalinist attacks
for two years. By the summer of 1931, anarchists decided they could no
longer defend the war-torn province and resolved to go underground.[^31]

## Fall

The Korean autonomous zone was eventually encircled by Imperial Japanese
forces to the south and Soviet forces to the north, with covert
operatives sent into the territory to target prominent Korean
anarchists.[^32] The association suffered a series of blows with the
loss of leading figures. On 20 January 1930, Kim Jwa-jin was
assassinated by Gong Do-jin, a member of the *Hwa yo pa*
Marxist-Leninist faction, while repairing a rice mill. In September
1930, Yi Eul-gyu was arrested by Japanese imperial police and extradited
to Korea. In July 1931 Kim Jong-jin was also assassinated.[^33][^34]

After Kim Jwa-jin's killing, the KPAM moved its headquarters to Jilin
and attempted to rebuild. Funds raised at an anarchist meeting in
Beijing were confiscated when ten members were arrested by Chinese
police collaborating with the Japanese embassy. Internal divisions
within the association deepened, and friction between the anarchist and
nationalist wings grew sharper. Anarchists found themselves pushed out
of the leading positions, and the assassinations of Yi Jun-geun, Kim
Ya-un and Kim Jong-jin by the nationalist wing closed the Shinmin
chapter for good.[^35]

The <a href="Japanese_invasion_of_Manchuria" class="wikilink"
title="Japanese invasion of Manchuria">Japanese invasion of
Manchuria</a> on 18 September 1931 brought a definitive end to the
anarchist experiment, with the puppet state of Manchukuo established in
its place.[^36] From southern China, the KAF-M and KACF continued
fighting against Japan until Japan's defeat at the end of World War
II.[^37] The Tokologo African Anarchist Collective describes what
happened next: "The American ruling class conquered the Japanese state,
and then placed Korea under American control. A war broke out, and the
country was eventually split into a Russian- and American-backed North
and South, each run by dictators."[^38]

## Assessment

Writers reviewing Shinmin from within the anarchist tradition have drawn
out both its achievements and its unresolved tensions. The KPAM was
structured along anarchist lines of bottom-up organisation through free
association, with regions sending delegates to handle the wider affairs
of the federation. The pressures of war and exile, however, pushed it
toward a partly top-down approach, in which candidates were selected for
each structure and then put to election.[^39]

A deeper tension lay in the composition of the federation itself.
Although its structures were anarchist, not every member of the
association was an anarchist, and one clause of its programme spoke of
"the complete independence of the nation" — implicitly accepting the
form of the <a href="state" class="wikilink" title="state">state</a>
that anarchists otherwise reject. Cooperation with nationalists secured
a regional base the anarchists could not have built alone, but it also
seeded the internal conflicts that eventually destroyed the federation
from within.[^40]

Despite these contradictions, the experience demonstrated, as Peter
Gelderloos put it, that "large populations had freed themselves from the
authority of landlords and governors and reasserted their power to come
to collective decisions, to organize their day-to-day life, pursue their
dreams, and defend those dreams from invading armies".[^41] Michael
Schmidt notes that Shinmin showed "how the uplift of the working class
through economic autonomy and education could combine seamlessly with a
bottom-up system of decision-making and a militant defensive
programme".[^42]

After the collapse of Shinmin, surviving Korean anarchists scattered
across mainland China. Without a Korean population to organise among,
and discouraged by the destruction of the only base they had built, many
turned toward direct action and assassination of Japanese officials,
sometimes in collaboration with nationalist figures such as Kim Koo. The
bitterness left by the killing of Kim Jwa-jin also drove sustained
anti-Marxist-Leninist activity through the 1930s.[^43]
