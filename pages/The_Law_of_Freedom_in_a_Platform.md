## Overview

Published in 1652 and dedicated to
<a href="Oliver_Cromwell" class="wikilink"
title="Oliver Cromwell">Oliver Cromwell</a>, ***The Law of Freedom in a
Platform, or True Magistracy Restored*** represents both the culmination
and the limit of <a href="Gerrard_Winstanley" class="wikilink"
title="Gerrard Winstanley">Gerrard Winstanley</a>'s radical thought.
Where his earlier writings had articulated a vision of communal life
rooted in conscience and mutual aid, the *Law of Freedom* turned toward
institutions — elected government, enforced labour, codified law — in an
attempt to make that vision palatable to the new Commonwealth. The
manuscript was heavily censored by its publishers before reaching
Cromwell, who read an edited version on 21 April 1652 and reportedly
found parts of it sympathetic.[^1]

## Summary

### Background

After the
<a href="Diggers" class="wikilink" title="Diggers">Diggers</a>'
settlement at <a href="St_George&#39;s_Hill" class="wikilink"
title="St George&#39;s Hill">St George's Hill</a> was broken up by
<a href="Thomas_Fairfax" class="wikilink" title="Thomas Fairfax">Thomas
Fairfax</a>, Winstanley set about converting the movement's experience
into a body of writing. His earlier works had developed an essentially
libertarian communism grounded in natural law and the rejection of
private property. The *Law of Freedom* emerged from a different moment:
in June 1651,
<a href="Hugh_Peter" class="wikilink" title="Hugh Peter">Hugh Peter</a>
had published *Good Work for a Good Magistrate*, proposing a series of
reforms to the Commonwealth, and the victory at the
<a href="Battle_of_Worcester" class="wikilink"
title="Battle of Worcester">Battle of Worcester</a> had opened a window
of political possibility. Winstanley responded to this climate by
drafting a programme addressed directly to those in power.[^2]

### Property and Labour

The book proposed abolishing <a href="private_property" class="wikilink"
title="private property">private property</a> and
<a href="money" class="wikilink" title="money">money</a>, reorganising
society around <a href="common_ownership" class="wikilink"
title="common ownership">common ownership</a> of land and collective
labour — the model Winstanley had attempted to put into practice at
George's Hill.[^3] Households were permitted to retain their homes and
basic necessities as personal possessions, but productive land and
commodities would be held in common. Overseers would be appointed to
ensure everyone contributed their share of work. Those who refused were
to be stripped of civil rights and impressed into forced labour — a
provision that sat awkwardly with Winstanley's earlier emphasis on
freely-given cooperation.[^4]

### Law and Coercion

The *Law of Freedom* marked a decisive shift toward what Marshall called
an authoritarian vision of communism. Winstanley's conflicts with the
<a href="Ranters" class="wikilink" title="Ranters">Ranters</a> and
freeholders at George's Hill had convinced him that a "righteous
government" was necessary to maintain social order. He argued for law
enforced by a standing army to suppress monarchism and guard against
what he called "rudeness." His proposed legal code followed an
eye-for-an-eye principle, applying capital punishment to murder, rape,
and trading — as well as to lawyers and parsons.[^5]

### Government

Winstanley proposed an annually elected parliament to enforce laws,
oversee a planned economy, and — more troublingly — uphold patriarchal
authority. Freedom was redefined narrowly as the right to vote,
entitlement to the product of one's labour, and the capacity to employ
domestic workers. He retained commitments to freedom of religion and
compulsory education, and proposed elected peacemakers in each town to
settle disputes without imprisonment.[^6]

## Critiques

The *Law of Freedom* presents a genuine problem for those who read
Winstanley primarily as a precursor to anarchism. Peter Marshall
considered it a work of proto-Marxism that broke sharply from the
libertarian character of the earlier Digger writings. The
<a href="Marxism" class="wikilink" title="Marxist">Marxist</a> historian
<a href="Christopher_Hill" class="wikilink"
title="Christopher Hill">Christopher Hill</a> read it more charitably as
an exercise in possibilism — a tactical document aimed at nudging
Cromwell toward reform rather than a full statement of Winstanley's
ideals.[^7]

The tension is real either way. A text that advocates forced labour,
capital punishment for trade, and the institutionalisation of patriarchy
sits uneasily beside the communal experiments at George's Hill. Whether
the *Law of Freedom* represents Winstanley's mature thinking or a
strategic compromise with political reality remains an open question —
and one that matters for how his legacy is understood within the broader
history of radical thought.
