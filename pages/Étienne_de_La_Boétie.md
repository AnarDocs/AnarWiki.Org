# Étienne de La Boétie

**Étienne de La Boétie** (1530–1563) was a French magistrate, humanist,
poet, and political theorist whose
*<a href="Discourse_on_Voluntary_Servitude" class="wikilink"
title="Discourse on Voluntary Servitude">Discourse on Voluntary
Servitude</a>* stands as one of the earliest and most radical critiques
of political authority in Western thought. Written when he was still a
student, the text asks a single devastating question: why do people
consent to their own oppression? His answer — that tyranny rests not on
force but on the active compliance of the governed — laid the groundwork
for centuries of anti-authoritarian thought, from the Huguenot
resistance pamphlets of the 16th century through to
<a href="Gustav_Landauer" class="wikilink"
title="Gustav Landauer">Gustav Landauer</a>,
<a href="Simone_Weil" class="wikilink" title="Simone Weil">Simone
Weil</a>, and the nonviolent direct action movements of the 20th
century.

## Family

La Boétie was born into a family of educated magistrates in Sarlat, in
the Périgord region of southwest France. His father was a royal official
of the region and his mother was the sister of the president of the
Bordeaux Parliament. Orphaned young, he was raised by his uncle, a
curate with sympathies for the new humanist ideas circulating through
Renaissance France — an influence that would shape the intellectual
formation of the boy in his care.

## Early Life

La Boétie's education placed him at the centre of the intellectual
ferment of the French Renaissance. He studied Latin and Greek from an
early age and went on to take his law degree at the
<a href="University_of_Orléans" class="wikilink"
title="University of Orléans">University of Orléans</a> in 1553, an
institution already known as a hotbed of humanist thought. Among his
professors was Anne du Bourg, a Protestant reformer who would be hanged
in 1559 for defying the king — an early lesson in the mortal stakes of
challenging power.

It was during these student years, around 1548–1553, that La Boétie
composed the *Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*. The precise date
remains disputed: <a href="Michel_de_Montaigne" class="wikilink"
title="Michel de Montaigne">Michel de Montaigne</a> later claimed his
friend wrote it at sixteen or eighteen, but scholars now consider it
more likely the work was composed at twenty-two or twenty-three.[^1]
Montaigne's insistence on extreme youth was probably a deliberate
manoeuvre — casting the text as a student exercise to distance his dead
friend from the Calvinist rebels who had seized on it as a revolutionary
manifesto. Whatever its precise date of composition, the *Discourse* was
produced in the wake of the 1548 popular uprising against a salt tax in
southwest France, which was crushed by royal forces with considerable
violence. The connection is hard to miss.

## Adulthood

Despite the radicalism of his unpublished treatise, La Boétie pursued a
respectable career within the very institutions he had subjected to
theoretical demolition. Appointed to the Bordeaux Parliament as a
counsellor in 1554 — below the minimum age, on account of his
exceptional ability — he went on to serve as a judge and diplomatic
negotiator for the remainder of his short life. He was also a
distinguished classical scholar, translating
<a href="Xenophon" class="wikilink" title="Xenophon">Xenophon</a> and
<a href="Plutarch" class="wikilink" title="Plutarch">Plutarch</a>, and
was connected to the leading poets of the
<a href="Pléiade" class="wikilink" title="Pléiade">Pléiade</a> movement,
including <a href="Pierre_de_Ronsard" class="wikilink"
title="Pierre de Ronsard">Pierre de Ronsard</a> and
<a href="Jean_Daurat" class="wikilink" title="Jean Daurat">Jean
Daurat</a>.

### Personal Life

It was through the Bordeaux Parliament that La Boétie met
<a href="Michel_de_Montaigne" class="wikilink"
title="Michel de Montaigne">Michel de Montaigne</a>, probably around
1558. The two men formed an immediate and profound bond. Their
friendship lasted only six years before La Boétie's death, but it marked
Montaigne permanently — his famous essay *On Friendship* is essentially
a monument to La Boétie, concluding with the line: "Because it was him,
because it was me." Some historians have raised the question of whether
the relationship was also romantic, though the evidence is
inconclusive.[^2]

La Boétie was sympathetic to religious reconciliation between Catholics
and Protestants, though he stopped short of advocating full tolerance —
he was concerned about the political instability of a kingdom divided
between two faiths, and saw institutional reform of the Church as the
more sustainable path to peace.[^3]

## Death

La Boétie died on 18 August 1563 at Germignan, near Bordeaux, almost
certainly of dysentery, aged thirty-two. Montaigne was present
throughout his three days of agony, having rushed to his friend's side
despite the risk of contagion. On his deathbed, La Boétie bequeathed
Montaigne his library and all his manuscripts. He had never published
the *Discourse* in his own lifetime, and it is to Montaigne's
stewardship — protective and at times ambivalent — that the text's
survival is owed.

Montaigne had originally planned to place the *Discourse* at the centre
of his *Essays*. After the St Bartholomew's Day Massacre of 1572,
Protestant pamphleteers republished the text anonymously under the title
*Contr'un* to justify armed resistance to the crown. Montaigne withdrew
it from his *Essays* rather than be associated with the Calvinist cause,
replacing it with sonnets. In 1579, the Bordeaux Parliament condemned
the work to be burned. La Boétie had been dead for sixteen years, and
his book was already making power nervous.

## The Discourse on Voluntary Servitude

The argument of the *Discourse* is simple and ruthless. The tyrant has
only two eyes, two hands, one body. He has no more strength than the
lowliest subject in his kingdom. His power comes entirely from the
consent — active or passive — of those he governs. Every eye that spies
for him, every hand that feeds him, every arm that defends him is a gift
freely given by the governed. Withdraw that consent collectively, and
the tyrant is nothing: not overthrown, not defeated, simply abandoned.

La Boétie identifies several mechanisms by which voluntary servitude
reproduces itself. Habit is the most durable: those born under tyranny
know nothing else, and have no memory of freedom to mourn. Spectacle and
distraction keep the population occupied — he uses the Roman formula of
bread and circuses as his example, though the principle extends freely
to any era. Power surrounds itself with religious mystification and
theatrical ceremony, encouraging the belief that rulers are of a
different order of being from the ruled. And the system is sustained by
a pyramid of petty accomplices — ministers, officials, informers, minor
functionaries — each drawing enough advantage from their position to
guarantee their loyalty and implication in the whole.

Most arresting is his concept of the *Malencontre* — a primordial
misfortune, some original moment when humanity slipped from freedom into
submission, and the memory of freedom began its long fade. The concept
functions almost mythically, but its political implication is clear:
servitude is not natural, not inevitable, and not permanent. It is a
condition that was entered into and can be exited, if the desire to do
so can be rekindled.

La Boétie proposes no replacement system. He does not advocate a better
king, a reformed church, or a different constitutional arrangement. He
targets the principle of one person's power over another, and his
solution — the simple, collective withdrawal of consent — requires no
vanguard, no revolution, and no violence.

## Anarchist Influence

La Boétie was not an anarchist in any self-conscious sense, and spent
his adult life as a servant of the very state his text had theoretically
dismantled. Yet the *Discourse* has an anarchist logic that no amount of
biographical context can dissolve. The insistence that all authority
rests on consent, that no replacement system is needed, and that
liberation requires a change of consciousness rather than a change of
regime are not liberal arguments about better governance. They are
anti-authoritarian arguments about the nature of power itself —
arguments that later thinkers in the anarchist tradition would develop
at length, but that La Boétie had already stated with devastating
economy.[^4]

In the 20th century, European anarchists began to cite La Boétie
explicitly. <a href="Gustav_Landauer" class="wikilink"
title="Gustav Landauer">Gustav Landauer</a>,
<a href="Bart_de_Ligt" class="wikilink" title="Bart de Ligt">Bart de
Ligt</a>, and
<a href="Simone_Weil" class="wikilink" title="Simone Weil">Simone
Weil</a> all drew on the *Discourse*.[^5] Autonomist thinker
<a href="John_Holloway" class="wikilink" title="John Holloway">John
Holloway</a> cites him in *<a href="Crack_Capitalism" class="wikilink"
title="Crack Capitalism">Crack Capitalism</a>* as a precursor to his
concept of breaking with capitalist social relations.[^6]
<a href="Gene_Sharp" class="wikilink" title="Gene Sharp">Gene Sharp</a>,
the principal theorist of nonviolent struggle, cites the *Discourse*
repeatedly in both
*<a href="The_Politics_of_Nonviolent_Action" class="wikilink"
title="The Politics of Nonviolent Action">The Politics of Nonviolent
Action</a>* and
*<a href="From_Dictatorship_to_Democracy" class="wikilink"
title="From Dictatorship to Democracy">From Dictatorship to
Democracy</a>*.

The chain of transmission to later movements runs through several hands.
<a href="Henry_David_Thoreau" class="wikilink"
title="Henry David Thoreau">Henry David Thoreau</a>'s *Civil
Disobedience* (1849) carries the same core argument — the duty to
withdraw consent when the state institutionalises injustice — into the
American context.
<a href="Leo_Tolstoy" class="wikilink" title="Leo Tolstoy">Leo
Tolstoy</a>, who translated the *Discourse* into Russian, passed these
ideas to
<a href="Mahatma_Gandhi" class="wikilink" title="Mahatma Gandhi">Mahatma
Gandhi</a>, who developed them into his practice of *Satyagraha*. Gandhi
led the Indian independence movement on the mass withdrawal of popular
consent as its primary weapon.
<a href="Martin_Luther_King_Jr." class="wikilink"
title="Martin Luther King Jr.">Martin Luther King Jr.</a> drew
explicitly on that legacy in the United States. The line from a student
essay written in 1550s France to the Montgomery Bus Boycott is not
straight, but it is traceable.
