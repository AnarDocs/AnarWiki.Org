# Council Communism

## Overview

**Council Communism** (also **Councilism**) is a
<a href="Libertarian_Marxism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian Marxist">libertarian Marxist</a> current that emerged
in the early 1920s, primarily in Germany and the Netherlands, in the
wake of the <a href="October_Revolution_(Russia)" class="wikilink"
title="Russian Revolution">Russian Revolution</a> and the
<a href="German_Revolution_of_1918–1919" class="wikilink"
title="German Revolution">German Revolution</a>. Its central claim is
that democratic <a href="Workers&#39;_Councils" class="wikilink"
title="workers&#39; councils">workers' councils</a> — arising
organically in workplaces and communities — are the only genuine vehicle
for building a communist society.

Council communism rejects both reformist social democracy and
<a href="Leninism" class="wikilink" title="Leninism">Leninism</a>,
arguing that vanguard parties and parliamentary manoeuvring will
inevitably reproduce hierarchies rather than abolish them. It frequently
overlaps with <a href="Anarcho-Syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-syndicalism">anarcho-syndicalism</a> in its suspicion of
party structures and its emphasis on working-class self-organisation,
while retaining a strong engagement with
<a href="Marxism" class="wikilink" title="Marxist">Marxist</a> economic
analysis.

## Origins

### The post-revolutionary break

Council communism emerged from disillusionment with the trajectory of
the Russian Revolution. Its most prominent early proponents were the
German educator
<a href="Otto_Rühle" class="wikilink" title="Otto Rühle">Otto Rühle</a>,
the Dutch astronomer
<a href="Anton_Pannekoek" class="wikilink" title="Anton Pannekoek">Anton
Pannekoek</a>, and the Dutch poet Herman Gorter. Initially all three had
been enthusiastic supporters of the Bolsheviks: in 1918 Gorter declared
that Lenin "stands out above all other leaders of the Proletariat," and
in 1919 Pannekoek wrote that "in Russia communism has been put into
practice for two years now."[^1]

That enthusiasm did not survive contact with Comintern orthodoxy. When
the <a href="Communist_Party_of_Germany" class="wikilink"
title="Communist Party of Germany">Communist Party of Germany</a> (KPD)
was formed in December 1918, a majority opposed electoral politics and
trade unionism — positions to the left of Bolshevik policy. In October
1919, KPD leader Paul Levi pushed through a new party line following
Comintern direction: participation in parliamentary elections and work
within established labour unions. Roughly half the party's 100,000
members refused, and in April 1920 this left majority formed the
<a href="Communist_Workers&#39;_Party_of_Germany" class="wikilink"
title="Communist Workers&#39; Party of Germany">Communist Workers' Party
of Germany</a> (KAPD), with an initial membership of around 38,000. The
same year saw the founding of the General Workers' Union of Germany
(AAUD) as a revolutionary labour union partly modelled on the American
<a href="Industrial_Workers_of_the_World" class="wikilink"
title="IWW">IWW</a>.[^2][^3]

Pannekoek's 1920 pamphlet *World Revolution and Communist Tactics*
argued that communist strategy in Western Europe necessarily differed
from Russia: the bourgeoisie was more entrenched, class struggle had to
take on bourgeois institutions directly, and the vanguard party model
was a potential obstacle rather than an asset. Gorter's *The World
Revolution* (1918) had already pointed to these structural differences
between East and West.[^4]

### The Comintern split

At the Second World Congress of the Comintern in 1920, Lenin, Trotsky,
and Zinoviev unanimously rejected the KAPD's positions. Lenin's 1920
pamphlet *"Left-Wing" Communism: An Infantile Disorder* attacked
Pannekoek and the left groups for "spreading confusion," insisting that
the Russian model revealed something "highly significant" and
"universally valid" for all countries. Gorter replied directly in his
*Open Letter to Comrade Lenin*, arguing that differences in class
structure between East and West made identical tactics
impossible.[^5][^6]

The KAPD initially sought to reform the international communist movement
from within. After failing to rally a left opposition at the Third World
Congress in 1921, it withdrew from the Comintern entirely. The
theoretical break deepened: Pannekoek argued that the Russian Revolution
was in essence a bourgeois revolution; Gorter characterised it as
initially dual in character — simultaneously a working-class revolution
against capitalism and a capitalist revolution against feudalism — but
concluded that the New Economic Policy of 1921 had resolved this
ambiguity, leaving Soviet Russia an unambiguously capitalist state.[^7]

By 1921, according to historian Marcel van der Linden, council communism
had broken with the official communist movement and constituted a
distinct current defined by five positions: that capitalism had to be
abolished immediately; that workers' council democracy must replace it;
that social democracy functioned as a tool of bourgeois manipulation;
that electoral and trade union politics must therefore be boycotted; and
that the Soviet Union represented not an alternative to capitalism but a
new variant of it.[^8]

### Parallel formations

The KAPD's formation prompted analogous splits elsewhere. In September
1921 the Communist Workers' Party of the Netherlands (KAPN) was formed,
though it never exceeded 200 members. In Bulgaria, the Bulgarian
Communist Workers' Party (BRKP) emerged in January 1922 under Ivan
Ganchev's leadership, with just over a thousand members. In Britain,
former suffragist <a href="Sylvia_Pankhurst" class="wikilink"
title="Sylvia Pankhurst">Sylvia Pankhurst</a> was expelled from the
Communist Party of Great Britain in September 1921 and formed the
Communist Workers' Party in February 1922.[^9]

In April 1922 the Essen KAPD and the KAPN formed the Communist Workers'
International (KAI), later joined by the Bulgarian and British
organisations. It claimed a Russian affiliate, in reality consisting of
two Russians living in Berlin. Herman Gorter wrote its programme. The
KAI's structure resembled the Comintern's, but it never achieved
significant influence.[^10][^11]

### Fragmentation and decline

From its inception the KAPD was riven by disputes. Its constituent
tendencies were too varied to hold together and, as Weimar Germany
stabilised in the early 1920s, the tensions became more pronounced.[^12]

Otto Rühle had already argued in his 1920 brochure *Revolution is not a
Party Matter* that the revolutionary movement's goal was to take over
production, not to build a party — any party would inevitably become
opportunist. Rühle and his supporters left the KAPD in November 1920 and
formed the AAUD–Unitary Organisation (AAUD–E), criticising the KAPD for
differing from the KPD only in its rejection of parliamentarism. A
National Bolshevik wing led by Heinrich Laufenberg and Fritz Wolffheim
was expelled at the KAPD's second congress in August 1920; they had
advocated post-revolutionary alliance with the Soviet Union against
Western capital and deployed antisemitic stereotypes in their attacks on
the KPD leadership.[^13]

A further split in March 1922 produced an Essen tendency led by Karl
Schröder and a Berlin tendency, each with its own AAUD affiliate.
Schröder argued that capitalism was in terminal crisis but that workers
were not yet ready, meaning the KAPD's role was to hold firm to strict
revolutionary principles. His opponents agreed capitalism was declining
but drew the opposite tactical conclusion: winning workers over required
flexibility, including participation in wage struggles. Pannekoek stayed
out of the dispute, though his sympathies lay with Berlin. Most
intellectuals, including Gorter, went with Essen.[^14][^15]

By 1923 the German organisations had around 20,000 supporters; by 1933,
a few hundred. The Essen KAPD collapsed fastest — by 1929 it could not
afford its newspaper. The Berlin KAPD spent its remaining years issuing
ignored calls for insurrection. The AAUD–E became an assortment of
tendencies rather than a coherent organisation, losing Rühle in 1925.
Various mergers produced the Communist Workers' Union of Germany in
1931, with a membership of 343.[^16]

### Continuation in small groups

After the Nazis took power in 1933, organised council communism
disappeared in Germany, though small groups continued in the resistance.
In the Netherlands, the Group of International Communists (GIC) served
as a coordinating centre for international debates until the late 1930s,
publishing Henk Canne Meijer's "The Rise of a New Labour Movement" and
Helmut Wagner's "Theses on Bolshevism."[^17]

German emigrant
<a href="Paul_Mattick" class="wikilink" title="Paul Mattick">Paul
Mattick</a> brought council communism to the United States, where he
published the *International Council Correspondence*. J.A. Dawson
published the *Southern Advocate of Workers' Councils* in Australia;
Laín Diez published council communist texts in Chile. The 1960s student
movement produced a brief resurgence, mainly in France, Italy, and
Germany, but after the decline of the 1968 movements the current largely
disappeared again, surviving only in small groups in Europe and North
America.[^18]

## Theory

### Workers' councils as the basis of organisation

The central argument of council communism is that democratic workers'
councils arising in factories and communities are the natural form of
working-class organisation and power. The working class should not rely
on vanguard parties or on reforms of the capitalist system.[^19] A
revolutionary party might play a propagandistic and minority role, but
it cannot substitute for the councils themselves.[^20]

Council communists see the mass strike — and forms of mass action yet to
emerge — as the primary revolutionary means. Where a network of workers'
councils exists, it constitutes the apparatus through which
working-class power operates and develops.[^21]

The government and economy should be managed by workers' councils made
up of delegates elected at workplaces, recallable at any moment. Council
communists oppose authoritarian socialism and reject the idea that a
party-led revolution can avoid becoming a party dictatorship.[^22]

### Steps toward council communism

Council communists generally identify a series of conditions and stages
through which a council communist society would emerge:

- Revolutionary class consciousness develops within the working class
  during a severe crisis of capitalism, encouraged by revolutionary
  organisation.
- Workers establish councils in workplaces and communities as the basic
  organs of the revolution — as happened with the Soviets in Russia and
  the Shoras in the Iranian Revolution.
- Using these councils, workers seize the means of production and
  dismantle the state.
- Society and the economy are then managed and coordinated through the
  council network, with production in each workplace governed by its
  workers through participatory decision-making.
- In workplaces too large for full assembly, decision-making passes to
  councils of recallable delegates who continue to perform normal work
  alongside their fellow workers.
- Delegates do not govern the workplace directly; their role is to
  facilitate discussion, carry out decisions made by those who elected
  them, and coordinate between workplaces and between producers and
  community groups.

### Relation to anarchism

Council communism and other
<a href="Libertarian_Marxism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian Marxist">libertarian Marxist</a> currents such as
<a href="Autonomism" class="wikilink" title="autonomism">autonomism</a>
are often compared to
<a href="Anarchism" class="wikilink" title="anarchism">anarchism</a>,
given their shared critiques of Leninist authoritarianism and their
rejection of the vanguard party.[^23] The overlap with
<a href="Anarcho-Syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-syndicalism">anarcho-syndicalism</a> is particularly
close: both traditions emphasise workplace organisation, suspicion of
parties, and the self-emancipation of the working class rather than
liberation administered from above.

The differences are real, however. Council communism retains the Marxist
framework of historical materialism and tends to conceive of the council
system in terms of the dictatorship of the proletariat — a concept most
anarchists reject as inevitably statist. Anarchists, conversely, are
generally more sceptical of any transitional state form, however
democratic. These tensions were acknowledged within the council
communist tradition itself, particularly by Pannekoek, who engaged
seriously with anarchist critiques.
