## Overview

The <a href="United_States_of_America" class="wikilink"
title="United States of America">United States of America</a> is one of
the largest and most powerful
<a href="capitalism" class="wikilink" title="capitalist">capitalist</a>
states in the world — a constitutional republic built on the colonial
dispossession of indigenous nations, the labour of enslaved people, and
the gradual concentration of wealth in the hands of a ruling class that
has operated through both democratic and repressive means. Its
institutions present themselves as the apex of liberal civilization
while administering a global
<a href="US_Hegemony" class="wikilink" title="hegemony">hegemony</a>
that has overthrown governments, sponsored coups, and waged wars across
every inhabited continent.

The United States was born out of the
<a href="American_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="American Revolution">American Revolution</a>, as wealthy
landlords and slaveowners forged their own nation-state away from the
<a href="British_Empire" class="wikilink" title="British Empire">British
Empire</a>. Progressive forces for land and wealth reform were rapidly
suppressed, and neighbouring indigenous nations were conquered through a
slow process of manipulation, war and genocide. Black people and women
remained in conditions of subjugation while limited democratic rights
for white men were consolidated. The
<a href="American_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="American Civil War">American Civil War</a> abolished most of the
slaveholding elements of the economy, but the economy became
increasingly dominated by capitalism and corporations, replacing one
form of exploitation with another.

Anarchists in the United States have consistently refused the terms on
which American political life presents itself. Where liberal ideology
offers managed electoral competition, anarchists have pointed to what
persists regardless of which party governs: the state's monopoly on
violence, the dominance of capital over labour, the policing of dissent,
and the maintenance of racial hierarchy. The mythology of American
freedom has been a particular target, as has the equation of capitalism
with liberty that runs through both mainstream conservatism and
mainstream liberalism alike.

The history of the United States is also a history of political
repression aimed specifically at radical and anarchist organizing — from
the execution of the <a href="Haymarket_affair" class="wikilink"
title="Haymarket">Haymarket</a> defendants, to the mass deportations of
the <a href="Palmer_Raids" class="wikilink" title="Palmer Raids">Palmer
Raids</a>, to the FBI's
<a href="COINTELPRO" class="wikilink" title="COINTELPRO">COINTELPRO</a>
programme. This repression reflects the state's recognition that
anti-authoritarian organizing poses a different kind of challenge than
opposition that accepts the legitimacy of the system it criticises.

## History

### Early anarchism and individualism

The earliest anarchist currents in the United States grew from the
specific conditions of early nineteenth-century American life, in which
most working people were self-employed farmers or artisans, and the full
subordination of labour to capital had not yet taken hold.
<a href="Josiah_Warren" class="wikilink" title="Josiah Warren">Josiah
Warren</a>, considered the first American anarchist, emerged from
<a href="Robert_Owen" class="wikilink" title="Robert Owen">Robert
Owen</a>'s experimental commune at
<a href="New_Harmony,_Indiana" class="wikilink"
title="New Harmony, Indiana">New Harmony, Indiana</a> in the 1820s.
Warren developed a theory of "individual sovereignty" and proposed
replacing capitalist exchange with labour-time certificates, putting his
ideas into practice through a series of cooperative ventures including
the <a href="Cincinnati_Time_Store" class="wikilink"
title="Cincinnati Time Store">Cincinnati Time Store</a> and the
intentional communities of Utopia and Modern Times.[^1]

<a href="Henry_David_Thoreau" class="wikilink"
title="Henry David Thoreau">Henry David Thoreau</a> was a formative
influence on individualist anarchist thought both in the United States
and beyond. His 1849 essay
*<a href="Civil_Disobedience_(Thoreau)" class="wikilink"
title="Civil Disobedience">Civil Disobedience</a>* argued that
individuals have a duty not to permit governments to make them
instruments of injustice, driven by his opposition to slavery and the
Mexican-American War. His book
*<a href="Walden" class="wikilink" title="Walden">Walden</a>*, an
account of deliberate withdrawal from industrial society into simple
living, made him an early precursor of ecological anarchism, later
recognized as such by writers including
<a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a> and
<a href="John_Zerzan" class="wikilink" title="John Zerzan">John
Zerzan</a>.[^2]

<a href="Voltairine_de_Cleyre" class="wikilink"
title="Voltairine de Cleyre">Voltairine de Cleyre</a> was among the most
important anarchist voices of the late nineteenth century — a prolific
writer and speaker who moved from individualism through mutualism toward
what she called "<a href="anarchism_without_adjectives" class="wikilink"
title="anarchism without adjectives">anarchism without adjectives</a>",
holding that no single economic form should be imposed on a free
society. Her 1895 lecture *Sex Slavery* attacked marriage laws as a
system of legal rape, arguing they made "every married woman a bonded
slave, who takes her master's name, her master's bread, her master's
commands, and serves her master's passions." She brought together
opposition to the state, to institutional religion, and to the
domination of women into a single framework decades before such
syntheses became common.[^3]

An important strand within American individualist anarchism was
<a href="Free_Love" class="wikilink" title="free love">free love</a> —
the demand that sexual relationships be freed from state and religious
regulation. The journal
*<a href="Lucifer_the_Lightbearer" class="wikilink"
title="Lucifer the Lightbearer">Lucifer the Lightbearer</a>*
(1883–1907), edited by
<a href="Moses_Harman" class="wikilink" title="Moses Harman">Moses
Harman</a> and <a href="Lois_Waisbrooker" class="wikilink"
title="Lois Waisbrooker">Lois Waisbrooker</a>, took as its mission
helping women "break the chains that for ages have bound her to the rack
of man-made law." Its editors were repeatedly prosecuted under the
<a href="Comstock_laws" class="wikilink" title="Comstock laws">Comstock
laws</a> for publishing material on contraception, rape within marriage,
and sexual autonomy. Harman spent years in prison; the journal continued
despite sustained state harassment for more than two decades.[^4]

<a href="Benjamin_Tucker" class="wikilink"
title="Benjamin Tucker">Benjamin Tucker</a>'s journal
*<a href="Liberty_(1881–1908)" class="wikilink"
title="Liberty">Liberty</a>* (1881–1908) was the central forum for
American individualist anarchism — publishing
<a href="Lysander_Spooner" class="wikilink"
title="Lysander Spooner">Lysander Spooner</a>, de Cleyre, and Tucker's
own increasingly egoist positions alongside translations of
<a href="Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon" class="wikilink"
title="Pierre-Joseph Proudhon">Pierre-Joseph Proudhon</a> and
<a href="Max_Stirner" class="wikilink" title="Max Stirner">Max
Stirner</a>. Tucker identified himself as a socialist in the sense of
opposing usury and the concentration of capital, though he drifted
toward an egoism that eventually broke with natural rights
altogether.[^5]

### Anarcho-communism and the labour movement

By the 1880s anarcho-communism had established itself as a distinct
current, centred on the labour movement and the fight for the eight-hour
day. <a href="Lucy_Parsons" class="wikilink" title="Lucy Parsons">Lucy
Parsons</a> co-founded the journal *Freedom: A Revolutionary
Anarchist-Communist Monthly* and, with her husband
<a href="Albert_Parsons" class="wikilink" title="Albert Parsons">Albert
Parsons</a>, led tens of thousands of workers through Chicago on the
first May Day march in 1886. Days later, at a rally in
<a href="Haymarket_Square" class="wikilink"
title="Haymarket Square">Haymarket Square</a>, a bomb was thrown by an
unknown party and police opened fire on the crowd. Eight anarchists were
arrested; four were executed and a fifth died in his cell before the
sentence could be carried out, in what was widely understood as a
political frame-up.[^6]

The <a href="Haymarket_affair" class="wikilink"
title="Haymarket affair">Haymarket affair</a> became a defining moment
for the international anarchist and labour movements.
<a href="Emma_Goldman" class="wikilink" title="Emma Goldman">Emma
Goldman</a> described the executions as "the events that had inspired my
spiritual birth and growth." Goldman went on to become the central
figure of American anarchism in the early twentieth century — a
theorist, orator, and organizer whose writing and lectures addressed
prisons, militarism, capitalism, marriage, free love, and homosexuality.
She was among the first in the United States to defend the rights of gay
people in public. With <a href="Alexander_Berkman" class="wikilink"
title="Alexander Berkman">Alexander Berkman</a> she attempted the
assassination of Homestead factory manager
<a href="Henry_Clay_Frick" class="wikilink"
title="Henry Clay Frick">Henry Clay Frick</a> in 1892, in response to
the killing of striking workers; Berkman served fourteen years in
prison. Goldman was imprisoned multiple times, for "inciting to riot"
and for distributing information about birth control. In 1917 both she
and Berkman were sentenced to two years for opposing conscription, and
on their release were deported to Russia alongside hundreds of other
radicals swept up in the
<a href="Palmer_Raids" class="wikilink" title="Palmer Raids">Palmer
Raids</a>.[^7]

Goldman's experience of the Russian Revolution was decisive. She and
Berkman toured the country during the Civil War period and found
repression, mismanagement, and the crushing of independent working-class
organization. She published her account as
*<a href="My_Disillusionment_in_Russia" class="wikilink"
title="My Disillusionment in Russia">My Disillusionment in Russia</a>*
(1923). The anarchist critique of Bolshevism — that seizing state power
reproduces domination regardless of the intentions of those who hold it
— was confirmed for Goldman by direct experience, and her account
remained a reference point for anti-authoritarian critique of the Soviet
Union for decades afterward.

<a href="Johann_Most" class="wikilink" title="Johann Most">Johann
Most</a>, who had moved to the United States in 1882, was the leading
proponent of <a href="propaganda_of_the_deed" class="wikilink"
title="propaganda of the deed">propaganda of the deed</a> — the view
that acts of political violence against ruling-class figures could
catalyse mass revolt. His pamphlet
*<a href="The_Science_of_Revolutionary_Warfare" class="wikilink"
title="The Science of Revolutionary Warfare">The Science of
Revolutionary Warfare</a>* (1885) earned him the name "Dynamost." Most
attracted numerous followers but also critics within the anarchist
movement, including eventually Goldman herself, who came to view his
politics as authoritarian. The Galleanist current, inspired by the
Italian anarchist
<a href="Luigi_Galleani" class="wikilink" title="Luigi Galleani">Luigi
Galleani</a>, carried out a series of bombings and assassination
attempts between 1914 and 1932. The trial and execution of
<a href="Nicola_Sacco_and_Bartolomeo_Vanzetti" class="wikilink"
title="Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti">Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo
Vanzetti</a> in 1927 — widely regarded as a judicial murder motivated by
their anarchist beliefs — provoked international outrage and a wave of
solidarity strikes and protests.[^8]

The <a href="Industrial_Workers_of_the_World" class="wikilink"
title="Industrial Workers of the World">Industrial Workers of the
World</a> (IWW), founded in Chicago in 1905 by socialists, anarchists,
and radical trade unionists, represented the syndicalist current within
American radicalism — building industrial unions that could challenge
capitalist power at the point of production and prefigure a self-managed
economy. Its membership included miners, timber workers, and
agricultural labourers across the western United States, and it was met
with sustained state and employer violence. The IWW's commitment to
organising across race and gender lines, and its rejection of business
unionism, made it both distinctive and a specific target of repression.

### Modern Schools and anarchist education

The <a href="Modern_School_(United_States)" class="wikilink"
title="Modern Schools">Modern Schools</a>, modelled on the
<a href="Escuela_Moderna" class="wikilink"
title="Escuela Moderna">Escuela Moderna</a> of the executed Catalan
anarchist <a href="Francesc_Ferrer_i_Guàrdia" class="wikilink"
title="Francesc Ferrer i Guàrdia">Francesc Ferrer i Guàrdia</a>,
established a network of libertarian schools across the United States in
the early twentieth century. The first opened in New York City in 1911,
founded by Goldman, Berkman, de Cleyre, and others. The schools combined
daytime classes for children with evening lectures for adults,
emphasising secular learning, academic freedom, and class-conscious
education. The most developed was the Stelton Colony in New Jersey,
which operated for decades as both a school and an intentional
community, the last surviving school lasting until 1958.[^9]

### Post-war anarchism

After the devastation of the First and Second World Wars,
anarcho-pacifism became a significant tendency within American
radicalism. The Catholic anarchist
<a href="Dorothy_Day" class="wikilink" title="Dorothy Day">Dorothy
Day</a>, who co-founded the
<a href="Catholic_Worker_movement" class="wikilink"
title="Catholic Worker movement">Catholic Worker movement</a> in 1933
with <a href="Peter_Maurin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Maurin">Peter
Maurin</a>, combined direct aid for the poor with nonviolent direct
action — refusing to pay taxes, organising against conscription, and
practising civil disobedience over decades. This tendency fed into the
Beat Generation's anti-authoritarian sensibility, influencing writers
including
<a href="Allen_Ginsberg" class="wikilink" title="Allen Ginsberg">Allen
Ginsberg</a> and
<a href="Gary_Snyder" class="wikilink" title="Gary Snyder">Gary
Snyder</a>.[^10]

<a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a> defined himself as an
anarchist in 1958 and became the most theoretically ambitious figure in
post-war American anarchism. His essay "Ecology and Revolutionary
Thought" introduced ecology as a concept in radical politics; his
*<a href="Post-Scarcity_Anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="Post-Scarcity Anarchism">Post-Scarcity Anarchism</a>* (1971)
argued that advanced industrial technology had created the material
conditions for a society without scarcity, making the case for
decentralised, ecologically grounded self-governance. His later work
developed the theory of
<a href="libertarian_municipalism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian municipalism">libertarian municipalism</a> — the
building of self-governing neighbourhood assemblies as a counterpower to
the state — which eventually influenced the model of
<a href="Democratic_Confederalism" class="wikilink"
title="Democratic Confederalism">Democratic Confederalism</a> as
practised in Rojava.[^11]

The <a href="Libertarian_League" class="wikilink"
title="Libertarian League">Libertarian League</a>, founded in New York
City in 1954, brought together anarcho-syndicalists including
<a href="Sam_Dolgoff" class="wikilink" title="Sam Dolgoff">Sam
Dolgoff</a> and
<a href="Dave_Van_Ronk" class="wikilink" title="Dave Van Ronk">Dave Van
Ronk</a> around a commitment to "equal freedom for all in a free
socialist society." Dolgoff remained active in the IWW throughout his
life and produced important historical work on the
<a href="Spanish_Revolution_of_1936" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish anarchist collectives">Spanish anarchist collectives</a>.[^12]

### Counterculture and the 1960s

Anarchism fed into the counterculture of the 1960s through multiple
channels. The Diggers — taking their name from the seventeenth-century
English <a href="Diggers" class="wikilink" title="Diggers">Diggers</a> —
opened free stores in San Francisco, distributed free food, and
performed political street theatre, creating zones of life outside the
money economy. The Yippies, led by
<a href="Abbie_Hoffman" class="wikilink" title="Abbie Hoffman">Abbie
Hoffman</a>, used theatrical provocation to mock the political
establishment.
<a href="David_Graeber" class="wikilink" title="David Graeber">David
Graeber</a> later argued that both currents were "squarely within the
anarchist tradition" in their emphasis on building autonomous practices
rather than appealing to existing power.[^13]

<a href="Noam_Chomsky" class="wikilink" title="Noam Chomsky">Noam
Chomsky</a>, whose political work from the 1960s onward combined
anarcho-syndicalist commitments with systematic critique of US foreign
policy, became the most widely read anarchist intellectual of the late
twentieth century. His analysis of the propaganda model of media,
developed with
<a href="Edward_Herman" class="wikilink" title="Edward Herman">Edward
Herman</a> in *<a href="Manufacturing_Consent" class="wikilink"
title="Manufacturing Consent">Manufacturing Consent</a>* (1988),
provided tools for understanding how consent to imperial policy is
manufactured through ostensibly free institutions.

### Anti-globalization and Occupy

The <a href="1999_Seattle_WTO_protests" class="wikilink"
title="1999 Seattle WTO protests">1999 Seattle WTO protests</a> brought
anarchists to mass visibility in the United States, as
<a href="black_bloc" class="wikilink" title="black bloc">black bloc</a>
tactics, affinity groups, and decentralized direct action confronted the
institutions of global capital in the streets. Anarchists became
prominent at protests against the WTO, G8, and World Economic Forum
throughout the following years. The
<a href="Occupy_movement" class="wikilink"
title="Occupy movement">Occupy movement</a> of 2011, with its leaderless
structure and horizontal organization, drew directly on anarchist
practices disseminated through decades of movement-building. Graeber
played a central role in convening the first New York City General
Assembly and developing the movement's organizational framework.[^14]

In the wake of <a href="Hurricane_Katrina" class="wikilink"
title="Hurricane Katrina">Hurricane Katrina</a> in 2005, anarchists were
founding members of the
<a href="Common_Ground_Collective" class="wikilink"
title="Common Ground Collective">Common Ground Collective</a>, providing
disaster relief organized outside state and NGO structures — a practical
demonstration of
<a href="mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="mutual aid">mutual aid</a>
under conditions of state failure.

### Recent years

During the COVID-19 pandemic, anarchists participated in a proliferation
of mutual aid networks across the country, filling gaps left by the
state's failure to provide for people's basic needs. In June 2020, the
<a href="Capitol_Hill_Autonomous_Zone" class="wikilink"
title="Capitol Hill Autonomous Zone">Capitol Hill Autonomous Zone</a>
was established in Seattle during the
<a href="George_Floyd_protests" class="wikilink"
title="George Floyd protests">George Floyd protests</a>, creating a
temporary self-governed space in the neighbourhood around an occupied
police precinct. In January 2023, eco-anarchist Manuel Esteban Paez
Terán was shot and killed by Atlanta police during the
<a href="Stop_Cop_City" class="wikilink" title="Stop Cop City">Stop Cop
City</a> protests against the construction of a police training facility
on forest land. In February 2024, anarchist and US Air Force serviceman
Aaron Bushnell self-immolated outside the Israeli embassy in Washington
DC in protest against the genocide in Gaza.

The <a href="Black_Rose_Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Black Rose Anarchist Federation">Black Rose Anarchist
Federation</a> (BRRN), founded in 2013 from a merger of several regional
groups, pursues a platformist orientation combining anarcho-communism,
syndicalism, and <a href="especifismo" class="wikilink"
title="especifismo">especifismo</a>, with an explicit commitment to
anti-racism and building popular power outside electoral politics.[^15]
