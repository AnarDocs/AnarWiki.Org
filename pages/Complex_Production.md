## Complex Production Under Anarchism

The microchip is often raised as the test case that anarchism cannot
pass. Modern semiconductors require supply chains spanning dozens of
countries, fabrication plants costing tens of billions of dollars,
decades of accumulated specialist knowledge, and the kind of process
discipline that seems to demand top-down command. If anarchists cannot
account for the chip in your phone, the argument runs, then anarchism is
at best a politics for handicrafts and small farms, with no answer to
the demands of an industrialised world.

This argument has a long history and an older shape. Leninists in the
1920s claimed that anarchists wanted "small autonomous communities,
devoted to small scale production",[^1] and the same charge was levelled
at <a href="Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon" class="wikilink"
title="Proudhon">Proudhon</a>,
<a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a>
and <a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink"
title="Kropotkin">Kropotkin</a> by their Marxist contemporaries.
<a href="Luigi_Fabbri" class="wikilink" title="Luigi Fabbri">Luigi
Fabbri</a>, replying to Bukharin, asked simply where in any anarchist
book or programme such an "ideal" had ever been set out.[^2] The answer
was that it had not. Proudhon explicitly rejected the notion that
workers should "return to the system of primitive indivision" and argued
that large industry should "rise from the \[workers'\] association".[^3]
Bakunin warned that dividing existing capital among individual workers
would "reduce to a large degree its productive power" and called instead
for "the collective property of capital" amalgamated into "one vast
economic federation".[^4] Kropotkin, often miscast as a romantic of the
village, wrote that he "understood the poetry of machinery" and that the
misery of factory work "has nothing to do with the machine itself" but
with "bad organisation".[^5] His vision in *Fields, Factories and
Workshops* was of regions large enough to dispose of varied resources,
integrating agriculture and industry, with "large establishments" still
existing where the work objectively required them.[^6]

The microchip case can be approached on this same ground. Anarchists
have never proposed abandoning complex production; they have proposed
organising it without bosses, landlords, shareholders or commissars.

## Why microchips

The chip is chosen because it appears to be the hardest possible case. A
leading-edge fabrication plant of the kind operated by TSMC in Taiwan or
Samsung in South Korea costs twenty to thirty billion dollars to build.
Its processes operate at the physical limits of matter, with features
measured in nanometres and cleanrooms maintained at Class 1
contamination levels. The supply chain reaches into cobalt mines in the
Democratic Republic of the Congo, lithium fields in Chile, lithography
machines from ASML in the Netherlands, photoresists from Japan and
assembly lines across East Asia. The knowledge required to design and
operate any part of this is the product of decades of specialist
training, much of it locked behind corporate intellectual property
regimes.

Three claims tend to follow from this picture. The first is that only
enormous concentrations of capital, whether private or state, can fund
such an enterprise. The second is that only rigid hierarchical command
can enforce the process discipline required. The third is that only
monetary or state incentives can motivate the long, narrow specialism
the work demands. Each of these claims deserves to be taken seriously,
but none of them survives close inspection.

It is worth pausing on a fact often glossed over in these arguments.
Microchips are made by people. Whether the setting is a Silicon Valley
corporation, a Taiwanese foundry or a Soviet-era research institute, the
actual work is done by human beings using accumulated human knowledge
and physical raw materials. What changes between these settings is the
economic and political form within which that work is organised, and
frequently the limits placed upon the technology by that form. The chip
exists because of human cooperation, not because of capitalism or the
state.

## What hierarchy and profit actually do

The case for hierarchy and profit usually rests on the claim that they
coordinate production at scale. The historical record suggests instead
that they coordinate it badly, and at enormous human and ecological
cost.

The Soviet experience with microelectronics is instructive precisely
because it is a cautionary tale about state coordination, not about
coordination as such. Soviet engineers had access to the underlying
science, substantial industrial capacity and trained personnel. What the
system lacked was horizontal information flow between institutions,
feedback from end users, and incentive structures that rewarded honest
reporting of problems. Factories had every reason to hit quotas on paper
and none to expose flaws that would damage their statistics before they
could be corrected. When Soviet designers could copy Western chips they
did reasonably well; when they had to innovate without reference points
the bureaucratic structure strangled the process. The Soviet Union
failed at chips not because coordination is impossible but because
hierarchical coordination is brittle, information-poor and perversely
incentivised.

China's contemporary semiconductor policy presents a different lesson.
Beijing is outspending every other state on chip fabrication, with
hundreds of billions directed through SMIC, Huawei's HiSilicon and the
broader industrial-policy apparatus, and yet it cannot reliably produce
leading-edge chips domestically. The bottleneck is in equipment,
particularly the extreme ultraviolet lithography machines made by ASML
in the Netherlands, which the United States has pressured the Dutch
government into withholding. State-backed vertical integration on a vast
scale fails the moment the relevant knowledge and tooling are held
elsewhere. Meanwhile the supply chain on which Chinese production
depends is endemic with exploitation: cobalt mined by children in the
Congo, silicon refined under poor labour and environmental controls,
assembly facilities in the Foxconn mould notorious for their conditions
and their suicides.

Taiwan is the most interesting case for the anarchist argument. TSMC
produces the overwhelming majority of the world's most advanced chips
from a small island state with a modest population. It does not dominate
because of the scale of its sponsoring state; it dominates because of
decades of accumulated tacit knowledge concentrated in the dense Hsinchu
Science Park, a culture of intense technical specialism and
information-sharing within the cluster (even across nominally competing
firms), and Morris Chang's decision to make TSMC a pure-play foundry,
serving customers rather than competing with them. The model relies on a
global network of suppliers rather than autarchy. What makes TSMC work
resembles a dense, trust-based, collaborative network far more than it
resembles a command hierarchy, even though the legal form is a
corporation.

The suffering in the chip supply chain is not evenly distributed. It
concentrates at the bottom, in extraction and assembly, where labour is
cheapest and least organised, and the profit concentrates at the top, in
design and intellectual property. This is not incidental to the system;
it is the system. Capitalism does not produce chips because private
ownership is efficient. It produces them despite the inefficiencies of
private ownership, because the profit motive concentrates enough capital
to overcome the coordination problems that hierarchy itself creates.

## Coordination without bosses

The objection that anarchism cannot coordinate at scale assumes that
coordination requires command. The actual record shows otherwise. The
collectivised industries of revolutionary Catalonia between 1936 and
1939, run by worker committees without bosses, sustained production
under wartime conditions until they were militarily destroyed. The
medieval guild system coordinated complex craft production across Europe
through horizontal networks of mutual obligation. The entire open-source
software ecosystem is a massive, non-hierarchical, globally distributed
production network creating infrastructure of immense practical and
economic value.
<a href="Wikipedia" class="wikilink" title="Wikipedia">Wikipedia</a>
coordinates hundreds of thousands of contributors without wages or
bosses, and in blind peer review has been judged comparable in accuracy
to the *Encyclopædia Britannica*.[^7] The technical standards of the
internet itself, the protocols on which the entire digital economy
depends, are set by the IETF through a rough consensus process with no
single authority.

The relevant precedents in chip development are now substantial. RISC-V
is an open instruction set architecture released without licensing fees
or usage restrictions, developed and improved by a globally distributed
community of engineers, universities and hobbyist projects, now in
production across applications from embedded systems to server-grade
processors. It is anarchist epistemology applied to chip design:
decentralised contribution, no single authority controlling the
knowledge, improvements returned to the commons. The OpenROAD project
and other open-source electronic design automation tools are maturing as
alternatives to the proprietary software of Cadence and Synopsys.
Efabless and Google's Open MPW programme have produced functional
silicon from open-source designs through shared multi-project wafer
runs, with hundreds of community-designed chips taped out and
fabricated. Tiny Tapeout has brought chip design within reach of
students and hobbyists at modest cost. Andrew "bunnie" Huang's Novena
project produced an entirely open-source laptop, designed to be built,
modified and repaired by its users. The community around *Chips and
Cheese* performs detailed analyses of commercial chips at a level once
confined to the inside of corporations. Individual experimenters have
fabricated working integrated circuits in home laboratories, including
the small runs of dynamic RAM produced by Sam Zeloof in his parents'
garage using equipment salvaged and built from scratch.

None of this yet produces a 3-nanometre server chip in a kitchen. What
it demonstrates is that the knowledge layer of chip design is
increasingly held in common, and that the fabrication barrier is a
question of capital and equipment rather than of any inherent need for
capitalist or state ownership.

The argument that because something has not yet happened it therefore
cannot or will not happen is one with an unfortunate pedigree. It was
made against the abolition of slavery, against the end of feudalism,
against universal suffrage and against every revolution that did in fact
occur, including the one in Russia in 1917. The fact that no anarchist
society has yet built a chip fab proves only that no anarchist society
has yet built a chip fab.

## Transforming an existing fab

The most concrete way to address the question is to ask what would have
to change for an existing chip fabrication plant to become an anarchist
one. The change is not a single moment of takeover but a layered
transformation, each layer reinforcing the others.

The ownership change is the most direct. The fab is held as inalienable
collective property by those who work in it and the federated
communities it serves. It cannot be sold, used as collateral or
extracted from the network that depends on it. The legal vehicle matters
less than the principle that no individual or group can accumulate
claims over it. Suppliers become federated partners rather than vendors:
chemical producers, equipment maintainers and raw-material refiners,
each themselves under worker or community governance, related to the fab
through federated agreements rather than through priced market
transactions. The land beneath the facility passes into a community
trust, removing it from speculative ownership.

The management change addresses what is genuinely demanding about a fab.
Cleanrooms require rigorous process discipline; equipment worth hundreds
of millions needs careful maintenance. This is sometimes presented as
proof that hierarchy is necessary, but it is only proof that
coordination is necessary, which is a different thing. The workers'
assembly becomes the sovereign body, with all workers across all roles
having equal standing in decisions about the direction of the facility.
Self-organising functional circles take responsibility within their
domains, in process engineering, equipment maintenance, health and
safety, and logistics, reporting back to the assembly rather than
upwards to managers. Coordination roles rotate rather than accumulating
into a permanent managerial caste. Wage hierarchies flatten or give way
to needs-based remuneration. The genuine challenge is that fabs depend
on tacit knowledge that senior engineers carry and that is rarely
written down; the transition needs to actively transfer that knowledge
into the collective through documentation, mentorship and a culture that
treats expertise as something to be shared rather than hoarded.

The production change is where the anarchist model diverges most sharply
from both capitalism and state planning. A capitalist fab produces
whatever generates the highest margin, which is why leading-edge
capacity flows to graphics processors for AI data centres and high-end
consumer devices while genuinely useful applications such as medical
sensors, agricultural monitoring and low-cost computing for the global
South go underserved. A state-planned fab produces what the central
authority prioritises, with all the information and incentive failures
that entails. A commons fab asks instead what the federated network of
communities it is accountable to actually needs. Production planning
happens through federated demand aggregation: medical cooperatives need
biosensor chips, community energy networks need power-management
silicon, educational initiatives need cheap repairable computing, and
these become the priorities. Designs are released as open hardware by
default, the fab participating in and contributing to the same commons
that produced RISC-V. Without the planned-obsolescence pressure of
consumer capitalism, production shifts towards chips designed to last,
to be diagnosed and to be replaced at the component rather than the
device level, dramatically reducing material throughput. Environmental
limits become genuine constraints on what is made, with the communities
bearing the impact holding real authority rather than the consultation
rights that are all states currently grant them.

The distribution change breaks the price mechanism's grip on who gets
what. Chips currently flow toward whoever can pay most, which is why so
much capacity ends up in surveillance systems and luxury devices rather
than the hospitals and schools of poorer regions. Federated allocation
replaces market distribution: chips move through networks of established
mutual obligation between communities and cooperatives, with
needs-critical applications such as medical equipment, infrastructure
and food systems given priority over extractive or luxury uses.
Transparency runs through the chain, so every community can see
capacity, allocations and the reasoning behind them. The communities
doing the most dangerous and lowest-paid work in the present system, in
extraction and assembly, gain genuine standing in the federated
governance rather than remaining the most disposable link in a
profit-maximising chain.

The transition is not simple. It faces real obstacles, particularly
around capital equipment (no federation builds an EUV machine overnight)
and around geopolitics (states will not passively allow strategic assets
to leave their control). The model is nonetheless coherent, has
historical analogues at smaller scales, and addresses the actual
pathologies of the present system rather than merely moderating them.

## What capitalism does that anarchism would not

A commons-oriented production model has no incentive to design chips to
fail, no reason to lock users into upgrade cycles and no use for the
patent thickets that consume enormous resources in licensing and
litigation while slowing development. The geopolitical chip war between
the United States and China, which is currently destroying coordination
and efficiency on a vast scale in the service of state power, has no
mechanism within a federated global commons. The civilisational
fragility produced by the concentration of advanced fabrication in a
single firm in a single contested territory gives way to the resilience
of distributed production. The exploitation that concentrates surplus
upward through the supply chain is replaced by horizontal distribution
among the people who actually do the work.

The strongest version of the argument is not that an anarchist society
could produce chips just like capitalism does, only nicer. It is that
the specific ways in which microchip production is broken under
capitalism, exploitation, hoarding, planned obsolescence, geopolitical
weaponisation, fragility, are direct products of hierarchy and private
ownership rather than necessary features of producing microchips at all.
Removing the hierarchy removes the pathologies, not the capacity.

## When complexity is the wrong question

A final possibility deserves to be named directly. There may be
technologies whose complexity rests on suffering that cannot be removed
without losing the technology itself. If a particular chip cannot be
made without children dying in cobalt mines, without communities
poisoned by refining runoff, without workers ground down by repetitive
injuries, then the anarchist answer is that the chip should not be made.
Technology is not neutral. As
<a href="Uri_Gordon" class="wikilink" title="Uri Gordon">Uri Gordon</a>
put it, the development of technology reflects the interests and needs
of the ruling members of society and reshapes the physical world in ways
that reinforce authority and discourage rebellion.[^8] Solar steam
engines were viable two centuries ago and were sidelined when the
British Empire conquered both a coal deposit and the population to mine
it. The technologies that flourish are the ones compatible with the
social relations of the moment, and a different set of social relations
would produce a different set of technologies.

This is not primitivism. The point is not to abandon complex production
but to refuse to treat the products of capitalism as the only possible
measure of what complex production can be. An anarchist society would
invent. The Bonnot gang invented the getaway car.
<a href="Nestor_Makhno" class="wikilink" title="Makhno">Makhno</a>
mounted machine guns on peasant carts and outmanoeuvred armies. The
collectives of the <a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a> founded an
agricultural university and a centre for the study of plant diseases.
The highlanders of New Guinea developed seven thousand years of
sustainable intensive agriculture without states or markets, using
techniques sophisticated enough that Western scientists still do not
fully understand them.[^9] The free software movement built the
infrastructure of the modern internet by gift. The capacity for complex
coordination is not the property of capitalism or the state; it is a
property of human beings cooperating, and capitalism and the state are
very often what gets in the way.

The chip in the phone is not a refutation of anarchism. It is a test
that anarchism has the resources to meet, and a reminder that the ways
things are made now are not the only ways they can be made.
