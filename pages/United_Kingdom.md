# United Kingdom Of Great Britain And Northern Ireland

## Overview

The
<a href="United_Kingdom" class="wikilink" title="United Kingdom">United
Kingdom</a> is a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary state
comprising England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, formed through
centuries of conquest, union and colonial expansion. Its institutions —
the Crown, Parliament, the established Church, the City of London —
represent one of the oldest and most durable concentrations of
ruling-class power in the world. The British state also administered one
of the largest empires in history, spreading capitalism, enclosure and
colonial violence across much of the globe.

Anarchists in Britain have long regarded the United Kingdom's
constitutional arrangements not as a bulwark of liberty but as a
sophisticated mechanism for managing class rule — one that incorporates
dissent, grants limited reforms, and thereby stabilises a fundamentally
hierarchical order. The apparent moderation of British political life
has historically made radical organizing more difficult, with successive
labour movements drawn into the orbit of parliamentary politics and away
from more direct challenges to state and capital. The
<a href="Labour_Party" class="wikilink" title="Labour Party">Labour
Party</a>, in particular, has functioned — in the anarchist analysis —
as a vehicle for absorbing working-class energy into the management of
capitalism rather than its abolition.

The
<a href="British_Empire" class="wikilink" title="British Empire">British
Empire</a> occupies a specific place in anarchist thinking about the
United Kingdom. The extraction of wealth from colonised peoples, the
suppression of indigenous self-organization, and the global reach of
British military and financial power are understood not as aberrations
but as expressions of the same logic of domination that structures life
at home. British anarchists have therefore often linked domestic
struggles against the state and capital to anti-colonial and
anti-imperialist movements abroad.

## Anarchism Within The United Kingdom

Anarchist ideas in Britain have roots stretching back to the
<a href="Peasants&#39;_Revolt" class="wikilink"
title="Peasants&#39; Revolt">Peasants' Revolt</a> of 1381 and the
radical religious dissent that followed the
<a href="English_Reformation" class="wikilink"
title="English Reformation">English Reformation</a>. The tradition took
recognizable philosophical form during the
<a href="English_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="English Civil War">English Civil War</a>, developed through
<a href="Enlightenment" class="wikilink"
title="Enlightenment">Enlightenment</a> radicalism, and found its first
systematic expression in
<a href="William_Godwin" class="wikilink" title="William Godwin">William
Godwin</a>'s
*<a href="Enquiry_Concerning_Political_Justice" class="wikilink"
title="Enquiry Concerning Political Justice">Enquiry Concerning
Political Justice</a>* (1793) — widely regarded as the founding text of
modern <a href="philosophical_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="philosophical anarchism">philosophical anarchism</a>.

From the mid-nineteenth century onward, classical anarchism arrived in
Britain largely through <a href="migrant_workers" class="wikilink"
title="migrant workers">migrant workers</a> and political exiles, and
took organized form with the founding of the
<a href="Labour_Emancipation_League" class="wikilink"
title="Labour Emancipation League">Labour Emancipation League</a> in
1881. Over the following century and a half the movement passed through
successive phases: anarcho-communist agitation around the turn of the
twentieth century, <a href="syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="syndicalist">syndicalist</a> organizing in the 1910s, the impact
of the <a href="Spanish_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a>, post-war pacifism, the
<a href="anarcho-punk" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-punk">anarcho-punk</a> explosion of the late 1970s, and
the anti-globalisation and anti-war movements of the early twenty-first
century.

## History

### Medieval precursors

The historian
<a href="Peter_Marshall" class="wikilink" title="Peter Marshall">Peter
Marshall</a> traced the roots of British anarchism back to the
<a href="Peasants&#39;_Revolt" class="wikilink"
title="Peasants&#39; Revolt">Peasants' Revolt</a> of 1381, when yeomen
rose against the poll tax of the
<a href="Bad_Parliament" class="wikilink" title="Bad Parliament">Bad
Parliament</a>, fearing it as a move to force them into serfdom.[^1] The
radical priest
<a href="John_Ball_(priest)" class="wikilink" title="John Ball">John
Ball</a> drew on the imagery of the
<a href="Garden_of_Eden" class="wikilink" title="Garden of Eden">Garden
of Eden</a> as a state of nature free from class stratification,
attacked <a href="private_property" class="wikilink"
title="private property">private property</a> and social inequality, and
called for common ownership and a classless society.[^2] With
<a href="Wat_Tyler" class="wikilink" title="Wat Tyler">Wat Tyler</a> at
their head, some 100,000 rebels marched on London, demolished the
<a href="Savoy_Palace" class="wikilink" title="Savoy Palace">Savoy
Palace</a>, freed prisoners and executed the Archbishop of Canterbury.
Their demands included the abolition of
<a href="feudal_duties" class="wikilink" title="feudal duties">feudal
duties</a>, the introduction of
<a href="wage_labour" class="wikilink" title="wage labour">wage
labour</a>, and the expansion of liberty and social equality — though
the revolt was broken when the Lord Mayor of London killed Tyler, and
the king revoked his promises.[^3] Ball's egalitarian philosophy was
invoked again five centuries later by
<a href="William_Morris" class="wikilink" title="William Morris">William
Morris</a> in his 1888 novel
*<a href="A_Dream_of_John_Ball" class="wikilink"
title="A Dream of John Ball">A Dream of John Ball</a>*.[^4]

### The English Revolution

During the <a href="English_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="English Civil War">English Civil War</a>, radical republican and
democratic ideas began to circulate alongside older demands for liberty
and equality, producing what Marshall called recognizable anarchist
tendencies.[^5] The
<a href="Levellers" class="wikilink" title="Levellers">Levellers</a>
proposed a new constitution through their
*<a href="An_Agreement_of_the_People" class="wikilink"
title="Agreement of the People">Agreement of the People</a>*, advocating
progressive taxation, universal manhood suffrage and equality before the
law — positions that <a href="Oliver_Cromwell" class="wikilink"
title="Oliver Cromwell">Oliver Cromwell</a> condemned as a path to
"anarchy".[^6] The Levellers denied the charge, still holding to a form
of "good government", but their challenge to the Grandees of the
<a href="New_Model_Army" class="wikilink" title="New Model Army">New
Model Army</a> was ultimately defeated.[^7]

From the same environment of Christian mysticism, a variety of
anti-authoritarian religious currents emerged: the
<a href="Quakers" class="wikilink" title="Quakers">Quakers</a>,
<a href="Ranters" class="wikilink" title="Ranters">Ranters</a>,
<a href="Anabaptists" class="wikilink"
title="Anabaptists">Anabaptists</a>,
<a href="Diggers" class="wikilink" title="Diggers">Diggers</a> and
others separated themselves from the established church. The Ranters and
Diggers in particular have been described by historians as anarchist in
character, given their radical egalitarianism and communist
practices.[^8] The
<a href="Diggers" class="wikilink" title="Diggers">Diggers</a> attempted
to put their ideas into practice by occupying common land for collective
farming, until their settlements were suppressed by the authorities of
the <a href="Commonwealth_of_England" class="wikilink"
title="Commonwealth">Commonwealth</a>.[^9]

### The British Enlightenment

After the <a href="Glorious_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Glorious Revolution">Glorious Revolution</a> of 1688 and the
consolidation of constitutional monarchy,
<a href="Jonathan_Swift" class="wikilink"
title="Jonathan Swift">Jonathan Swift</a>'s
*<a href="Gulliver&#39;s_Travels" class="wikilink"
title="Gulliver&#39;s Travels">Gulliver's Travels</a>* (1726) offered an
early literary vision of a stateless society: the
<a href="Houyhnhnms" class="wikilink" title="Houyhnhnms">Houyhnhnms</a>,
an intelligent race of horses governing themselves through reason, with
only a representative body meeting every four years in an advisory
capacity and with no power to compel obedience. Swift's vision later
inspired
<a href="William_Godwin" class="wikilink" title="William Godwin">William
Godwin</a>, though
<a href="George_Orwell" class="wikilink" title="George Orwell">George
Orwell</a> criticized it as totalitarian in tendency and labelled Swift
a "Tory anarchist".[^10]

In 1756,
<a href="Edmund_Burke" class="wikilink" title="Edmund Burke">Edmund
Burke</a> published
*<a href="A_Vindication_of_Natural_Society" class="wikilink"
title="A Vindication of Natural Society">A Vindication of Natural
Society</a>*, in which he argued that human society had been governed by
reason until the invention of the state and the episcopacy — describing
the state as the root of all social conflict and war, and calling for a
return to "perfect liberty". Marshall described the text as "one of the
most powerful arguments for anarchist society made in the eighteenth
century."[^11] Burke later disowned the work as a satire, turned to
conservatism, but the *Vindication* went on to influence both Godwin's
anarchism and the libertarian socialism of
<a href="George_Holyoake" class="wikilink"
title="George Holyoake">George Holyoake</a>.[^12]

<a href="Thomas_Paine" class="wikilink" title="Thomas Paine">Thomas
Paine</a>'s
*<a href="Common_Sense" class="wikilink" title="Common Sense">Common
Sense</a>* (1776) and
*<a href="Rights_of_Man" class="wikilink" title="Rights of Man">Rights of
Man</a>* (1791) pushed Lockean liberalism toward libertarianism. In the
second part of *Rights of Man*, Paine came closest to anarchism in
arguing that social order would persist even if all government were
abolished: "society in every state is a blessing, but government even in
its best state is but a necessary evil." He concluded that civil society
"performs for itself almost everything which is ascribed to government",
and that individuals are chiefly regulated by common interest rather
than legal codes.[^13] Ultimately, though, his advocacy of
constitutionalism and limited government separated him from modern
anarchism.[^14]

It was during the Revolution Controversy sparked by Burke's
*<a href="Reflections_on_the_Revolution_in_France" class="wikilink"
title="Reflections on the Revolution in France">Reflections on the
Revolution in France</a>* that
<a href="William_Godwin" class="wikilink" title="William Godwin">William
Godwin</a> published
*<a href="Enquiry_Concerning_Political_Justice" class="wikilink"
title="Enquiry Concerning Political Justice">Enquiry Concerning
Political Justice</a>* (1793) — the first clear expression of
<a href="philosophical_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="philosophical anarchism">philosophical anarchism</a>, declaring
that all government ought to be abolished.[^15] Though expensive on
release — <a href="William_Pitt_the_Younger" class="wikilink"
title="Prime Minister Pitt">Prime Minister Pitt</a> decided against
banning it partly for that reason — workers pooled money to buy it by
subscription, pirated copies circulated in Ireland and Scotland, and
Godwin eventually reduced the price.[^16] When Pitt's government began
political persecutions of British radicals, Godwin was among those who
defended the accused, helping to secure their release.[^17]

Godwin's influence extended into the next generation: his son-in-law
<a href="Percy_Bysshe_Shelley" class="wikilink"
title="Percy Bysshe Shelley">Percy Bysshe Shelley</a> put much of his
philosophy into verse, while his disciple
<a href="Robert_Owen" class="wikilink" title="Robert Owen">Robert
Owen</a> became the founding figure of British socialism. After Godwin's
death, *Political Justice* continued to inspire the
<a href="Chartists" class="wikilink" title="Chartists">Chartists</a> and
<a href="Owenism" class="wikilink" title="Owenites">Owenites</a>, and
the <a href="Ricardian_socialism" class="wikilink"
title="Ricardian socialism">Ricardian socialism</a> of
<a href="Thomas_Hodgskin" class="wikilink"
title="Thomas Hodgskin">Thomas Hodgskin</a> and
<a href="William_Thompson_(philosopher)" class="wikilink"
title="William Thompson">William Thompson</a>.[^18]

### Nineteenth century

The labour movement began to take shape in Britain in the early
nineteenth century.
<a href="Robert_Owen" class="wikilink" title="Robert Owen">Robert
Owen</a>'s
<a href="Grand_National_Consolidated_Trades_Union" class="wikilink"
title="Grand National Consolidated Trades Union">Grand National
Consolidated Trades Union</a> contributed to the early development of
<a href="syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="syndicalism">syndicalism</a>, while the nonconformist preacher
<a href="William_Benbow" class="wikilink" title="William Benbow">William
Benbow</a> popularized the idea of the
<a href="general_strike" class="wikilink" title="general strike">general
strike</a> as a means of social revolution. The rise of
<a href="Chartism" class="wikilink" title="Chartism">Chartism</a>,
however, gave the British labour movement a largely reformist character
focused on parliamentary politics.[^19]

Classical anarchism arrived in Britain primarily through migrant workers
and asylum seekers in London in the wake of the
<a href="Revolutions_of_1848" class="wikilink"
title="Revolutions of 1848">Revolutions of 1848</a>. Isolated
individuals gathered in political clubs such as the
<a href="Rose_Street_Club" class="wikilink"
title="Rose Street Club">Rose Street Club</a> in Soho. The process
accelerated when
<a href="Johann_Most" class="wikilink" title="Johann Most">Johann
Most</a> moved to London and began printing
*<a href="Freiheit_(1879)" class="wikilink" title="Freiheit">Freiheit</a>*,
though it was soon suppressed after supporters signalled their approval
of the <a href="Phoenix_Park_Murders" class="wikilink"
title="Phoenix Park Murders">Phoenix Park Murders</a> and Most moved
operations to the United States.[^20]

By 1881 the movement of British revolutionary socialists toward
anarchism produced the
<a href="Labour_Emancipation_League" class="wikilink"
title="Labour Emancipation League">Labour Emancipation League</a> (LEL),
which declared itself against all forms of government and won support
among workers in London's East End before merging into the
<a href="Social_Democratic_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Social Democratic Federation">Social Democratic Federation</a>
(SDF). The authoritarianism of the SDF's leader
<a href="Henry_Hyndman" class="wikilink" title="Henry Hyndman">Henry
Hyndman</a> caused a split, and a number of libertarian socialists
around
<a href="William_Morris" class="wikilink" title="William Morris">William
Morris</a> formed the
<a href="Socialist_League_(UK,_1885)" class="wikilink"
title="Socialist League">Socialist League</a>. Morris himself left after
an anarchist faction took control in 1887, and the League's publications
were radicalized under
<a href="H._B._Samuels" class="wikilink" title="H. B. Samuels">H. B.
Samuels</a>.[^21]

Other tendencies developed alongside:
<a href="individualist_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="individualist anarchism">individualist anarchism</a> through
<a href="Henry_Seymour_(secularist)" class="wikilink"
title="Henry Seymour">Henry Seymour</a>'s journal
*<a href="The_Anarchist_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="The Anarchist">The Anarchist</a>*;
<a href="anarcho-communism" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-communism">anarcho-communism</a> through
<a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Kropotkin">Peter
Kropotkin</a>'s paper
*<a href="Freedom_(British_newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom">Freedom</a>*; and
<a href="Jewish_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="Jewish anarchism">Jewish anarchism</a> through the Yiddish
journal
*<a href="Arbeter_Fraynd" class="wikilink" title="Arbeter Fraynd">Arbeter
Fraynd</a>*. Anarchist ideas also worked their way into literature
through William Morris's *<a href="News_from_Nowhere" class="wikilink"
title="News from Nowhere">News from Nowhere</a>*,
<a href="Oscar_Wilde" class="wikilink" title="Oscar Wilde">Oscar
Wilde</a>'s *<a href="The_Soul_of_Man_Under_Socialism" class="wikilink"
title="The Soul of Man Under Socialism">The Soul of Man Under
Socialism</a>*, and the writing of
<a href="George_Bernard_Shaw" class="wikilink"
title="George Bernard Shaw">George Bernard Shaw</a>,
<a href="Edward_Carpenter" class="wikilink"
title="Edward Carpenter">Edward Carpenter</a> and
<a href="Henry_Stephens_Salt" class="wikilink"
title="Henry Stephens Salt">Henry Stephens Salt</a>.[^22]

### Syndicalism and the early twentieth century

In the 1910s,
<a href="Tom_Mann" class="wikilink" title="Tom Mann">Tom Mann</a>'s
<a href="Industrial_Syndicalist_Education_League" class="wikilink"
title="Industrial Syndicalist Education League">Industrial Syndicalist
Education League</a> pushed for
<a href="industrial_unions" class="wikilink"
title="industrial unions">industrial unions</a> and workers' control
through direct class conflict.
<a href="Anarcho-syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="Anarcho-syndicalism">Anarcho-syndicalism</a>'s influence waned
after the First World War, which split the anarchist movement, and by
the mid-1920s the British movement had largely dissolved, with only
scattered groups remaining in the cities.[^23]

The <a href="Spanish_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a> brought a revival.
British anarchists moved toward anarcho-syndicalism and formed
syndicalist organizations in solidarity with the
<a href="National_Confederation_of_Labour" class="wikilink"
title="CNT">CNT</a> in Spain.[^24] After the defeat of the Spanish
anarchists, the movement regrouped in the
<a href="Anarchist_Federation_of_Britain" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist Federation of Britain">Anarchist Federation of
Britain</a> (AFB), which encompassed several different tendencies.[^25]

### Post-war

When <a href="Vernon_Richards" class="wikilink"
title="Vernon Richards">Vernon Richards</a> and three editors were
arrested in early 1945 for attempting to undermine the loyalty of
soldiers, a <a href="Freedom_Defence_Committee" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom Defence Committee">Freedom Defence Committee</a> was
established by <a href="Benjamin_Britten" class="wikilink"
title="Benjamin Britten">Benjamin Britten</a>,
<a href="E._M._Forster" class="wikilink" title="E. M. Forster">E. M.
Forster</a>,
<a href="Augustus_John" class="wikilink" title="Augustus John">Augustus
John</a>,
<a href="George_Orwell" class="wikilink" title="George Orwell">George
Orwell</a>,
<a href="Herbert_Read" class="wikilink" title="Herbert Read">Herbert
Read</a>,
<a href="Osbert_Sitwell" class="wikilink" title="Osbert Sitwell">Osbert
Sitwell</a> and <a href="George_Woodcock" class="wikilink"
title="George Woodcock">George Woodcock</a> "to uphold the essential
liberty of individuals and organizations, and to defend those who are
persecuted for exercising their rights to freedom of speech, writing and
action."[^26]

The <a href="Syndicalist_Workers&#39;_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Syndicalist Workers&#39; Federation">Syndicalist Workers'
Federation</a> (SWF) was formed in 1950 from members of the dissolved
AFB, adopting a more explicitly syndicalist and worker-centred strategy.
It affiliated to the
<a href="International_Workers&#39;_Association" class="wikilink"
title="International Workers&#39; Association">International Workers'
Association</a> and gave particular support to the underground CNT
during the Franco years. When its most active member
<a href="Tom_Brown_(anarchist)" class="wikilink" title="Tom Brown">Tom
Brown</a> was forced out of activity, the SWF declined to a single
branch in Manchester by 1979, before dissolving into what became the
Direct Action Movement.[^27]

<a href="Colin_Ward" class="wikilink" title="Colin Ward">Colin Ward</a>
edited *Freedom* from 1947 to 1960 and founded the monthly
*<a href="Anarchy_Magazine" class="wikilink" title="Anarchy">Anarchy</a>*
in 1961, which ran for 118 issues until 1970. The anarcho-pacifist
writer <a href="Alex_Comfort" class="wikilink" title="Alex Comfort">Alex
Comfort</a>, an active member of the
<a href="Peace_Pledge_Union" class="wikilink"
title="Peace Pledge Union">Peace Pledge Union</a> and the Campaign for
Nuclear Disarmament, described pacifism as resting "solely upon the
historical theory of anarchism", and was imprisoned for a month
alongside <a href="Bertrand_Russell" class="wikilink"
title="Bertrand Russell">Bertrand Russell</a> for refusing to be bound
over before a mass protest in Trafalgar Square in September 1961.[^28]

<a href="Stuart_Christie" class="wikilink"
title="Stuart Christie">Stuart Christie</a> left London in July 1964 on
a mission to assassinate <a href="Francisco_Franco" class="wikilink"
title="Francisco Franco">Francisco Franco</a>, was captured and
imprisoned in Spain. After his release he continued his activism in
Britain: with
<a href="Albert_Meltzer" class="wikilink" title="Albert Meltzer">Albert
Meltzer</a> he re-formed the
<a href="Anarchist_Black_Cross" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist Black Cross">Anarchist Black Cross</a>, founded
*<a href="Black_Flag_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Black Flag">Black Flag</a>*, was acquitted of involvement with
the <a href="Angry_Brigade" class="wikilink" title="Angry Brigade">Angry
Brigade</a>, and established the Cienfuegos Press publishing house. He
and Meltzer co-authored *The Floodgates of Anarchy*.

### Squatting since the 1960s

In November 1968, anarchists together with homeless families launched
the London Squatters Campaign, with their first major action being the
occupation of seventeen houses in the east London borough of Redbridge.
After facing down private bailiffs, and with media coverage playing a
significant role, the campaigners persuaded the council to make empty
properties available under licence.[^29] Colin Ward, by then Education
Officer for the Town and Country Planning Association, provided
philosophical and political grounding for the practice in *Housing: an
anarchist approach* (1976).[^30]

By the end of the 1970s there were estimates of 50,000 squatters in
England and Wales, with 30,000 in London.[^31] The tabloid press raised
the alarm, though Metropolitan Police Special Branch, which had
infiltrated the squatting scene, concluded that squatting "did not exist
as a movement" — while identifying anarchists as the largest political
tendency within it.[^32]

Anarchists have remained active in squatting, responding to progressive
criminalisation by occupying non-residential properties — empty shops,
pubs, offices, warehouses, churches — as social centres and homeless
shelters. In London the criminal trespass law has been openly defied: a
group calling themselves the Autonomous Nation of Anarchist Libertarians
occupied a series of luxury mansions to draw attention to the housing
crisis, and by March 2015, when they occupied the former
<a href="Institute_of_Directors" class="wikilink"
title="Institute of Directors">Institute of Directors</a> building on
<a href="Pall_Mall,_London" class="wikilink" title="Pall Mall">Pall
Mall</a>, claimed it was the seventeenth building in the area they had
occupied.[^33]

### Anarcho-punk

A wave of popular interest in anarchism followed the emergence of
<a href="punk_rock" class="wikilink" title="punk rock">punk rock</a> in
the mid-1970s, shaped in part by the Situationist-influenced work of
<a href="Malcolm_McLaren" class="wikilink"
title="Malcolm McLaren">Malcolm McLaren</a> and graphic artist
<a href="Jamie_Reid" class="wikilink" title="Jamie Reid">Jamie Reid</a>
around the
<a href="Sex_Pistols" class="wikilink" title="Sex Pistols">Sex
Pistols</a> and their single
"<a href="Anarchy_in_the_U.K." class="wikilink"
title="Anarchy in the U.K.">Anarchy in the U.K.</a>".[^34]
<a href="Crass" class="wikilink" title="Crass">Crass</a> and the
<a href="Poison_Girls" class="wikilink" title="Poison Girls">Poison
Girls</a> funded the <a href="Wapping_Autonomy_Centre" class="wikilink"
title="Wapping Autonomy Centre">Wapping Autonomy Centre</a>, which
inspired a network of squatted self-managed social centres across London
including the Ambulance Station on Old Kent Road, Centro Iberico, and
others. The <a href="anarcho-punk" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-punk">anarcho-punk</a> aesthetic was taken up by bands
including <a href="Flux_of_Pink_Indians" class="wikilink"
title="Flux of Pink Indians">Flux of Pink Indians</a>, the
<a href="Subhumans_(UK_band)" class="wikilink"
title="Subhumans">Subhumans</a> and
<a href="Conflict_(band)" class="wikilink" title="Conflict">Conflict</a>.[^35]
By the early 1980s, an anarcho-punk scene had emerged in Leeds around
groups including <a href="Abrasive_Wheels" class="wikilink"
title="Abrasive Wheels">Abrasive Wheels</a>,
<a href="the_Expelled" class="wikilink" title="the Expelled">the
Expelled</a> and
<a href="Icon_A.D." class="wikilink" title="Icon A.D.">Icon A.D.</a>,
from which <a href="Chumbawamba" class="wikilink"
title="Chumbawamba">Chumbawamba</a> developed their confrontational
political activism.

In 1983, the anarchist
<a href="Ian_Bone" class="wikilink" title="Ian Bone">Ian Bone</a> moved
to London and proposed an anarchist tabloid newspaper aimed at a mass
working-class readership. The resulting
*<a href="Class_War" class="wikilink" title="Class War">Class War</a>*
paper attracted immediate attention for its celebration of workers
assaulting police officers, its open glorification of workers'
solidarity, and its contempt for Thatcherism.[^36] Class War broke from
anarchism's previous association with lifestylism and the
<a href="Campaign_for_Nuclear_Disarmament" class="wikilink"
title="Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament">Campaign for Nuclear
Disarmament</a>, and deliberately targeted the middle-class character of
pacifist anarchism.[^37]
<a href="Albert_Meltzer" class="wikilink" title="Albert Meltzer">Albert
Meltzer</a> recalled that older anarchists initially took it for a
parody; correspondents of *Freedom* denounced it as nihilist and
Marxist.[^38]

### Anti-war and anti-globalisation

The anthropologist and activist
<a href="David_Graeber" class="wikilink" title="David Graeber">David
Graeber</a> argued that many direct action protests are implicitly
anarchist — that the very logic of prefigurative action, rejecting
appeals to governments in favour of physical intervention that itself
models an alternative, "emerges directly from the libertarian
tradition."[^39] On this basis he identified substantial anarchist
involvement in opposition to both Gulf Wars and in the broader
<a href="anti-globalisation_movement" class="wikilink"
title="anti-globalisation movement">anti-globalisation movement</a>. The
<a href="Occupy_movement" class="wikilink"
title="Occupy movement">Occupy movement</a>, with its decentralised,
leaderless structure and emphasis on consensus decision-making, drew
directly on anarchist organizational practices.[^40]

In March 2009, anarchist groups participated in the "Put People First"
march through central London against G20 policies. A "G20 Meltdown"
group declared 1 April "Financial Fools Day" and called for a carnival
outside the
<a href="Bank_of_England" class="wikilink" title="Bank of England">Bank
of England</a>; a small number of activists broke away to ransack an
office of the <a href="Royal_Bank_of_Scotland" class="wikilink"
title="Royal Bank of Scotland">Royal Bank of Scotland</a>. In March
2011, anarchists intervened in a half-million strong TUC anti-austerity
demonstration, attacking the Ritz Hotel, smashing bank windows and
fighting police — justifying the action by pointing to the failure of
the even larger anti-war march of 2003 to prevent the invasion of Iraq,
and arguing that political change was not "predicated on being right and
winning a debate."[^41]
