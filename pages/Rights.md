# Rights

From an anarchist perspective, the concept of legal rights — dependent
on civil law — represents one of the more dangerous myths perpetuated by
state power. While commonly understood as protections for individual
freedom, anarchists argue that governments granting rights is actually a
mechanism for authority to control people while making them think they
are being protected.

## The Myth of Human Rights

### Natural vs Legal Rights

Rights are typically divided into two categories. Legal rights (also
called civil rights or statutory rights) are privileges granted by the
state to certain segments of the population; they depend entirely on the
rule of law within a nation and can be revoked at any time by those in
power. Natural rights (also called human rights, moral rights, or
inalienable rights) are claimed to be fundamental to human existence,
supposedly granted by nature or divine authority, and theoretically
universal and eternal.

Both categories, through anarchist analysis, are constructs serving
state power rather than genuine protections for human freedom.

Some anarchists and radicals have attempted to reclaim the language of
'rights' to describe basic human needs, dignity, and freedoms — what
people actually require to live full lives, such as food, shelter,
autonomy, community, and freedom from domination. Most anarchist
thinkers argue, however, that using rights language, even for these
genuine human needs, ultimately legitimises the authority structures
that grant or deny such rights in the first place. They advocate instead
for direct fulfilment of human needs through mutual aid and voluntary
cooperation, without appealing to any authority for permission.

### Rights as State Privileges

As <a href="George_Carlin" class="wikilink" title="George Carlin">George
Carlin</a> observed, rights function as 'a bill of temporary privileges'
that can be withdrawn whenever convenient for those in power. The
<a href="Japanese_American_internment" class="wikilink"
title="Japanese American internment">Japanese American internment</a>
during World War II illustrates the point: 110,000 law-abiding citizens
were stripped of all their supposed constitutional rights and imprisoned
simply because their ancestry was deemed threatening. Their only
remaining 'right' was 'right this way' into the camps.[^1]

<a href="Voltairine_de_Cleyre" class="wikilink"
title="Voltairine de Cleyre">Voltairine de Cleyre</a> articulated the
distinction directly: 'Note the difference between a right and a
privilege. A right, in the abstract, is a fact; it is not a thing to be
given, established, or conferred; it is. Of the exercise of a right
power may deprive me; of the right itself, never. Privilege, in the
abstract, does not exist; there is no such thing. Rights actually
recognized, privilege is destroyed. But, in the practical, the moment
you admit a supreme authority, you have denied rights. Practically the
supremacy has all the rights, and no matter what the human race
possesses, it does so merely at the caprice of that authority.'[^2]

## The Function of Rights Discourse

### Legitimising Authority

Rights discourse serves state power in several interlocking ways. By
first stripping away freedoms and then parcelling back select
privileges, the state creates dependency and gratitude for what should
be natural. Rights create hierarchies between those who have them and
those who don't — citizens versus migrants, law-abiding versus criminal
— dividing populations that might otherwise act collectively. Social
movements get drawn into demanding rights from authority rather than
rejecting authority entirely. And the illusion of protection obscures
the reality that the state maintains power through violence.

### State Overcoding

Drawing on
<a href="Gilles_Deleuze" class="wikilink" title="Gilles Deleuze">Gilles
Deleuze</a> and
<a href="Félix_Guattari" class="wikilink" title="Félix Guattari">Félix
Guattari</a>, anarchist theorist
<a href="Aragorn_Eloff" class="wikilink" title="Aragorn Eloff">Aragorn
Eloff</a> argues that rights operate as part of 'State overcoding' — 'a
reification of these arrangements of coded institutions and practices,
as well as various material flows, that allows them to resonate
together'. Rights discourse creates 'a rigid segmentarity' that divides
human experience into legally manageable categories while establishing
the state as the sole legitimate arbiter of what people deserve.[^3]

## The Impossibility of Universal Rights

### Cultural and Historical Relativity

<a href="Bob_Black" class="wikilink" title="Bob Black">Bob Black</a>
argues that 'belief in human rights is far from universal' and that
moral systems vary dramatically across cultures and historical periods.
What one society considers a fundamental right, another may view as
abhorrent. The supposedly universal 'right to life' coexists with
capital punishment, war, and systematic state violence in virtually
every society that proclaims it.[^4] As
<a href="Blaise_Pascal" class="wikilink" title="Blaise Pascal">Blaise
Pascal</a> observed in the 17th century: 'Larceny, incest, parricide,
everything has at some time been accounted a virtuous action.'[^5]

### The Absence of Objective Foundation

Natural rights proponents variously ground their claims in human nature
(which varies and cannot be objectively defined), divine command (which
requires belief in deities and faces the
<a href="Euthyphro_dilemma" class="wikilink"
title="Euthyphro dilemma">Euthyphro dilemma</a>), self-evidence (which
is not self-evident to everyone), or human dignity (which is itself
culturally relative). As
<a href="J.L._Mackie" class="wikilink" title="J.L. Mackie">J.L.
Mackie</a> argued: 'If there were objective values, then they would be
entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly
different from anything else in the universe.'[^6]

## Rights vs. Freedom

### The Authoritarian Nature of Rights

Rights discourse carries an authoritarian logic that is rarely
acknowledged. Someone must have the power to grant, interpret, and
enforce rights; every right in one person creates obligations in others;
enforcement requires expanding state power and legitimacy; and the
adversarial framing produces win/lose conflicts where cooperative
solutions might otherwise be possible. As
<a href="Mary_Ann_Glendon" class="wikilink"
title="Mary Ann Glendon">Mary Ann Glendon</a> noted: 'Our rights talk,
in its absoluteness, promotes unrealistic expectations, heightens social
conflict, and inhibits social dialogue that might lead toward consensus,
accommodation, or at least the discovery of common ground.'[^7]

### Rights vs. Desires and Needs

Rather than demanding rights from authority, anarchists advocate for
directly fulfilling human desires and needs through mutual aid and
voluntary cooperation. As Bob Black puts it: 'Don't claim that your
rights be respected. Demand that your interests be served. Better yet,
if you can, serve them yourself.'[^8]

## Anarchist Alternatives

### Beyond Rights Discourse

<a href="Proudhon" class="wikilink" title="Proudhon">Proudhon</a>
defined right as 'the recognition of human dignity in all its faculties,
attributes and prerogatives' — but this recognition, in his account,
emerges from social relationships rather than state authority.[^9]

Anarchists still recognise that people have genuine needs, deserve
dignity, and should be free from domination. The difference is in how
these are pursued. Rather than framing them as rights to be granted,
anarchists speak of human needs (food, shelter, healthcare, community)
to be met through cooperation; personal autonomy over one's own life
without interference from rulers; dignity as inherent worth emerging
from mutual recognition rather than legal declaration; freedom as the
absence of domination achieved through dismantling hierarchies; and
liberties as the actual capacity to exercise freedoms without
repression. The aim is to fulfil these directly through voluntary
cooperation — mutual aid, direct action, free association, autonomous
communities handling conflicts restoratively — rather than petitioning
authority for permission.

### Freedom Through Anarchy

As <a href="Pëtr_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Pëtr Kropotkin">Pëtr
Kropotkin</a> observed: 'This is what these so-called liberties can be
reduced to. Freedom of press and of meeting, inviolability of home and
all the rest, are only respected if the people do not make use of them
against the privileged classes. But the day the people begin to take
advantage of them to undermine those privileges, the so-called liberties
will be cast overboard.'[^10]
