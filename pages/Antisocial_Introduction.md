# Anarchist Approaches to Crime and Harm

Anarchists reject the state's definition of 'crime' as violations
against state authority, focusing instead on preventing and addressing
**social harm** — actions that genuinely hurt individuals or
communities. Rather than relying on police, courts, and prisons,
anarchist communities have developed alternative approaches grounded in
community accountability, restoration, and collective self-defence.

## Core Principles

Anarchist approaches to harmful behaviour share a common orientation:
address root causes rather than simply respond to effects, and centre
those most affected in deciding how harm is addressed. Restoration
matters more than retribution — the aim is to heal harm, not inflict
punishment in kind. Harmful behaviour is understood as emerging from
social conditions that communities share responsibility for changing,
which means responses are collective rather than individualised. Where
someone has been harmed, their autonomy over what justice looks like for
them is treated as non-negotiable.

## Handling Murder

For cases of murder, anarchist communities would likely employ several
approaches in combination.

### Immediate Response

- The person who caused harm would be isolated from the community to
  prevent further harm while next steps are determined.
- Care and resources would be provided to families and communities
  affected by the loss.
- Community members would investigate circumstances and context, rather
  than deferring to state authorities.

### Long-term Approaches

- Where appropriate, restorative processes would facilitate dialogue
  between the person who caused harm and affected communities,
  establishing accountability measures.
- Therapeutic intervention would address underlying causes such as
  mental health issues, trauma, or substance dependency.
- For cases involving persistent danger or psychopathy, permanent
  exclusion from the community may be judged necessary.
- Some propose supervised communities for those too dangerous to
  integrate into general society but who should not be subjected to
  prison conditions.

The appropriate response depends heavily on circumstances: whether the
killing was impulsive or premeditated, involved self-defence, resulted
from mental illness, and so on.

## Handling Sexual Assault

Sexual assault has received significant attention in anarchist
communities, leading to the development of several accountability
models.

### Transformative Justice Approaches

Accountability processes typically involve separate support teams for
survivors and for those who caused harm, structured facilitated
dialogue, and community agreements that establish ongoing monitoring to
ensure accountability measures are followed.

### Survivor-Led Responses

Some survivors and communities have chosen more direct approaches:
public exposure to warn others about dangerous individuals, banishment
from community spaces and events, physical confrontation, or collective
social pressure.

### Prevention Strategies

Preventive work tends to focus on community-wide education about consent
and healthy relationships, men's groups addressing patriarchal
attitudes, and broader cultural challenges to rape culture within
radical spaces.

## Handling Non-Cooperative Individuals

When someone refuses to participate in restorative processes and
continues to pose a danger, communities face difficult decisions.

### Escalating Responses

Responses typically escalate from collective social pressure and
exclusion from community resources and events, through geographic
exclusion (asking the person to leave the area), to physical
intervention where ongoing harm requires it.

### Network Coordination

Where dangerous individuals move between areas, communities share
information through mutual aid networks and coordinate regional
responses to address repeat offenders collectively.

### Final Options

For those who refuse all accountability and remain an ongoing danger,
options discussed include permanent isolation from anarchist
communities, placement in supervised environments, and — as a last
resort — the use of force to protect against persistent threats.

## Current Practice

In practice, contemporary anarchist communities use a mixture of
approaches depending on circumstances, available resources, and the
specific nature of harm involved. Most practitioners treat these as
experimental models requiring ongoing development and critical
evaluation rather than settled doctrine.

The aim is not a perfect system but approaches that are more effective
at reducing harm than existing institutions, while remaining consistent
with anarchist principles of mutual aid, collective self-determination,
and opposition to authoritarian structures.
