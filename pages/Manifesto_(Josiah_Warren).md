## Overview

Published in 1841, the **Manifesto** is
<a href="Josiah_Warren" class="wikilink" title="Josiah Warren">Josiah
Warren</a>'s first systematic public statement of the principles he
called <a href="equitable_commerce" class="wikilink"
title="equitable commerce">equitable commerce</a> — a framework for
economic life grounded in individual sovereignty and the rejection of
combination in any form. Warren had spent the preceding fourteen years
testing these ideas in practice, first at the
<a href="Cincinnati_Time_Store" class="wikilink"
title="Cincinnati Time Store">Cincinnati Time Store</a> (opened 1827)
and then through a series of intentional communities, before setting
them down as a formal declaration.

## Summary

The *Manifesto* opens by correcting a widespread misunderstanding:
Warren was not, he insisted, proposing to form societies. The formation
of societies was precisely the error he sought to move beyond. All
combinations, however ingeniously designed or honestly intended, require
individuals to surrender their natural sovereignty over their own
person, time, property, and responsibilities to the governing logic of
the group. The result is that people are reduced to components of a
machine, entangled in responsibilities for the acts of others and unable
to act freely on their own judgment. Warren drew directly on his
experience of
<a href="Robert_Owen" class="wikilink" title="Robert Owen">Robert
Owen</a>'s
<a href="New_Harmony,_Indiana" class="wikilink" title="New Harmony">New
Harmony</a> experiment (1825–1827) as proof: two years of trial had
demonstrated to his satisfaction that combination could not achieve the
objects reformers set for it.[^1]

The alternative Warren proposed was the principle of **Individuality** —
or, as he also called it, DISCONNECTION. Where combination dissolved
individual sovereignty into collective governance, Individuality left
each person in undisturbed possession of their own sovereignty. No one
joining a community on these principles would be required to surrender
any portion of their natural liberty, become responsible for the acts or
opinions of anyone else, or submit to any form of collective authority
over their person, time, or property.[^2]

The practical expression of this principle was
<a href="equitable_commerce" class="wikilink"
title="equitable commerce">equitable commerce</a>: a system of exchange
in which labor is traded for labor, and the cost of production — not its
supposed value to the buyer — sets the limit of price. Warren had
developed this through the Time Store, where goods were sold at cost
price plus a small percentage for expenses, and the store owner's time
in serving the customer was repaid not in money but in a
<a href="labor_note" class="wikilink" title="labor note">labor note</a>
— a written promise to return an equivalent amount of the customer's own
labor. The circulating medium was thus disconnected from money and
grounded entirely in labor time.[^3]

Warren argued that this system would concentrate wealth and power in the
hands of those who actually worked, since the labor note could only be
issued by laborers. It would also tend to make money progressively less
useful and eventually powerless — sweeping away, in his words, "all the
crushing masses of fraud, iniquity, cruelty, corruption and imposition
that are built upon it." Machinery, rather than displacing workers,
would under equitable commerce work on their behalf: as it reduced the
labor required to produce goods, it would reduce their cost, benefiting
consumers rather than enriching owners.[^4]

The *Manifesto* closes with a characteristic reassurance: none of this
required joining anything. The experiments Warren described had involved
hundreds of people, none of whom were organised into a sect, party, or
society. Each participant remained entirely anonymous as such.
Reformation, Warren argued, need not wait for the world to become
learned — the practical operation of equitable commerce was itself a
process of re-education that no one could estimate without experience.

## Legacy

The *Manifesto* established the theoretical foundations that Warren
developed at greater length in
*<a href="Equitable_Commerce" class="wikilink"
title="Equitable Commerce">Equitable Commerce</a>* (1846) and
*<a href="True_Civilization" class="wikilink"
title="True Civilization">True Civilization</a>* (1863).
<a href="Benjamin_Tucker" class="wikilink"
title="Benjamin Tucker">Benjamin Tucker</a>, who credited Warren as "the
first man to expound and formulate the doctrine now known as Anarchism,"
dedicated his collected essays to Warren's memory as "my friend and
master ... whose teachings were my first source of light."[^5] The
individualist current Warren helped originate also reached Europe, where
<a href="Émile_Armand" class="wikilink" title="Émile Armand">Émile
Armand</a> and others drew on his communal experiments in developing
their own practice of intentional community.[^6]
