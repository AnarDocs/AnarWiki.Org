# El Alto Neighbourhood Councils (FEJUVE)

## History

### Origins

Over a million people[^1] live in the Bolivian city of
<a href="El_Alto" class="wikilink" title="El Alto">El Alto</a>, mostly
indigenous Aymarans whose families migrated from the countryside over
the preceding decades. The city's growth was driven in large part by
economic displacement: following the
<a href="Bolivian_National_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Bolivian National Revolution">Bolivian National Revolution</a> of
1952, indigenous communities found themselves unable to compete with
cheap imported food after successive governments implemented neoliberal
restructuring programmes. The closure of mines and the expulsion of
peasants from the Altiplano sent thousands to settle in what had been a
small town on the edge of
<a href="La_Paz" class="wikilink" title="La Paz">La Paz</a>.[^2]

The Bolivian state had little presence in El Alto, leaving residents
without paved roads, waste management, running water, electricity or
healthcare. Three quarters of the city's population had no access to
healthcare and nearly half were not literate. Around 70% of residents
earned their living from small family businesses, forming a city-wide
informal economy that only expanded under neoliberalism.[^3]

In response, residents began forming self-organised neighbourhood
councils (*juntas*) during the 1950s, drawing on the traditional
practices of the
<a href="Aymara_people" class="wikilink" title="Aymara people">Aymara
people</a>. The first council was established in 1957 to provide basic
services where the state offered none. By 1979, these councils had
federated into the Federation of Neighbourhood Councils, known as FEJUVE
(*Federación de Juntas Vecinales de El Alto*).[^4] FEJUVE's first major
public demonstrations came in 1987, demanding paved roads, electricity,
drinking water, a hospital, and a public university.[^5]

In its early years, the FEJUVE's leadership was often elitist, keeping
decision-making power within itself and only occasionally sharing it
with assemblies. Political party activists sought to capture the
organisation for electoral ends, and the FEJUVE was eventually coopted
by the left-wing party CONDEPA, losing much of its autonomy in the
process. By the early 1990s this had produced a damaging factional
split, with the FEJUVE dividing into two rival federations aligned with
different political tendencies.[^6]

The grassroots response was not to abandon the federation but to
disperse across both factions and work to reclaim it from within.
Activists also established the El Alto Assembly, which returned control
to local councils and trade unions, overseeing strike actions and
infrastructure construction as a grassroots democratic alternative to
party domination.[^7] By the end of the 1990s, the local councils had
reasserted control. Leadership figures faced growing scrutiny for
corruption: on one occasion, after FEJUVE president Mauricio Cori was
accused of negotiating personal positions within municipal government,
he was publicly beaten and stripped of his clothes by outraged
residents.[^8] Positions in the organisation became increasingly
difficult to hold without popular consent.

The experience of the miners' unions — accustomed to organising outside
state and party control — provided further impetus towards
democratisation. The influence of party politicians was structurally
curtailed, and political party leaders, merchants, real estate
speculators, and those who had collaborated with the dictatorship were
prohibited from leadership or delegate positions.[^9][^10]

In 1994, the government passed the Law of Popular Participation,
devolving a fifth of the national budget to local municipalities and
granting communities a participatory role in budgeting and planning.
Though it imposed new legal requirements on FEJUVE, it also
significantly expanded the councils' influence — they began to function
as *de facto* local governments, increasingly subverting the formal
authority of the state.[^11]

### The Gas War and Insurrection

The infrastructure the FEJUVE had spent decades building became the
foundation for collective resistance during the
<a href="Bolivian_gas_conflict" class="wikilink"
title="Bolivian gas conflict">Bolivian gas conflict</a> of 2003, when
people across Bolivia rose up against plans to export the country's gas
cheaply to the United States. In October 2003, residents of El Alto
organised through neighbourhood councils and operated as a neighbourhood
government that supplanted the delegitimised state. Accounts of the
uprising consistently describe the absence of any single organisation or
leadership: actions were carried out directly by the residents of the
neighbourhoods, overriding all other institutions — including those the
residents themselves had created.[^12]

The local culture of collective identity, bound to both the
neighbourhood councils and residents' original communities in the
Altiplano, linked urban and rural struggles. Despite peasant roadblocks
causing food shortages and inflation in El Alto, urban residents
maintained solidarity with the peasantry. The conflict fused indigenous
and trade union organising traditions, with elements of both converging
into collective resistance.[^13] A general strike shut down El Alto,
with thousands taking to the streets. The sense of collective
responsibility inherited from the traditional
*<a href="Ayllu" class="wikilink" title="Ayllu">Ayllu</a>* produced high
levels of participation, sustained through a system of shared benefits
and symbolic penalties.[^14]

Horizontally-organised assemblies made decisions by consensus. The
neighbourhood councils organised roadblocks, social kitchens and
community self-defence, rotating shifts to keep the protest continuous.
They worked alongside trade unions to maintain access to markets and
roads. The FEJUVE itself did not lead: guided by the indigenous
principle of *mandar obedeciendo* — leading by obeying — it followed the
movement rather than directing it. One FEJUVE leader described it
plainly: "We were no longer an executive, the people didn't take any
notice \[of us\]. The people rose up \[...\] They triumphed, not us
\[the leaders\]. We simply obeyed orders."[^15]

The neighbourhood councils seized control of resources, territory and
infrastructure, achieving a high degree of autonomy from the state.
FEJUVE organised a blockade of La Paz, effectively placing the capital
under siege and shutting down its airport. Uruguayan political theorist
<a href="Raúl_Zibechi" class="wikilink" title="Raúl Zibechi">Raúl
Zibechi</a> and sociologist
<a href="Álvaro_García_Linera" class="wikilink"
title="Álvaro García Linera">Álvaro García Linera</a> both considered
that El Alto's "community of neighbours" was consolidated as a political
force during this uprising.[^16] The FEJUVE nevertheless refused to
seize state power, instead laying the groundwork for the broader
political shift that brought
<a href="Evo_Morales" class="wikilink" title="Evo Morales">Evo
Morales</a> and the Movement for Socialism (MAS) to power in 2005.[^17]

### The Morales Period

Morales came to power on the basis of the "October Agenda" proposed by
social movements including FEJUVE: the return of popular sovereignty
over natural resources and the reconstitution of the Bolivian state
along plurinational lines. The efforts to implement this were frustrated
by Bolivia's integration into the globalised economy and by decades of
embedded neoliberal structures. The nationalisation of oil supplies was
met with cutbacks in private investment; agrarian reform was limited to
already publicly-held lands; and the new plurinational constitution was
stonewalled by right-wing regional elites.[^18]

FEJUVE found itself caught between its own autonomy and the new
government, as a number of its leaders joined the administration. Abel
Mamani, who had led the FEJUVE's campaign against water privatisation,
was appointed Minister of Public Works — and when water provision in El
Alto degraded under his oversight, he was forced to resign.[^19] Former
FEJUVE leader Julieta Mabel Monje drew particular criticism as Minister
of Environment and Water, due to her mismanagement of the return of El
Alto's water supply from private to public hands. FEJUVE had proposed
that the new public water company be managed through participatory
assemblies; the government rejected this.[^20]

The government also rejected FEJUVE's proposal to elect the Constituent
Assembly on a non-partisan basis, with delegates drawn from ethnic,
trade union and neighbourhood lists rather than from political parties.
The MAS progressively demobilised the popular movements that had brought
it to power. Despite this, when right-wing paramilitary violence erupted
in 2008, neighbourhood councils in
<a href="Santa_Cruz_de_la_Sierra" class="wikilink"
title="Santa Cruz de la Sierra">Santa Cruz de la Sierra</a> formed
spontaneously in self-defence, directly inspired by the FEJUVE model. A
coalition of social movements placed Santa Cruz under siege, and FEJUVE
joined Morales in a march on the capital demanding a constitutional
referendum.[^21]

The new constitution, ratified after concessions to the opposition,
stripped out its most directly democratic and anti-capitalist provisions
while still establishing state ownership of natural resources and
expanded indigenous rights. FEJUVE shifted from confrontation to what it
described as "constructive but critical collaboration" with MAS, working
alongside the Bolivian Workers' Centre (COB) to push for public housing,
participatory planning, and workers' control in El Alto's economy.[^22]

### Current Status

By 2010, El Alto faced serious water scarcity as climate change caused
Bolivia's glaciers to melt. FEJUVE, having previously supported a
state-owned water company, began calling instead for an autonomous
municipal water company for El Alto — reflecting its continued suspicion
of centralised control from La Paz.[^23]

In 2011, FEJUVE called a general strike demanding increased state
funding for a city that had seen a 50% population increase over the
previous decade. Roads into La Paz were blockaded for two days before
Morales negotiated a settlement — though two of FEJUVE's fourteen
district councils refused to accept the terms and briefly continued
their blockades independently.[^24]

On the tenth anniversary of the gas conflict in 2013, FEJUVE reported
that conditions had not improved substantially since Morales's election:
60% of the city still lacked a proper sewage system and 10% had no
running water. The Federation called a 24-hour general strike and a
boycott of government celebrations, demanding infrastructure funding and
a share of petroleum revenues. Morales responded by announcing a dam and
committing \$1 billion to infrastructure improvements.[^25]

Autonomous organising had significantly declined by 2016. Researcher
Angus McNelly attributes this to the government's combination of
co-option and carrot-and-stick economic measures — absorbing activist
energy into state structures while using selective investment to reward
or punish communities.[^26] A general strike called by FEJUVE in June
2016 drew only minimal turnout.[^27]

## Organisation

Between 500 and 600 neighbourhood councils make decisions and
confederate into FEJUVE, which meets monthly. Each council has at least
200 members, elects its own leadership committee, and holds monthly
assemblies. Regular decisions are made by voting; very important
decisions require consensus.[^28] Positions of political representation
are compulsory and held by rotation. Each council's executive committee
consists of 15 secretariats, fewer than a third of which are permanent.
Presidents and secretaries are typically elected on the basis of
experience in community organising or public speaking.[^29]

Council meetings are held in circles, with men and women on opposite
sides — an arrangement that makes women more comfortable speaking. Women
make up around 20–30% of neighbourhood council leadership, a higher
proportion than in most other Bolivian popular organisations, though men
still outnumber women on committees overall.[^30][^31] Participation of
women and young people has reportedly increased during the 21st
century.[^32]

Every two years, a congress of neighbourhood councils is held, with each
council represented by four delegates — two from its leadership and two
elected by grassroots assembly. These congresses elect the FEJUVE's
central executive committee of 29 ministries, along with a President and
Vice-President, with each district of El Alto represented according to
proportional representation.[^33]

Bolivian sociologist <a href="Álvaro_García_Linera" class="wikilink"
title="Álvaro García Linera">Álvaro García Linera</a> described FEJUVE's
governance as a "horizontal kind of organisational experience", as local
councils largely make decisions autonomously without mediation from
senior leadership. Zibechi argues that FEJUVE fulfils the functions of
the state in El Alto, but as a dispersion rather than a concentration of
authority — around 500 autonomous units rather than one. "Therefore," he
writes, "no representation or control of the population is
possible."[^34] The
<a href="United_States_Agency_for_International_Development"
class="wikilink"
title="United States Agency for International Development">United States
Agency for International Development</a> considered this dispersal of
power and community cohesion a "problem", as it placed obstacles in the
way of state bureaucracy and real estate speculation.[^35]

## Economy

Neighbourhood councils organise families to access electricity and
water, build sewers, streets and pavements, and construct public goods
like parks and schools. Parent committees manage the education of
children. Communities run preventative health clinics rooted in
traditional and herbal medicines.[^36] The councils led the campaign to
establish a public university in El Alto.[^37] Collective goods are
usufructory — any individual or family can acquire them simply by using
them.[^38]

The main economic unit in El Alto is the family, and the majority of
workers are able to rotate between jobs, avoiding extreme division of
labour. Zibechi argues that adult men experience little economic
alienation, though women and children remain subjugated within community
structures.[^39]

Market vendors are represented by a street merchants' association that
regulates stall access, maintains sanitation, mediates disputes and
negotiates with municipal government. These trade union associations
also collect dues for the regional branch of the Bolivian Workers'
Centre (COB).[^40] Taxi and bus drivers are organised within trade
unions that regulate routes and allocate itineraries. El Alto's trade
union federation cooperates closely with FEJUVE on key issues.[^41]

Construction projects are collectively organised through the
neighbourhood councils, which bring together residents to gather
materials and work in shifts. Those who refuse to contribute to
collective projects are issued a symbolic fine — often uncollected.[^42]
Bolivian sociologist Félix Patzi has argued that this collective
ownership and self-management generates high community cohesion without
coercive mechanisms.[^43]

Many families sell meat, bread and groceries at market stalls managed by
the unions. The *New York Times* described the large market:

> Here vendors by the thousands offer a huge array of goods: piles of
> used T-shirts and other clothing that arrive in bales from the United
> States; cars, new or used (and sometimes stolen); neatly arranged
> arms, legs and heads from broken Barbie dolls; electric guitars;
> mummified baby llamas; pickax handles; and myriad other items. Each
> week, millions of dollars pour through the market, which operates in
> an almost total vacuum of government intervention, taxes or
> regulations.[^44]

Due to the absence of state regulation, some observers describe El
Alto's economy as an example of neoliberalism in practice — a
characterisation that sits uneasily alongside its deep traditions of
collective ownership and mutual aid.[^45]

## Criticism

### Justice and community self-defence

State police in El Alto have frequently acted as accomplices to theft,
rape and assault rather than preventing them, making recourse to the
state justice system effectively "unthinkable" for many residents.
Neighbourhood councils resolve conflicts between neighbours where
possible.[^46]

Collective self-defence groups hang dolls outside houses that have been
robbed as a symbolic warning to thieves. Residents have sometimes killed
those caught stealing. Zibechi acknowledges that this form of justice is
"too drastic", but notes that alternatives are not straightforward: an
expelled thief may return the following day with police on his side,
seeking revenge; police and thieves have both infiltrated community
justice meetings and retaliated against residents based on what was said
there.[^47]

Zibechi suggests that rural Aymaran traditions of community justice — in
which popular assemblies elect communal authorities, administer
punishment, and aim wherever possible to reintegrate the person back
into the community — may in time develop further in the urban
context.[^48]

### Gender

While FEJUVE has a comparatively higher proportion of women in
leadership than other Bolivian popular organisations, men still dominate
committee positions. Women and children remain economically and socially
subordinated within communities, even where those communities are
organised on collective and anti-hierarchical principles.[^49]

### Cooptation

The tension between FEJUVE's autonomy and its engagement with electoral
politics has been a recurring problem. The organisation was coopted by
CONDEPA in the 1980s, fragmenting its base. Its relationship with MAS
after 2005 repeated elements of this pattern: leaders who entered
government found themselves defending policies that undermined the
interests of El Alto's residents, and the organisation's capacity for
independent mobilisation declined substantially by the mid-2010s.[^50]
