# Dictatorship of the Proletariat

## Overview

The **dictatorship of the proletariat** is a concept originating in
<a href="Marxism" class="wikilink" title="Marxism">Marxism</a> that
refers to the political rule of the working class following a
revolution. In
<a href="Karl_Marx" class="wikilink" title="Karl Marx">Karl Marx</a> and
<a href="Friedrich_Engels" class="wikilink"
title="Friedrich Engels">Friedrich Engels</a>'s original usage, it was
not a description of government structure but a shorthand for the class
content of post-revolutionary power — synonymous with phrases like "rule
of the proletariat" or "workers' state." It described a transitional
period between
<a href="capitalism" class="wikilink" title="capitalism">capitalism</a>
and <a href="communism" class="wikilink" title="communism">communism</a>
during which the working class would hold political power, dismantle
capitalist <a href="class" class="wikilink" title="class">class</a>
relations, and lay the groundwork for a classless society.

The concept has a contested and shifting history. Its meaning changed
dramatically across the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and it
became one of the central points of division between anarchists and
Marxists, and later between different Marxist tendencies. By the time of
the <a href="Russian_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Russian Revolution">Russian Revolution</a>, the term had been
fundamentally redefined by
<a href="Lenin" class="wikilink" title="Lenin">Lenin</a> as rule by a
party vanguard, exercised through force unconstrained by law — a
conception sharply at odds with both Marx's original meaning and the
anarchist understanding of proletarian self-emancipation.

From an anarchist perspective, the concept encapsulates a fundamental
strategic error: the belief that the state can be used as a tool for
liberation, when in practice it reproduces hierarchy, bureaucracy, and
class domination in new forms.

## Origins

### The Roman and Jacobin background

The word "dictatorship" carried different connotations in the nineteenth
century than it does today. Its root meaning came from the Roman
Republic's *dictatura* — a constitutionally sanctioned emergency office
granted to a magistrate for a fixed term, with extraordinary but bounded
powers. It was not inherently associated with one-man tyranny; that
association came later, when figures like Sulla and Caesar converted the
institution into a vehicle for personal rule.[^1]

During the <a href="French_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a> the term broadened
further. It was applied not just to individuals but to political bodies
— the <a href="Paris_Commune_(1789)" class="wikilink"
title="Paris Commune">Paris Commune</a>, the National Convention — and
began to refer to the exercise of extraordinary power by a class or
assembly as much as by a single ruler. Revolutionary figures like
<a href="Jean-Paul_Marat" class="wikilink"
title="Jean-Paul Marat">Jean-Paul Marat</a> explicitly advocated a
temporary dictatorship to consolidate the revolution, envisioned as
strictly limited in scope and duration.[^2]

Early communist thought inherited a distinct variant of this idea: the
"educational dictatorship." Associated with followers of
<a href="Gracchus_Babeuf" class="wikilink"
title="Gracchus Babeuf">Gracchus Babeuf</a>, particularly
<a href="Philippe_Buonarroti" class="wikilink"
title="Philippe Buonarroti">Philippe Buonarroti</a>, this held that the
population, corrupted by class society, required guidance from a
revolutionary elite before it could exercise meaningful self-governance.
This minority-led provisional authority would eradicate old
institutions, establish common ownership, and educate the masses — a
conception that <a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink"
title="Mikhail Bakunin">Mikhail Bakunin</a> and subsequent anarchists
would directly challenge.[^3]

<a href="Auguste_Blanqui" class="wikilink"
title="Auguste Blanqui">Auguste Blanqui</a> developed this further,
arguing for revolutionary seizure of power by a small conspiratorial
group who would impose a temporary dictatorship to bring about
communism. It was almost certainly Blanqui who first used the phrase
"dictatorship of the proletariat," around 1837.[^4]

### Marx and Engels

Marx first used the phrase "dictatorship of the working class" in 1850
in *<a href="The_Class_Struggles_in_France" class="wikilink"
title="The Class Struggles in France">The Class Struggles in France</a>*,
and elaborated on it in correspondence and later writings. Scholar
<a href="Hal_Draper" class="wikilink" title="Hal Draper">Hal Draper</a>
argues that for Marx, the phrase was a deliberate counter to the
Blanquist conception: where Blanqui envisioned dictatorship *over* the
proletariat by a conspiratorial elite, Marx counterposed dictatorship
*of* the proletariat — the collective rule of the entire working class,
not a party or clique.[^5]

In his 1852 letter to <a href="Joseph_Weydemeyer" class="wikilink"
title="Joseph Weydemeyer">Joseph Weydemeyer</a>, Marx described the
dictatorship of the proletariat as a necessary transitional stage
between capitalism and a classless society.[^6] In his 1875
*<a href="Critique_of_the_Gotha_Program" class="wikilink"
title="Critique of the Gotha Program">Critique of the Gotha Program</a>*
he described it as "the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat" —
a transitional political form between capitalism and communism.

For Engels, writing in 1891, the concrete example of what this looked
like was the
<a href="Paris_Commune" class="wikilink" title="Paris Commune">Paris
Commune</a> of 1871: "Look at the Paris Commune. That was the
Dictatorship of the Proletariat."[^7] The Commune's features — abolition
of the standing army in favour of a popular militia, fully recallable
elected delegates paid workers' wages, unification of executive and
legislative functions, separation of church and state — represented what
Marx called a "revolution against the State itself." Crucially, neither
Marx nor Engels ever mentioned the role of a political party in their
discussions of the Commune or of the dictatorship of the
proletariat.[^8]

Engels also stated explicitly that the democratic republic was "the
specific form for the dictatorship of the proletariat," equating
proletarian rule not with one-party government but with thoroughgoing
popular democracy.[^9]

When <a href="Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a>
accused Marx of advocating an authoritarian "educational dictatorship"
by learned socialists, Marx's marginal note in his copy of
*<a href="Statism_and_Anarchy" class="wikilink"
title="Statism and Anarchy">Statism and Anarchy</a>* was simply: *Quelle
rêverie!* ("What a pipe dream!").[^10] Marx also directly rejected
Bakunin's characterisation that a workers' government would simply
dominate the peasantry, writing that the proletariat must take steps to
"win the peasant over to the revolution" and, on the question of
governance, that it "begins with the self-government of the
communities."[^11]

### The Second International

After Engels's death the concept became contested within the
<a href="Second_International" class="wikilink"
title="Second International">Second International</a>. The revisionist
wing led by <a href="Eduard_Bernstein" class="wikilink"
title="Eduard Bernstein">Eduard Bernstein</a> called for dropping it
entirely, seeing it as incompatible with parliamentary democracy.
Orthodox Marxists like
<a href="Karl_Kautsky" class="wikilink" title="Karl Kautsky">Karl
Kautsky</a> offered an inconsistent defence but essentially reduced it
to a question of governmental measures rather than class power.
<a href="Rosa_Luxemburg" class="wikilink" title="Rosa Luxemburg">Rosa
Luxemburg</a> used the term in what Draper regards as its original sense
— simply the working class in power — and saw no contradiction between
dictatorship and democracy, since proletarian rule was the rule of the
vast majority.[^12]

## Tendencies

### The Leninist Redefinition

The decisive shift came with <a href="Vladimir_Lenin" class="wikilink"
title="Vladimir Lenin">Vladimir Lenin</a>. In 1906, in the aftermath of
the failed Russian Revolution, Lenin produced a new and theoretically
unprecedented definition: "The scientific term 'dictatorship' means
nothing more nor less than authority untrammelled by any laws,
absolutely unrestricted by any rules whatever, and based directly on
force."[^13]

This definition had no basis in Marx's usage or in the historical
meaning of the term. It transformed "dictatorship of the proletariat"
from a description of class power into a justification for government
operating outside all legal frameworks. In
*<a href="The_State_and_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="The State and Revolution">The State and Revolution</a>* (1917),
Lenin drew on Marx's analysis of the Paris Commune and appeared to
advocate something close to the Commune model — abolition of the
standing army, fully recallable delegates, workers' wages for officials.
For a time his analysis seemed to converge with anarchist positions, and
several Russian anarchists joined the Bolsheviks on this basis.

In practice, the gulf was fundamental. The Bolsheviks moved rapidly to
concentrate power in the party apparatus. Factory committees were
suppressed, soviets that returned non-Bolshevik majorities were
disbanded by force, the elected Constituent Assembly was dissolved,
opposition parties were outlawed, and one-man management was imposed in
industry before the Civil War had even begun.[^14] Lenin had argued for
appointed managers with "dictatorial powers" as early as April–May
1918.[^15]

By 1919–1921 the equation was made explicit.
<a href="Leon_Trotsky" class="wikilink" title="Leon Trotsky">Leon
Trotsky</a> wrote that it was possible to speak of "the dictatorship of
the proletariat which is led by the dictatorship of its party."
<a href="Nikolai_Bukharin" class="wikilink"
title="Nikolai Bukharin">Nikolai Bukharin</a> argued that the
dictatorship of the proletariat was "in essence the dictatorship of its
vanguard." Lenin stated in 1921 that the dictatorship of the proletariat
was impossible through any organisation "embracing the whole of that
class," since the proletariat could only be led by its vanguard
party.[^16]

What had begun as Marx's term for the democratic self-rule of the
working class had become, in theory and in practice, the dictatorship of
a party over the working class.

### Later Variants

Under
<a href="Joseph_Stalin" class="wikilink" title="Joseph Stalin">Joseph
Stalin</a>, the formula was formalised: the dictatorship of the
proletariat was officially the dictatorship of the Communist Party, and
this was used to justify mass terror and the Gulag system. The
<a href="1936_Soviet_Constitution" class="wikilink"
title="1936 Soviet Constitution">1936 Soviet Constitution</a> quietly
retired the phrase, replacing it with "socialist state of workers and
peasants" — effectively admitting that the transitional period it
described had not led where promised.

After
<a href="World_War_II" class="wikilink" title="World War II">World War
II</a>, the concept fragmented further. The Soviet bloc initially
presented Eastern European states as "people's democracies" — a
distinct, supposedly multi-class path to socialism — before
reclassifying them as dictatorships of the proletariat after the 1948
split with Yugoslavia. In 1961
<a href="Nikita_Khrushchev" class="wikilink"
title="Nikita Khrushchev">Nikita Khrushchev</a> declared that the
dictatorship of the proletariat had "fulfilled its historic mission" in
the Soviet Union, redefining the USSR as a "state of the whole people" —
a formula the Chinese Communist Party denounced as revisionist betrayal.
The <a href="Eurocommunist" class="wikilink"
title="Eurocommunist">Eurocommunist</a> parties of Western Europe,
particularly the <a href="Italian_Communist_Party" class="wikilink"
title="Italian Communist Party">Italian Communist Party</a> under
<a href="Enrico_Berlinguer" class="wikilink"
title="Enrico Berlinguer">Enrico Berlinguer</a>, formally abandoned the
concept in the 1970s in favour of a democratic parliamentary road to
socialism. The <a href="French_Communist_Party" class="wikilink"
title="French Communist Party">French Communist Party</a> dropped it at
its 22nd Congress in 1976.

## Criticism

### The Anarchist Critique

Anarchists have engaged critically with this concept since it became a
point of division within the
<a href="First_International" class="wikilink"
title="First International">First International</a>. The core objection
is not to the idea of working-class power or to forceful resistance
against capitalist reaction, but to the specific claim that a state —
even a workers' state — can serve as a vehicle for liberation.

<a href="Errico_Malatesta" class="wikilink"
title="Errico Malatesta">Errico Malatesta</a> put the distinction
clearly in 1919: if "dictatorship of the proletariat" means the
effective power of all workers dismantling capitalist society, then
anarchists have no quarrel with it — but that is not a dictatorship at
all, since the rule of everyone is rule by no one in the authoritarian
sense. The problem is that its actual proponents in Russia understood
something different: the dictatorship of a party, with its decrees, its
penal laws, and its armed force — "a genuine dictatorship" that would
first defend the revolution against external enemies, then "impose upon
the workers the will of the dictators, arrest the revolution,
consolidate new interests and finally defend a new privileged class
against the masses."[^17]

<a href="Luigi_Fabbri" class="wikilink" title="Luigi Fabbri">Luigi
Fabbri</a>, in *Dictatorship and Revolution* (1921), drew a clear line
between revolutionary violence and governmental authority. Violence used
by the oppressed against those who command them is an act of liberation.
Violence that crystallises into a new power claiming authority over
others is counter-revolution, whatever name it gives itself.[^18]

<a href="Rudolf_Rocker" class="wikilink" title="Rudolf Rocker">Rudolf
Rocker</a> traced the concept's Jacobin inheritance and argued that
dictatorship of a class is impossible in practice — it always becomes
the dictatorship of a party, or of a party's leadership. The logic of
power compels even the best-intentioned rulers toward despotism:
"Dictatorship of a class cannot exist as such, for it ends up, in the
last analysis, as being the dictatorship of a given party which
arrogates to itself the right to speak for that class."[^19]

### The Bug in the Dialectic

The Marxist argument rests on Engels's formula: the state seizes the
means of production on behalf of society; classes dissolve; the state
therefore withers away. The flaw is that state ownership is not common
ownership. If the institutional structure of the state is not
fundamentally transformed — if decision-making power is not devolved to
the working class itself through genuine self-governance — then
nationalisation simply replaces private capitalists with state managers.
A new class of administrators holds power over those who work, with
distinct interests and the coercive apparatus to defend them.

As social ecologist Mason Herson-Hord argues, drawing on
<a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a>'s distinction between states
and governments: the state is not merely an organisation with a monopoly
on force, but specifically an institution of elite rule. A workers'
government organised around popular assemblies, recallable delegates,
and workers' self-management of production need not be a state in this
sense — but a centralised party apparatus claiming to speak for the
working class invariably is.[^20]

This is not simply a theoretical problem. The historical record of every
state that adopted the dictatorship of the proletariat as its guiding
principle — the Soviet Union, the People's Republic of China, Cuba —
shows the pattern Malatesta predicted: the suppression of workers'
autonomous organisations, the subordination of soviets or councils to
party control, and the permanent deferral of the promised transition to
communism. As Trotsky himself eventually acknowledged, state ownership
of the economy is only the property of "the whole people" to the degree
that social privilege disappears — and to the degree that it does not
disappear, state property remains the property of those who control the
state.[^21]

### Means and Ends

Anarchists argue that the structure of the revolutionary process cannot
be separated from the society it produces. Authoritarian means produce
authoritarian ends. A socialist society characterised by free
association, self-management, and the absence of domination cannot be
reached through a transitional period of one-party rule, bureaucratic
command, and the suppression of working-class autonomy. As
<a href="Iain_McKay" class="wikilink" title="Iain McKay">Iain McKay</a>
(writing as Anarcho) put the point in a critique of Bolshevik practice:
"If the means are authoritarian, the ends will also be so."[^22]

The anarchist alternative is not passivity or the naive belief that
capitalism will dissolve without resistance. It is the insistence that
the forms of struggle must prefigure the free society they seek to
create — workers' councils, popular assemblies, federated communes, and
workers' self-management of production built from the bottom up, which
constitute not just instruments of revolution but the embryo of the new
society within the shell of the old.
