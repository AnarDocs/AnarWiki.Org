## Overview

Sex work sits at the intersection of capitalism, patriarchy, and state
power — a point where the anarchist commitment to workers' solidarity
meets some of its most contentious internal debates. The term covers a
wide range of labour: street-based prostitution, escorting, pornography,
stripping, webcam work, and other forms of paid sexual or erotic
performance.
<a href="Emma_Goldman" class="wikilink" title="Emma Goldman">Emma
Goldman</a> observed as early as 1910 that women's economic and social
subordination made transactional sex an almost inevitable consequence of
their conditions, writing that under capitalism it is "merely a question
of degree whether she sells herself to one man, in or out of marriage,
or to many men."[^1] That analysis — grounded in class and gender rather
than moralism — remains a touchstone for anarchist engagement with the
subject.

## The Abolitionist Debate

Within anarchism, the sharpest division is between those who advocate
the abolition of sex work as a precondition of liberation, and those who
argue that solidarity with sex workers in their present conditions must
come first. Both positions claim anti-capitalist and feminist
credentials, and the conflict between them has played out in anarchist
spaces from the 2011
<a href="London_Anarchist_Bookfair" class="wikilink"
title="London Anarchist Bookfair">London Anarchist Bookfair</a> to
debates in the pages of the
<a href="Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist Federation">Anarchist Federation</a>'s *Organise*
magazine.

The abolitionist argument holds that prostitution cannot be disentangled
from male domination — that payment for sex is not really about sex but
about the assertion of power over another person's body. From this view,
campaigns for unionisation or decriminalisation represent reformism that
normalises the sex industry rather than challenging the structures that
produce it.[^2] Some abolitionist feminists, drawing on the work of
Melissa Farley, go further, treating sex work as a form of violence
against women as such, regardless of the stated views of those
performing it.

Anarcha-feminist responses to this position have been pointed. Writing
in the *Irish Anarchist Review*, Leticia Ortega noted that when
feminists use their social power to force sex workers to conform to
dominant cultural norms around sexuality, they replicate exactly the
patriarchal mechanisms they claim to oppose.[^3] The practical critique
is at least as strong: abolitionist campaigns that rely on state
intervention — increased policing, criminalisation of clients under the
so-called "Nordic model," or raids on working premises — consistently
make conditions more dangerous for the workers they claim to protect.
The Bradford women who left a brothel over poor working conditions and
set up their own co-operative were subsequently raided by police;[^4]
the Policing and Crime Act 2009 in Britain, lobbied for by feminist
organisations, effectively criminalised sex workers attempting to work
together indoors for safety.[^5]

The Australian IWW member who wrote "Sex Work: Solidarity Not Salvation"
for *Direct Action* drew a parallel that anarchists in other contexts
often avoid: the abolitionist impulse carries a class character. Like
the Victorian middle-class women's movements that originated the project
of "rescuing" prostitutes — positioning themselves as benevolent
saviours while gaining public authority in a male-dominated sphere —
contemporary abolitionists often advocate for sex workers without
listening to them, treating their stated interests and analysis as
products of false consciousness.[^6] The anthropologist Laura Agustín
has traced this lineage directly, showing how "helping" as a mode of
social power over marginalised women has a continuous institutional
history from the nineteenth century to the present.[^7]

## Workers' Organising and Solidarity

The sex worker rights position, articulated by anarchists across
multiple tendencies, begins from a simple premise: sex workers are
workers, their material interests as workers matter regardless of
whether we endorse the industry, and solidarity with them in their
struggles is not optional. As Red and Black Leeds put it, the need for
workers to organise collectively to improve their conditions is
something anarchists support irrespective of whether the work is chosen
— workers who would rather be doing a different job are not in less need
of better conditions.[^8]

In practice this has meant sex workers organising within existing union
structures — notably the
<a href="Industrial_Workers_of_the_World" class="wikilink"
title="Industrial Workers of the World">Industrial Workers of the
World</a> (IWW) in Britain, the US, and Australia, and the United Voices
of the World (UVW) in England — as well as building sex-worker-led
organisations like
<a href="SWARM" class="wikilink" title="SWARM">SWARM</a> (Sex Worker
Advocacy and Resistance Movement), the English Collective of
Prostitutes, Scot-Pep, X-talk, and Scarlet Alliance in Australia. The
importance of workers themselves controlling these organisations is
consistently emphasised: Scarlet Alliance bars employers from
membership, ensuring that its advocacy reflects workers' interests
rather than those of industry bosses.[^9]

Solidarity in practice looks like sharing information on abusive
clients, backing each other up against harassment, taking direct action
against theft and violence, and refusing to compete with one another in
ways that drive down safety standards. One sex worker in the IWW
described the union as offering something that most workplaces deny: to
be met as a worker on equal terms, rather than defined by the stigma
attached to the work.[^10]

The criminalisation framework matters acutely here. Where full
decriminalisation removes the specific laws targeting sex work (while
leaving rape, trafficking, and assault illegal), legalisation introduces
a new tier of regulation that systematically excludes the most
marginalised workers — undocumented migrants, those who cannot afford
licensing fees, those unable to be open about their work. Many
anarchists accordingly support decriminalisation not as a final goal but
as the position that most immediately reduces harm to the workers most
exposed to it.[^11]

## Capitalism, Patriarchy, and the Anti-Work Perspective

Several anarchist sex workers have located the debate within a broader
anti-work politics. If the goal is the abolition of all work — all
coerced labour under capitalism — then singling out sex work for special
condemnation before any other industry is both tactically incoherent and
practically cruel. Attempts to abolish sex work before any other work,
as Red and Black Leeds noted, face the additional problem that they
involve a commodity which can be produced at any time by anyone, making
prohibition as futile as the war on drugs and considerably more
dangerous to the workers it targets.[^12]

The group Other Weapons Distro, writing in 2019, pushed this further,
arguing against both criminalisation and the demand for state
legalisation, rejecting reforms that would bring sex workers more fully
into the regulated economy as a path to greater exploitation rather than
freedom.[^13] From this perspective, the anti-trafficking movement —
funded by governments, NGOs, and religious organisations — functions not
as a protection for workers but as a mechanism of state control: driving
gentrification through raids, enabling immigration enforcement, and
increasing the surveillance and criminalisation of migrants and people
of colour who work in the sex trade.

Luna Celeste, writing as a stripper with fifteen years of anarchist
politics, identified how sex workers are triangulated between two
systems of "caring" labour — the paid reproductive labour performed for
clients and bosses, and the coercive "care" imposed on them by carceral
feminists, non-profits, and the state. Both systems serve
capitalist-patriarchy while positioning sex workers as objects to be
managed rather than subjects of their own struggle.[^14]

The Prostitutes' War Group, occupying an insurrectionist tendency,
rejected both reform and state recognition entirely, framing
prostitution as illegalist work that places sex workers in direct
contact with the ruling class and offers unique possibilities for
sabotage and exposure of elite hypocrisy.[^15] This position has little
purchase outside insurrectionist circles but reflects a real strand of
thinking about how marginalisation can produce its own forms of power.

## Sex Work and LGBTQ+ Experience

The connection between queerness and sex work is not incidental. LGBTQ+
people are disproportionately represented in the sex trade not because
of any particular sexual openness but because they are more likely to
face family rejection, homelessness, unemployment, and exclusion from
conventional labour markets. For many, transactional sex — whether
formally structured or not — becomes a survival strategy under these
conditions.[^16] The history of queer liberation is intertwined with sex
worker organising: the Stonewall uprising was led substantially by Black
and brown trans women, prostitutes, and other gender non-conforming
people, a history that continues to inform how sex workers understand
their own struggles.[^17]

Working class lesbian history, similarly, has long recognised that sex
work offered some women a way to avoid economic dependence on men
through marriage, a pattern whose political dimensions were understood
at the time even if later forgotten as other economic options
expanded.[^18]
