# Anarchist Programmes

## Overview

**Anarchist programmes** are strategic documents outlining the
theoretical foundations, practical goals, and organisational methods of
anarchist movements. Where conventional political manifestos target
electoral success and state power, anarchist programmes articulate
visions for revolutionary transformation grounded in
<a href="mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="mutual aid">mutual aid</a>,
<a href="direct_action" class="wikilink" title="direct action">direct
action</a>, and <a href="anti-authoritarianism" class="wikilink"
title="anti-authoritarianism">anti-authoritarianism</a>.

The tradition emerged in the late 19th century alongside organised
anarchist movements. The 1884 **Program and Organization of the
International Working Men's Association** was among the earliest
attempts to codify anarchist principles into a coherent political
framework, reflecting the influence of
<a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink"
title="Mikhail Bakunin">Mikhail Bakunin</a>'s collectivist anarchism and
the growing anarchist presence within international workers' movements.
The themes it established — rejection of state socialism,
<a href="workers&#39;_self-management" class="wikilink"
title="workers&#39; self-management">workers' self-management</a>, and
the inseparability of economic and social transformation — recur
throughout anarchist programme literature ever since.

## Origins

Anarchist programme writing developed in tension with a persistent
internal objection: that formal programmes contradict anarchist
commitments to spontaneity and anti-authoritarianism. The
<a href="Platform" class="wikilink" title="Platform">Platform</a> debate
of the 1926 is the clearest expression of this tension.
<a href="Dielo_Truda" class="wikilink" title="Dielo Truda">Dielo
Truda</a>'s **Organisational Platform of the Libertarian Communists**
argued that the movement's historic failures owed something to
organisational incoherence, and that a shared theoretical and tactical
basis was a precondition for effective revolution. Critics, including
<a href="Voline" class="wikilink" title="Voline">Voline</a> and
<a href="Sebastien_Faure" class="wikilink"
title="Sebastien Faure">Sebastien Faure</a>, countered that programmatic
discipline inevitably reproduced hierarchical structures. The argument
has never been fully resolved, and subsequent programme writing has
taken place against this background.

## Tendencies

Early 20th-century programmes, typified by
<a href="Errico_Malatesta" class="wikilink"
title="Errico Malatesta">Errico Malatesta</a>'s 1920 **An Anarchist
Program**, addressed fundamental questions of revolutionary strategy:
abolition of private property and the state, establishment of
<a href="free_association" class="wikilink"
title="free association">free associations</a> of producers and
consumers, and federalist rather than centralised organisation. The
anarcho-syndicalist tradition, represented by
<a href="Gregori_Petrovich_Maximoff" class="wikilink"
title="Gregori Petrovich Maximoff">Gregori Petrovich Maximoff</a>'s 1927
**Programme of Anarcho-Syndicalism**, placed greater weight on
<a href="workers&#39;_councils" class="wikilink"
title="workers&#39; councils">workers' councils</a> and
<a href="labour_unions" class="wikilink" title="labour unions">labour
unions</a> as vehicles for revolution, with the
<a href="general_strike" class="wikilink" title="general strike">general
strike</a> as the central tactic and detailed attention to economic
organisation in the post-revolutionary period.

The Spanish anarchist movement produced some of the most developed
programmatic thinking of the 20th century. The
<a href="Confederación_Nacional_del_Trabajo" class="wikilink"
title="CNT">CNT</a>'s 1936 **Confederal Concept of Libertarian
Communism** and <a href="Diego_Abad_de_Santillán" class="wikilink"
title="Diego Abad de Santillán">Diego Abad de Santillán</a>'s **After
the Revolution** (1937) addressed in concrete terms how a large, complex
economy might be reorganised along libertarian communist lines — drawing
on the movement's actual experience of workers' self-management during
the <a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a>.

Later programmes have incorporated ecological crisis, surveillance
capitalism, and questions of identity and intersectionality without
abandoning the core rejection of state and capital. Documents like
**Community, Democracy, and Mutual Aid** (2017) and the
<a href="Black_Rose_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Black Rose Federation">Black Rose Federation</a>'s **Turning the
Tide** (2023) reflect the influence of
<a href="prefigurative_politics" class="wikilink"
title="prefigurative politics">prefigurative politics</a>, emphasising
the construction of alternative institutions alongside resistance to
existing ones.

## Criticism

The standard objection to anarchist programmes — that they are utopian
and fail to address the practical challenges of large-scale social
organisation — is as old as the tradition itself. Questions about
economic coordination, conflict resolution, and defence against external
aggression remain genuinely difficult and are handled with varying
degrees of rigour across the literature. A related critique holds that
programmatic approaches tend toward reformism over time: the more
concretely a movement specifies its transitional arrangements, the more
it risks naturalising the very structures it set out to abolish.

From within the anarchist tradition, the deeper objection is that any
attempt to specify in advance what a free society must look like is
itself an authoritarian act. Against this, proponents of programmatic
thinking argue that vagueness has historically served ruling classes
better than oppressed ones, and that clear shared commitments are a
condition of effective collective action rather than a constraint on it.
