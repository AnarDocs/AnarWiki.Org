## Overview

During the
<a href="Belle_Époque" class="wikilink" title="Belle Époque">Belle
Époque</a> (1880–1914), a particular kind of anarchist emerged from the
pressures of state repression: one defined not by a fixed locality or
organisation, but by movement. The historian Vivien Bouhey, writing in
his 2008 work *Les anarchistes contre la République*, coined the term
**gyrovague anarchists** — or **gyrovague companions** — to describe
this type: militants characterised by high geographical mobility who
functioned as the connective tissue of the anarchist movement across
France and its neighbouring regions.[^1]

The term itself is borrowed from religious vocabulary, where a
*gyrovague* denoted a wandering monk with no fixed monastery — drawn
from the Greek *γῦρος* (*gyros*, meaning circle) and the Latin *văgor*
(to wander): literally, those who wander in circles.[^2] Bouhey
repurposed it to describe companions who moved between cities and
regions, carrying news, manifestos, and organisational links wherever
they went.

## Origins

### Repression and the turn to companionship

From the 1870s onward, and accelerating through the 1880s, states across
Europe intensified their repression of anarchist activity. In France and
across the Franco-Swiss-Italian border region, this pressure dismantled
the movement's earlier international structures — the
<a href="Anti-Authoritarian_International" class="wikilink"
title="Anti-Authoritarian International">Anti-Authoritarian
International</a> and the subsequent Black International both collapsed
during the 1880s — and drove many anarchists into exile or
underground.[^3] The movement's response was to turn inward, developing
informal webs of mutual trust and recognition among militants: what
Bouhey identifies as <a href="Companionship" class="wikilink"
title="companionship"><em>companionship</em></a>.[^4]

It was within this context — repression producing flight, flight
producing contact, contact producing networks — that the gyrovague
figure emerged. As Bouhey writes: "The repression affecting anarchist
circles in various countries during the 1890s contributed to the
multiplication of these contacts, as it induced, on one hand, the flight
of prosecuted companions across their respective countries or abroad, a
flight often requiring complicity and meetings with other companions or
outlaws, and, on the other hand, the expulsion of foreign anarchists
arrested on French soil."[^5]

### Three types of mobility

Bouhey distinguishes broadly between three modes of gyrovague
activity.[^6] Some companions moved under exceptional circumstances —
sent to spread propaganda in a new region, establish contacts, and
return. The companion Justin Cazenave exemplifies this: in 1892 he
travelled from
<a href="Lausanne" class="wikilink" title="Lausanne">Lausanne</a> to
<a href="Saint-Étienne" class="wikilink"
title="Saint-Étienne">Saint-Étienne</a>, held discussions with local
anarchists, went on to
<a href="Lyon" class="wikilink" title="Lyon">Lyon</a> to have a
manifesto printed, and was shortly after spotted in
<a href="Valence" class="wikilink" title="Valence">Valence</a> asking a
Parisian comrade to forward his mail to
<a href="Marseille" class="wikilink" title="Marseille">Marseille</a>.

Others maintained multiple local anchors simultaneously over longer
periods, moving between two or more cities to which they had ties,
conducting parallel activities in each. The brothers Jacques and
Geoffroy Pflug — originally from Troyes, stationed with the 37th
Infantry Regiment in Nancy — illustrate this pattern: their friendship
with the companion Paul Serrure allowed them to maintain active contacts
in Nancy long after they had left.

A third and more radical category comprised those whose mobility was
simply constitutive of how they lived: figures who moved from town to
town preaching anarchism, or who were structurally pushed toward nomadic
lives — including
<a href="Louise_Michel" class="wikilink" title="Louise Michel">Louise
Michel</a>, who undertook extensive lecture tours across France and
beyond, as well as exiles, beggars, criminals, and
<a href="sex_worker" class="wikilink" title="sex worker">sex workers</a>
whose circumstances made fixed residence difficult or impossible.[^7]

## Tendencies

Bouhey's broader argument — against the long-held view of the Belle
Époque anarchist movement as a loose scatter of isolated individuals —
places the gyrovague at its analytical centre. In his reading, the
movement was "highly structured and organised," and the gyrovagues were
among its key mechanisms of coherence.[^8] They served as *liaison
agents*: transmitting news between groups, coordinating actions across
regions, and weaving the local networks that allowed the movement to
survive repression and propagate.

This function has a certain kinship with the federalist and
anti-hierarchical principles that anarchist organisational thought has
long favoured. Where <a href="Platformism" class="wikilink"
title="Platformism">Platformism</a> would later seek to formalise
coordination through shared programme and tactical unity, the gyrovague
achieved something similar — though informally, organically, and through
the body in motion rather than through written agreement. The result was
a movement that, rather than centralising, extended horizontally across
territory through the accumulated travels of its most mobile members.

Bouhey lists the gyrovague companions as one of nine factors enabling
the movement's coordination during this period, alongside epistolary
networks and the anarchist press. His summary bears quoting: "These
individuals or groups did not live in isolation from one another as has
been written until now. On the contrary, they were generally in
permanent contact, and this in several ways \[...\] Thanks to a minority
of 'gyrovague companions,' who acted as liaison agents by transmitting
news from one group to another."[^9]
