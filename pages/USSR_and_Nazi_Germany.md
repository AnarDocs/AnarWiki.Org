# The USSR and Nazi Germany

The relationship between the
<a href="Soviet_Union" class="wikilink" title="Soviet Union">Soviet
Union</a> and Nazi Germany passed through several sharply distinct
phases between the rise of the Nazi Party and the end of the Second
World War. Throughout the 1920s and into the early 1930s,
<a href="Weimar_Germany" class="wikilink" title="Weimar Germany">Weimar
Germany</a> and the USSR maintained close military and economic
cooperation, formalised by the Treaty of Rapallo in 1922 and extended by
the Treaty of Berlin in 1926. After Hitler took power in 1933, the
relationship cooled but was never severed, culminating in the
Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939, under which the two states
divided Eastern Europe between them, jointly invaded Poland, and traded
materials critical to the Nazi war machine. The pact ended only when
Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941, after which the Soviet Union
became, by necessity, an ally of Britain and the United States.

The history of this relationship is contested. The dominant
Marxist–Leninist narrative — repeated in Soviet historiography and by
sympathetic writers since — frames the USSR as the principal force that
defeated fascism, and presents the alliance with the West as a
vindication of the Soviet system. Anarchists and other libertarian
socialists have long disputed this account, pointing to Stalinist
sabotage of the German left during the rise of Nazism, active
partnership with Hitler from 1939 to 1941, and the catastrophic human
cost paid by ordinary Soviet citizens conscripted into a war their
leadership had spent the previous decade enabling.

## Background: Weimar Germany and the early USSR

After the
<a href="First_World_War" class="wikilink" title="First World War">First
World War</a>, both Russia and Germany were treated as outcasts by the
victors. The <a href="Treaty_of_Brest-Litovsk" class="wikilink"
title="Treaty of Brest-Litovsk">Treaty of Brest-Litovsk</a> of March
1918 had ended hostilities between them on humiliating terms for the
<a href="Bolshevik" class="wikilink" title="Bolshevik">Bolshevik</a>
government, and the <a href="Treaty_of_Versailles" class="wikilink"
title="Treaty of Versailles">Treaty of Versailles</a> left Germany
burdened with reparations and territorial loss. The two pariah states
converged. The Treaty of Rapallo, signed in April 1922, restored full
diplomatic relations and laid the groundwork for substantial
cooperation.[^1]

A secret military relationship developed alongside the public treaty.
The Soviets offered facilities deep inside their territory where the
<a href="Reichswehr" class="wikilink" title="Reichswehr">Reichswehr</a>
could build and test weapons forbidden to it under Versailles, and where
German officers could train. In return Germany supplied technical
expertise to the
<a href="Red_Army" class="wikilink" title="Red Army">Red Army</a>. From
1922 <a href="Junkers" class="wikilink" title="Junkers">Junkers</a>
built aircraft outside Moscow; from 1925 a flying school operated at
Lipetsk training future Luftwaffe pilots; from 1926 a tank school at
Kazan and a chemical weapons facility at Saratov gave the German
military a clandestine training ground for the very capabilities Hitler
would later use across Europe.[^2] The Red Army, in turn, gained access
to German military theory and industrial techniques that shaped its
modernisation through the late 1920s.

This cooperation was not driven by ideological affinity. It served the
geopolitical interests of two states each looking to break diplomatic
isolation and to undermine the Versailles order, particularly the
<a href="Second_Polish_Republic" class="wikilink"
title="Second Polish Republic">Second Polish Republic</a> backed by
France. Soviet representatives were explicit from early on that Poland
was the shared target. As early as April 1920, Victor Kopp asked the
German Foreign Office whether "there was any possibility of combining
the German and the Red Army for a joint war on Poland".[^3]

## The Comintern, "social fascism", and the destruction of the German left

The political consequence of
<a href="Stalinism" class="wikilink" title="Stalinism">Stalinism</a> for
the German left was disastrous. From 1928 the
<a href="Communist_International" class="wikilink"
title="Comintern">Comintern</a>, under Stalin's direction, adopted what
came to be called the Third Period line. Capitalism was held to be
entering its final crisis, and
<a href="social_democracy" class="wikilink"
title="Social Democrats">Social Democrats</a> — rather than the rising
fascist movements — were declared the principal enemy of the working
class. The Comintern coined the term *social fascists* for the
<a href="Social_Democratic_Party_of_Germany" class="wikilink"
title="Social Democratic Party of Germany">Social Democratic Party of
Germany</a> (SPD) and instructed the
<a href="Communist_Party_of_Germany" class="wikilink"
title="Communist Party of Germany">Communist Party of Germany</a> (KPD)
under
<a href="Ernst_Thälmann" class="wikilink" title="Ernst Thälmann">Ernst
Thälmann</a> to refuse all united action with them.[^4]

The doctrine was enforced rigidly. The combined vote of the KPD and SPD
in the early 1930s exceeded the Nazi vote, and a unified left could
plausibly have blocked Hitler's path to power through both electoral and
extra-parliamentary means. Stalinist sectarianism made that unity
impossible. The KPD at times collaborated tactically with the Nazis
against Social Democrats, voting alongside the
<a href="Nazi_Party" class="wikilink" title="NSDAP">NSDAP</a> in the
Reichstag and joining the Berlin transport strike of 1932 side by side
with Nazi unions.

Stalin's strategic calculations also extended to the German armaments
industry. In December 1928 he reached an agreement with the so-called
Union of Industrialists, securing modern weapons for the Red Army in
exchange for hard currency and political concessions. From 1929 the KPD
voted with the far-right
<a href="DNVP" class="wikilink" title="DNVP">DNVP</a> and the NSDAP in
the Reichstag against the SPD's opposition to new armoured cruisers,
despite the Communists and Nazis fighting each other in the streets.[^5]

The conflict between the KPD and SPD contributed materially to the ease
with which Hitler replaced the
<a href="Weimar_Republic" class="wikilink"
title="Weimar Republic">Weimar Republic</a> with the Third Reich.[^6]
Anarchist analysis has long held that this was not a peripheral error
but a structural feature of authoritarian socialism: a movement
subordinated to the foreign-policy interests of a single state cannot
simultaneously act as a free organ of working-class self-defence. The
historian
<a href="Eric_D._Weitz" class="wikilink" title="Eric D. Weitz">Eric D.
Weitz</a> later documented that hundreds of German Communists, many of
them Jews, were handed over by Stalin's administration to the
<a href="Gestapo" class="wikilink" title="Gestapo">Gestapo</a> following
the 1939 pact, and that a higher proportion of the KPD Politburo died in
the USSR than in Nazi Germany.[^7]

## Ideological differences and convergences

The Marxist–Leninist and National Socialist movements presented
themselves as ideological opposites, and on the formal level they were.
The KPD identified as the party of the international proletariat; the
NSDAP identified as the party of the German racial nation. The Comintern
proclaimed world revolution; Hitler proclaimed
*<a href="Lebensraum" class="wikilink" title="Lebensraum">Lebensraum</a>*
and racial hierarchy.

Beneath the formal opposition, however, the two regimes shared a great
deal in their organisational logic. Both were one-party states that
abolished independent trade unions, free press, and political
opposition. Both built mass surveillance apparatuses — the
<a href="NKVD" class="wikilink" title="NKVD">NKVD</a> and the
<a href="Gestapo" class="wikilink" title="Gestapo">Gestapo</a> — that
disappeared, tortured, and executed citizens on political grounds. Both
created systems of camps for forced labour and political imprisonment.
Both subordinated all civil society to the central party-state, demanded
ideological conformity from cultural producers, and cultivated a
personality cult around a single leader.

For anarchists this convergence was not coincidental. From
<a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink"
title="Mikhail Bakunin">Mikhail Bakunin</a>'s critique of
<a href="Karl_Marx" class="wikilink" title="Karl Marx">Karl Marx</a>
onward, the anarchist tradition had argued that any movement that
captures and centralises state power, however revolutionary its
language, will reproduce the structures of domination it claimed to
overthrow. The convergence between Stalinism and Nazism in their
treatment of dissidents, minorities, workers, and peasants was for
anarchists the empirical confirmation of that prediction. The mutual
handover of prisoners across the German–Soviet frontier from 1939,
including the transfer of German Communists to the Gestapo and Polish
anti-Nazi resisters between the two security services, illustrated how
easily the supposedly opposite poles cooperated when state interests
aligned.[^8]

Both regimes were also expansionist. The secret protocols of the
Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact carved up the independent nations between them
as if those nations were objects rather than peoples — Finland, Estonia,
Latvia, Lithuania, Poland, and Romania were assigned to "spheres of
influence" in the manner of nineteenth-century imperial diplomacy.

## The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact

By the late 1930s the Soviet leadership under
<a href="Maxim_Litvinov" class="wikilink" title="Maxim Litvinov">Maxim
Litvinov</a> had pursued a policy of
"<a href="collective_security" class="wikilink"
title="collective security">collective security</a>" aimed at containing
Nazi Germany through alliance with Britain and France. That policy
collapsed at
<a href="Munich_Agreement" class="wikilink" title="Munich">Munich</a> in
September 1938, when Britain and France ceded the
<a href="Sudetenland" class="wikilink"
title="Sudetenland">Sudetenland</a> to Hitler without Soviet
involvement. In May 1939 Litvinov was dismissed and replaced by
<a href="Vyacheslav_Molotov" class="wikilink"
title="Vyacheslav Molotov">Vyacheslav Molotov</a>, who had long favoured
rapprochement with Berlin. The Foreign Affairs Commissariat was purged
of Litvinov's supporters and of Jewish officials.[^9]

Through the summer of 1939 the Soviet government conducted parallel
negotiations with the Western powers and with Germany. On 23 August,
<a href="Joachim_von_Ribbentrop" class="wikilink"
title="Joachim von Ribbentrop">Joachim von Ribbentrop</a> arrived in
Moscow and signed a ten-year non-aggression pact with Molotov in
Stalin's presence. Stalin reportedly toasted the agreement with the
words "I know how much the German nation loves its Führer".[^10]

### The secret protocols

The pact was supplemented by a secret additional protocol that divided
Eastern Europe into German and Soviet spheres. Finland, Estonia, Latvia,
and the Romanian region of
<a href="Bessarabia" class="wikilink" title="Bessarabia">Bessarabia</a>
were assigned to the USSR. Lithuania was initially assigned to Germany,
but transferred to the Soviet sphere in a further secret protocol of 28
September 1939. Poland was to be partitioned along the line of the
Narew, Vistula and San rivers.[^11]

The Soviet government denied the existence of the secret protocols for
nearly five decades. Only in December 1989, after the
<a href="Baltic_Way" class="wikilink" title="Baltic Way">Baltic Way</a>
demonstrations of 23 August that year, did a Soviet commission
acknowledge them; the documents were declassified only after the
dissolution of the USSR.

### Joint invasion of Poland

Germany invaded western Poland on 1 September 1939. The Soviet Union
invaded eastern Poland on 17 September. The two armies met along the
agreed demarcation line, and on 22 September held a joint victory parade
in <a href="Brest,_Belarus" class="wikilink"
title="Brest-Litovsk">Brest-Litovsk</a>. The Polish state was
extinguished by 6 October. Cooperation between the Wehrmacht and the Red
Army was followed by four
<a href="Gestapo–NKVD_conferences" class="wikilink"
title="Gestapo–NKVD conferences">Gestapo–NKVD conferences</a> at which
the two security services coordinated the suppression of Polish
resistance and exchanged prisoners.[^12]

### Material supply to the Nazi war machine

The pact was accompanied by a comprehensive economic relationship. Under
the German–Soviet Commercial Agreement of February 1940, the USSR
supplied Germany with the very raw materials the British naval blockade
was designed to deny it. In the first year alone the Soviet Union
delivered one million tons of cereals, half a million tons of wheat,
900,000 tons of oil, 100,000 tons of cotton, half a million tons of
phosphates, half a million tons of iron ore, and substantial quantities
of manganese, chromium and other strategic resources. The agreement also
provided for the transit of one million tons of soybeans from
<a href="Manchukuo" class="wikilink" title="Manchukuo">Manchukuo</a>
across Soviet territory.[^13] The Soviet Navy provided a base,
<a href="Basis_Nord" class="wikilink" title="Basis Nord">Basis Nord</a>,
near <a href="Murmansk" class="wikilink" title="Murmansk">Murmansk</a>,
from which German vessels could operate clear of British patrols.[^14]

The British historians Alan S. Milward and W. Medicott have shown that
Nazi Germany was prepared only for a short war. The German military
historian <a href="Andreas_Hillgruber" class="wikilink"
title="Andreas Hillgruber">Andreas Hillgruber</a> argued that without
Soviet supplies and strategic security in the East, Germany could not
have succeeded in the West; had Germany been forced to rely on its own
raw materials from September 1939, its resources would have lasted nine
to twelve months at most.[^15] The fall of Poland, Denmark, Norway, the
Low Countries, and France was materially enabled by Soviet supply and
the security of an untroubled eastern frontier.

### Soviet expansion under cover of the pact

The USSR used the pact's protocols to extend its territory. In late 1939
it presented Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania with ultimatums forcing them
to accept Soviet military bases; in June 1940 they were occupied
outright and absorbed as Soviet republics, with deportations and
killings affecting tens of thousands of Estonians, Latvians and
Lithuanians.[^16] The
<a href="Winter_War" class="wikilink" title="Winter War">Winter War</a>
against <a href="Finland" class="wikilink" title="Finland">Finland</a>
(November 1939 – March 1940) was launched after Finland refused similar
terms; it ended with substantial Finnish territorial losses, the
<a href="1939_Soviet_attack_on_Poland" class="wikilink"
title="Soviet invasion">Soviet invasion</a> having pushed Finland
decisively toward Nazi Germany. In June 1940 the USSR also seized
Bessarabia and Northern Bukovina from Romania.

### Soviet silence as Europe burned

Through the entire period from September 1939 to June 1941 the Soviet
Union watched Nazi Germany conquer Western and Northern Europe without
protest. Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France fell.
London was bombed. The
<a href="Comintern" class="wikilink" title="Comintern">Comintern</a>
reframed the war as an inter-imperialist conflict and instructed
Communist parties abroad to oppose the Allied resistance to Hitler.[^17]
No Soviet declaration of war followed Germany's invasions; the supply of
oil, grain, and ores continued. Stalin himself argued in public that it
was Britain and France who had attacked Germany, not the other way
around.[^18]

If Soviet opposition to Nazism had been principled, this silence would
be inexplicable. It is intelligible only as the realpolitik it was: Nazi
Germany was useful to Stalin so long as it left Soviet territory alone.

## Operation Barbarossa and the reactive turn

Hitler had ordered planning for the invasion of the USSR as early as the
summer of 1940, well before any change in formal relations. On 22 June
1941, three million German and Axis troops crossed the Soviet frontier
in <a href="Operation_Barbarossa" class="wikilink"
title="Operation Barbarossa">Operation Barbarossa</a>. The
Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact ended overnight, not because Stalin's analysis
of fascism had changed but because his territory had been invaded.

Stalin had been warned repeatedly. British intelligence, the Soviet spy
<a href="Richard_Sorge" class="wikilink" title="Richard Sorge">Richard
Sorge</a> in Tokyo, and several defectors had supplied detailed accounts
of the impending attack. Stalin dismissed or suppressed all of them. The
<a href="Great_Purge" class="wikilink" title="purges of 1937–38">purges
of 1937–38</a> had executed or imprisoned the majority of the Red Army's
senior officer corps, decapitating Soviet military capacity at the worst
possible moment. Soviet forces in the western military districts had
been positioned for offensive operations, leaving them exposed to
encirclement.[^19]

The result was catastrophic. In three weeks the Red Army suffered
750,000 casualties, lost 10,000 tanks and 4,000 aircraft. Within six
months casualties exceeded four million and three million Soviet
prisoners had been taken, two million of whom would die in German
captivity by February 1942.[^20] The disaster was directly attributable
to the system Stalin had built. The leadership that now claimed credit
for resisting fascism was the same leadership that had purged the army,
signed the pact, and ignored the warnings.

## The Eastern Front and the people who fought it

The human cost of the Eastern Front is staggering and demands
acknowledgement. Approximately 27 million Soviet citizens died, the
majority of them civilians. Soldiers and civilians from across the
territories of the USSR — many of them from nations the Soviet state had
recently invaded or oppressed — fought, suffered, and died at a scale
unmatched on any other front of the war.

That sacrifice cannot be reduced to a vindication of the system that
conscripted them. Stalin's
<a href="Order_No._227" class="wikilink" title="Order No. 227">Order No.
227</a> of July 1942 ("Not One Step Back") created blocking units
charged with shooting Soviet soldiers who retreated. Soldiers were sent
into combat without weapons and ordered to retrieve them from the
fallen. NKVD detachments operated behind the front to terrorise troops
into compliance. The Wehrmacht was destroyed in the East, but it was
destroyed by people fighting for their homes, families and villages,
often despite the leadership that claimed to direct them — by commanders
who succeeded when allowed to use their judgement, by soldiers and
partisans whose courage was a direct refusal of fascist conquest, and at
a cost magnified enormously by Stalin's prior decisions.

It is striking that more than a million Soviet citizens fought against
the Soviet state on the German side during the war, in formations such
as the <a href="Russian_Liberation_Army" class="wikilink"
title="Russian Liberation Army">Russian Liberation Army</a>, the
<a href="Kaminski_Brigade" class="wikilink"
title="Kaminski Brigade">Kaminski Brigade</a>, and the various national
legions raised among Ukrainians, Belarusians, Cossacks, and the peoples
of the Caucasus and Central Asia. This collaboration was driven by many
factors — including Nazi exploitation, opportunism, and atrocity — but
it also reflected the recent experience of
<a href="collectivisation_in_the_Soviet_Union" class="wikilink"
title="collectivisation">collectivisation</a>, deportation, the
<a href="Holodomor" class="wikilink" title="Holodomor">Holodomor</a>,
and the purges. For very large numbers of non-Russian Soviet citizens,
the Soviet state was, at first, perceived as the
<a href="lesser_of_two_evils" class="wikilink"
title="lesser of two evils">lesser of two evils</a> only in retrospect,
after the genocidal nature of Nazi rule became unmistakable.[^21]

## The role of Allied material support

The Soviet capacity to fight on the Eastern Front depended substantially
on Allied — and especially American — material aid. Through Lend-Lease
the United States supplied the USSR with approximately 400,000 trucks,
14,000 aircraft, around 13,000 tanks, vast quantities of explosives,
food, fuel, locomotives, rolling stock, telephone cable, and industrial
machinery. American trucks gave the Red Army the mobility that made its
later offensives possible; American food kept Soviet cities alive
through the worst years of the war.

This is not to diminish the labour and sacrifice of Soviet workers and
soldiers. It is to make clear that the Soviet war effort was not a
self-sufficient achievement of Leninism. It was the product of an
industrial alliance the USSR had sought neither to build nor to
acknowledge until invasion forced its hand.

## The claim that only Marxism–Leninism could defeat fascism

A persistent claim in Marxist–Leninist literature, repeated through
Soviet historiography and revived in recent campist tendencies on parts
of the left, is that only the USSR — and by extension the Leninist
political model — could have defeated Nazi Germany, and that the
destruction of fascism therefore vindicates the Soviet system. The
historical record will not bear this argument.

Leninism helped fracture the German left that might have stopped Hitler
in the first place. Leninism then partnered with Nazi Germany for nearly
two years, supplying the materials that fuelled the conquest of Western
Europe. Leninism remained neutral while Europe burned, and instructed
Communist parties abroad to oppose anti-Nazi resistance. Leninism was
dragged into the war against Hitler by invasion, not principle, and at
first conducted that war so badly that millions of its own conscripts
were killed or captured in months. The people who paid the price on the
Eastern Front were not fighting for Leninism. They were fighting for
survival, and they were fighting in a system that treated their lives as
expendable.

The defeat of Nazism was a collective achievement of many peoples,
soldiers, partisans, and resistance movements — including the
<a href="Anarchist_Resistance_To_Nazi_Germany" class="wikilink"
title="anarchist resistance">anarchist resistance</a> that operated
across occupied Europe. To claim it as a vindication of Leninism is to
claim credit for putting out a fire that one helped start, watched burn,
and was eventually engulfed by.

## What the Allies could and did achieve without the USSR

The Marxist–Leninist counterfactual rests on the proposition that the
Western Allies could not have defeated Hitler alone. The Allied military
record before and during the period of Soviet involvement suggests
otherwise. Several decisive theatres of the war involved no Soviet
participation at all.

The <a href="Battle_of_Britain" class="wikilink"
title="Battle of Britain">Battle of Britain</a> in 1940 denied Germany
air superiority over Britain and ended any prospect of invasion. The
Battle of the Atlantic, fought principally by the British and Canadian
navies, kept the supply lines open against German U-boats and was won by
1943. The North African campaign of 1940–43 destroyed the Afrika Korps
and removed the Axis threat to the Middle East and the Suez Canal. The
invasion of Sicily and Italy in 1943 knocked Italy out of the war and
opened a southern European front.
<a href="D-Day" class="wikilink" title="D-Day">D-Day</a> in June 1944
was the largest seaborne invasion in history and was followed by the
systematic liberation of Western Europe by Allied armies. The Pacific
War, fought against Imperial Japan, was conducted by the United States,
Britain, China, and the dominion forces, with the USSR entering only in
the final week.

American industrial capacity dwarfed Germany's. By 1944 the United
States was producing more aircraft per month than Germany produced in a
year. Nazi Germany suffered chronic structural weaknesses — perpetual
oil shortages, an overstretched logistical network, and an atomic
programme that had fallen years behind the Manhattan Project. Long-term
German victory was not credible against Anglo-American industrial
mobilisation, even setting aside the Eastern Front entirely.

The Eastern Front accelerated Nazi Germany's defeat enormously, and at
horrific human cost. But "accelerated" is not "made possible". The claim
that only the USSR stood between Hitler and world domination requires
that an enormous Allied war effort, already succeeding on multiple
fronts, simply be erased from the record.

## Counterfactual: what might have followed Soviet neutrality

A related question is what might have happened had Hitler not turned on
the USSR in 1941. The honest answer is that the partnership of 1939–41
would have continued indefinitely, with Stalin supplying the Wehrmacht
and consolidating his own gains in Eastern Europe and the Baltic states.
There is no evidence that Stalin ever intended to break the pact from
his side. Such a continuation would have meant a longer war in the West,
more material delivered to the Nazi war effort, and the indefinite
postponement — perhaps the abandonment — of any Soviet contribution to
the destruction of fascism.

The point is not to construct a hypothetical history but to clarify the
actual one. The USSR's contribution to defeating Nazism was real,
costly, and decisive in pace. It was also belated, reactive, and
conditional on invasion. The Marxist–Leninist narrative that treats it
as a self-evident proof of Soviet virtue requires that all the years
before June 1941 be forgotten.

## Post-war Soviet narratives

After 1945 Soviet historiography reorganised the entire arc of 1928–45
around the <a href="Great_Patriotic_War" class="wikilink"
title="Great Patriotic War">Great Patriotic War</a> and the defeat of
fascism, presenting the Stalinist state as the indispensable shield of
humanity against Hitler. The pre-war pact was minimised or excused as a
defensive necessity; the secret protocols were denied for half a
century. The handover of German Communists to the Gestapo, the joint
invasion of Poland, the supply of materials to the Wehrmacht, the Order
No. 227, the catastrophic effects of the purges on Soviet preparedness —
all were absent from official accounts.

This narrative has had a long afterlife. It survived in Soviet
propaganda through the
<a href="Cold_War" class="wikilink" title="Cold War">Cold War</a>, it
shaped Russian historical memory after 1991, and it continues to inform
certain currents of contemporary
<a href="campism" class="wikilink" title="campism">campism</a> that
defend authoritarian states on the grounds that they oppose Western
imperialism. Anarchist analysis has consistently rejected it. The defeat
of fascism was the work of many peoples, fought at enormous cost across
many fronts. It is not the property of the system that helped enable it.
