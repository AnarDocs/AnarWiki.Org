# Harm Investigation & Guilt Determination

In cases where harm has occurred but the perpetrators are unknown, or
there is genuine uncertainty over what happened, communities may need to
investigate, assess evidence, weigh accusations, and provide some form
of appeal.

These are proposals for possible approaches, not fixed prescriptions.
They assume communities starting from scratch, though that would not be
necessary in practice: specialists with relevant expertise already
exist, and existing processes could potentially be adapted with greater
accountability and without the exercise of hierarchy.

> "Of course there are anti-social acts which occur without witnesses
> and so the 'guilty' party cannot be readily identified. If such acts
> did occur we can imagine an anarchist community taking two courses of
> action. The injured party may look into the facts themselves or
> appoint an agent to do so or, more likely, an ad hoc group would be
> elected at a community assembly to investigate specific crimes of this
> sort (subject to control and recall by the community)." — *An
> Anarchist FAQ*, Iain McKay

## Current Situation

It is hard to imagine anything as ineffective as the current legal,
policing, and prison system.

In the US and UK, fewer than 1% of rapes result in a conviction, with
arrests occurring in only 12% of cases in the US. In the UK, only 18.9%
of women felt safe reporting spousal abuse to police, and only 1 in 25
child abuse cases (4%) ended in conviction. On murder, the US solves
only 47% of cases (the UK 85%). For crime generally, only 5.7% of
offences in the UK resulted in a charge or summons, and around 2% of
offences in the US lead to a conviction.

## Investigation Methods

Forensic collectives could operate as voluntary specialist groups with
members trained in evidence collection and investigation, organised into
inter-community networks that share resources across regions. Rotating
membership would prevent the emergence of a permanent investigative
class. All techniques and findings would be transparent and subject to
community scrutiny.

Evidence collection would emphasise community participation, collective
witness accounts rather than sole reliance on individual testimony,
multiple confirmation sources before reaching conclusions, and
documentation standards agreed on by the communities involved.

## Guilt Determination

Tribunals might operate on consensus rather than simple majority rule,
with community observers present to ensure transparency and
multiple-tribunal review available for serious cases. Both accusers and
accused would have access to community advocates, with burden-of-proof
standards set by each community.

## Appeals

Arbitration could draw on arbitrators from neighbouring communities for
neutrality, with review covering both evidence standards and procedural
compliance. Community assemblies could serve as final arbiters in
disputed cases, with restorative remedies available for wrongful
accusations and information-sharing networks helping to prevent repeat
false accusations.

## Practical Challenges

The most significant difficulties would likely be scale (these systems
assume communities where reputation and relationships carry real
weight), resource constraints (complex investigations require
significant time, skill, and equipment), coordination across community
boundaries, gaps in forensic expertise, and the potential for conflicts
of interest or community bias.

## Possible Solutions

Regional confederations could pool resources for complex investigations,
provide neutral arbitrators for inter-community disputes, and
standardise evidence collection procedures. Specialised collectives
could develop training for investigators and arbitrators alongside
quality-control standards for forensic work, appeal procedures, and
support for the wrongfully accused.

In practice, anarchist communities would develop these systems through
experience, drawing on indigenous community practices, restorative
justice programmes, and other non-state justice models. The procedural
details would evolve, but community control and transparency would
remain the anchoring commitments.
