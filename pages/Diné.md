# Diné (Navajo)

The Diné (Navajo) nation has traditions of anti-authoritarian living
that continue to inform present-day radical and anarchist currents. The
Diné live in Dinétah, meaning "among the people," in the Southwestern
United States.

In the words of Diné matriarch Roberta Blackgoat: "This land is a sacred
land. The man's law is not our law. Nature, food and the way we live is
our law."[^1]

Diné anarchist-led projects today include the
<a href="K&#39;é_Infoshop" class="wikilink" title="K&#39;é Infoshop">K'é
Infoshop</a>[^2] and <a href="Táala_Hooghan_infoshop" class="wikilink"
title="Táala Hooghan infoshop">Táala Hooghan infoshop</a>.[^3]

## Culture

Perhaps the core value in Diné culture is *hózhó*, meaning harmony with
existence. Other key values include *T'áá ni'ínít'éego t'éiyá*, meaning
autonomy, and *Nahasdzáán dóó Yádiłhił Bitsąądęę Beenahaz'áanii*,
meaning the natural order of Mother Earth and Father Sky.[^4]

The concept of *k'é* encompasses mutual aid with each other and with
human and nonhuman neighbors. According to Diné anarchists Brandon
Benallie, Radmilla Cody, and Kauy Bahe:

> Anthropologists often describe k'é as the Navajo kinship system, but
> Benallie and Bahe told me it's much more than that. "It's our theory
> of everything," Benallie said. "It's our string theory. It's how we're
> connected to everything—but specifically how that kinship is
> reciprocated and maintained."
>
> *K'é* "is this huge overlapping philosophy that the whole universe is
> interconnected," Bahe explained. "But it's also these relationships
> that we have with one another and with the elements that exist in the
> world, whether that be the weather or the water or the animals."
>
> \[...\] Benallie said his grandfather always told him, "We are all
> given unique gifts and abilities, and we must do what we can to
> provide for others with our unique gifts and abilities."
>
> He added that at the time, "we didn't know it as mutual aid, that was
> just *k'é*."[^5]

The Diné social structure was historically
<a href="matrilineal" class="wikilink"
title="matrilineal">matrilineal</a> and matrilocal: women owned
livestock, dwellings, and agricultural land, and upon marriage a man
moved to live with his wife's family. Daughters received generational
inheritance, and in cases of separation women kept both property and
children. This arrangement distributed economic power through the
maternal line rather than concentrating it in the hands of individual
male heads of household.

The clan system, or *k'é*, is exogamous — people marry outside their own
clans, which for this purpose include the clans of all four
grandparents. Clan membership creates bonds of reciprocal obligation
across large distances, such that members of a single clan living
hundreds of miles apart remain bound by mutual responsibility. This web
of obligation served as a practical mechanism of mutual aid prior to any
formal state structures.

## Organisation

Prior to the forced establishment of reservations, the Diné had no
hierarchical governance. Benallie describes traditional Diné political
structure as anarchist:

> "Being Diné could be considered anarchist because we never had chiefs;
> we didn't have a hierarchy. It was always horizontal," Benallie said.
> "Communism and anarchism derived ideology from Franciscan missionaries
> who came here in the 1500s and 1600s and studied Indigenous societies.
> And you have Engels, Marx, and Bakunin reading the journals of these
> religious figures and how these religious figures describe Indigenous
> societies at that time."
>
> Benallie argues that Navajo kinship began to break down as the tribe
> tried to negotiate with the United States government. "The first
> version of the Navajo Nation government was called the Navajo business
> council," he said. "It was formed primarily to facilitate the signing
> of oil and gas leases, and coal leases, in the 1920s."
>
> Once horizontal, Benallie said, Navajo society became patriarchal and
> hierarchical — and that structure facilitated the destruction of land
> and water for resource extraction.[^6]

Klee Benally elaborates on the non-coercive character of traditional
Diné leadership:

> "Naat'aáni (the one who speaks) have been misinterpreted by colonial
> anthropologists as 'leaders' of Diné yet their role, as those
> responsible for the medicine bundles for their families, was
> ceremonial and not absolute or coercive. This way of being is
> incompatible with any form of centralized governance. It is incoherent
> to the State."[^7]

The Naat'aáni thus functioned as speakers and ceremonial custodians
rather than rulers — their authority rested on persuasion and communal
trust, not coercion or inherited rank. Decisions were reached through
collective deliberation rather than imposed from above, a mode of
organisation consistent with what anarchists describe as
<a href="prefiguration" class="wikilink"
title="prefiguration">prefiguration</a> — the living-out of
non-hierarchical relations in the present rather than as a deferred
goal.

## Economy

The earliest Diné were gatherer-hunters before establishing permanent
roots in Dinétah. They subsequently shifted to plant-based farming,
likely learning agricultural techniques from the Pueblos. Diné
agriculture centred on the Three Sisters of corn, beans, and squash — a
polyculture well suited to the arid landscape and requiring collective
tending across extended family networks.[^8]

With Spanish colonisation the domestic sheep was introduced and became a
central part of Diné diet and economy.[^9] Sheep became a form of
currency and a measure of familial status, and women spun and wove wool
into blankets and clothing that were both used and traded. This economy
remained largely outside market relations and state control until
American expansion in the mid-nineteenth century forced the Diné into
cycles of treaty, dispossession, and dependency.

The federal government's Navajo Livestock Reduction programme, imposed
from 1933 into the 1950s, was a direct assault on this economic base.
Framed as environmental conservation, the programme forced the
compulsory slaughter of Diné livestock, cutting half the cash income of
many families. Women were especially harmed, as they were the principal
owners of herds within the matrilineal economy. The Diné were united in
opposition but unable to stop it — a stark illustration of how state
intervention dismantled a functioning non-capitalist economy.

## Environment

Diné culture is built around *hózhó* — harmony with existence,
encompassing human and nonhuman nature alike. Klee Benally describes
this as a living covenant rather than an abstract principle:

> "For Diné, our lives are guided in relation to six sacred mountains
> that are the pillars of our cosmology. Each of these mountains is
> adorned in sacred elements and presents a teaching of how we maintain
> and restore harmony as we exist in this world. Through our ceremonies
> and prayers we maintain a living covenant (physically maintained as
> Dził Leezh or mountain soil bundles) to exist in harmony with
> nature."[^10]

The Holy People instructed the Earth People to regard four sacred
mountains as the boundaries of Dinétah: Blanca Peak (Sisnaajiní) in
Colorado, Mount Taylor (Tsoodził) in New Mexico, the San Francisco Peaks
(Dookʼoʼoosłííd) in Arizona, and Hesperus Mountain (Dibé Nitsaa) in
Colorado. These mountains are not merely symbolic — they anchor the Diné
covenant with the land against the logic of extraction and enclosure.

This covenant has been tested repeatedly. From the 1940s onward, large
quantities of uranium were discovered in Navajo land and mined without
adequate environmental protection for workers, waterways, or land. The
Diné have reported high rates of death and illness from lung disease and
cancer resulting from contamination — a direct consequence of the
colonial state granting resource extraction rights over Diné territory
without consent.

## Justice

Robert Yazzie, former Chief Justice of the Navajo Nation Supreme Court,
describes the Diné approach to peacemaking, which operates outside the
punitive logic of the state:

> The first order of business the relatives would do in the peacemaking
> process is to get to the bottom of a problem. In court, I would sue
> you for battery and the state would say we have to prove all the
> elements of a crime and use the rules or the law to prove that you are
> guilty… that's beside the point. What matters here is: why did this
> act happen in the first place? There's a reason why the harm has
> occurred. Let's deal with that. Maybe we have a history of problems
> between the two of us. If we can get to the bottom of a problem, all
> the other stuff will fall into place. The damage can be acknowledged
> by you, and I can go away happy from the process, knowing that you say
> that you're not going to do it again.[^11]

This restorative approach — focused on repairing relationships rather
than punishing individuals — stands in contrast to the carceral logic of
the colonial state. It treats harm as a social rupture requiring
collective healing rather than a crime requiring state-administered
retribution.

## Colonisation and Resistance

Spanish contact brought trade, livestock, and eventually military
pressure. From the 1770s onward, Spanish expeditions pushed into Diné
territory. The subsequent American invasion of 1846 initiated a more
systematic campaign of dispossession. Between 1863 and 1864, Colonel Kit
Carson swept through Navajo land, destroying crops and dwellings and
forcing starvation, driving around 9,000 Diné on the Long Walk — a
forced march of over 300 miles to internment at Bosque Redondo, Fort
Sumner. The internment was catastrophic: the government failed to
provide adequate water, food, or shelter, and disease and crop failure
were endemic.

The Long Walk represents the violence by which horizontal Diné society
was broken and subordinated to state authority. The 1868 Treaty of
Bosque Redondo allowed survivors to return to a portion of their
homeland — but as a reservation, with sovereignty curtailed and
territory diminished.

Further assault came through the boarding school system. From 1870, Diné
children were forcibly removed to schools operating under English-only
curricula and militaristic discipline, where speaking Navajo was
punished. The explicit aim was cultural destruction — the elimination of
the social fabric through which Diné non-hierarchical relations were
reproduced across generations.

The Navajo Nation government itself, as Benallie notes, was not an
expression of Diné tradition but a colonial imposition — first convened
in the 1920s to sign off on resource extraction leases, and structured
from the outset to serve external economic interests rather than Diné
communal life.

## K'é Infoshop

An anarchist organisation on the Navajo Nation reservation in so-called
"Arizona," the
<a href="K&#39;é_Infoshop" class="wikilink" title="K&#39;é Infoshop">K'é
Infoshop</a> engages in mutual aid. During the COVID-19 pandemic in
2020, they were "supplying elders, families, and the immunocompromised
with food and medical supplies."[^12] Their donation page is at
[2](http://www.keinfoshop.org/donate/).
