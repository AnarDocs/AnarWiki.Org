# Incentives

**Incentives** are rewards or costs used to influence behaviour. From an
anarchist perspective, the design of incentive systems reveals
fundamental questions about power, autonomy, and social organisation.
Capitalist and statist societies rely heavily on coercive incentives
(the profit motive, legal punishment) and manipulative techniques
(nudging, choice architecture); anarchist alternatives emphasise
intrinsic motivation, mutual aid, and freely chosen cooperation.

## Types of Incentive Systems

### Capitalist Incentives

In capitalist systems, the primary incentive structure is the **profit
motive** — the drive to maximise financial gain.[^1] Survival depends on
monetary exchange, so people work primarily to earn money for basic
needs rather than from intrinsic interest or community benefit. Wealth
accumulation becomes the measure of success, tying social status, power,
and security to financial assets. Competition replaces cooperation as
the dominant mode of social relation.

Critics argue the profit motive leads to exploitation, environmental
destruction, and the subordination of human needs to capital
accumulation. As Michael Moore argued regarding healthcare: "We should
have no talk of profit when it comes to helping people who are sick. The
profit motive should be nowhere involved in this."[^2]

### State-Based Incentives

State systems employ positive incentives (tax breaks, subsidies, public
recognition) and negative incentives (fines, imprisonment, loss of
rights). Anarchists criticise these as coercive by nature — state
incentives ultimately rest on the threat of violence — and as
hierarchical by design, treating people as subjects to be managed rather
than autonomous agents.

### Libertarian Paternalism and Nudging

**Libertarian paternalism** and **nudging** represent a supposedly
'soft' approach to behavioural control. Developed by Richard Thaler and
Cass Sunstein, these techniques involve designing 'choice architecture'
to steer people toward particular decisions while preserving the
appearance of free choice.[^3] Examples include setting organ donation
to 'opt-out' rather than 'opt-in', placing healthy foods at eye level in
cafeterias, default enrolment in retirement savings plans, and framing
tax payments as 'contributions' rather than 'obligations'.

#### Anarchist Critique of Nudging

While presented as preserving freedom of choice, nudges are explicitly
designed to manipulate behaviour without the subject's conscious
awareness — violating principles of transparency and informed consent.
The technique requires 'choice architects' who decide what behaviours
are desirable for others, creating a class of experts with power over
ordinary people and replicating hierarchical structures. Rather than
helping people develop critical thinking and make genuinely autonomous
decisions, nudging bypasses rational deliberation to produce
predetermined outcomes.

The same techniques used to encourage healthy eating can be deployed for
social control. The 1938 Austrian Anschluss referendum and 1978 Chilean
consultation both used ballot design to 'nudge' voters toward desired
outcomes.[^4] As critics note, 'nudging is also a euphemism for
psychological manipulation as practiced in social engineering.'[^5]
Nudging also accepts existing power structures rather than challenging
their systemic causes.

## Behavioral Engineering: The Case of Walden Two

B.F. Skinner's 1948 novel *Walden Two* presents a utopian community
based on **behavioural engineering** — the systematic application of
operant conditioning to shape human behaviour.[^6] Children are raised
communally using conditioning techniques; a 'Walden Code' encourages
members to credit achievements to the community rather than to
individuals; work assignments operate on a credit system rewarding
unpleasant tasks with more leisure time; and a small group of 'Planners'
design the social structure based on behavioural science.

### Anarchist Concerns with Walden Two

Despite Skinner's claims to avoid authoritarianism, Walden Two relies on
behavioural experts who design the environment to produce predetermined
outcomes. As one critic noted, Frazier 'has sole control over the
political system and its policies. It is he who regulates food, work,
education, and sleep.'[^7] The community produces compliance through
environmental manipulation rather than genuine voluntary agreement —
members are 'engineered' to want what the system needs them to want. The
system explicitly rejects free will and human agency, viewing people as
products of environmental conditioning, which contradicts the anarchist
emphasis on self-determination. Like Plato's Republic, it requires wise,
benevolent rulers who somehow remain uncorrupted by their power.

Real-world attempts to create Walden Two communities, such as Twin Oaks,
have largely abandoned Skinner's behavioural engineering model while
retaining some cooperative economic structures.

## Anarchist Alternatives: Incentives in The Dispossessed

Ursula K. Le Guin's *The Dispossessed* (1974) depicts an anarchist
society (Anarres) where incentives function without state coercion or
capitalist accumulation, contrasted against the propertarian planet
Urras where work is motivated by financial need, social status,
careerism, and fear of poverty.

On Anarres, where money and government do not exist, people work from
intrinsic satisfaction in contributing, from a strong sense of
collective responsibility — particularly during crises — and from the
desire not to be seen by peers as a 'shirker'. Individual initiative is
possible through freedom to pursue passions and form voluntary work
syndicates. Le Guin does not romanticise this: she shows how even
anarchist societies develop informal hierarchies and bureaucratic
tendencies that require continual challenge.

## The Myth of Rational Self-Interest

### Ayn Rand's Egoism

Ayn Rand's *The Virtue of Selfishness* (1964) presents perhaps the most
explicit defence of self-interest as a moral principle. Rand argues that
selfishness is 'a moral good', that each person must act as 'the
beneficiary of his action', and that altruism is immoral because it
subordinates the individual to others.

### Anarchist Critique

Anarchists reject this framework on several grounds. Rand presents a
false choice between dominating selfishness and self-sacrificing
altruism, ignoring mutual aid, where helping others also benefits
oneself. By elevating self-interest above all else, the philosophy
provides moral cover for exploitation, property accumulation, and
refusal to address systemic injustice. It also ignores the fundamentally
social nature of human beings, whose wellbeing depends on relationships
and community. Rand mistakes individual autonomy — which anarchists
support — for atomised selfishness, which is something else entirely.
True autonomy emerges through free association, not isolation. Finally,
by presenting self-interest as timeless human nature, the argument
obscures how current economic systems actively create and reward selfish
behaviour while punishing cooperation.

As Kropotkin demonstrated in *Mutual Aid*, cooperation and solidarity
have been far more important to human evolution and survival than
competitive self-interest. The widespread existence of cooperative
behaviours — from childcare to barn-raising to labour unions — suggests
humans are naturally inclined toward mutual support when not constrained
by hierarchical systems.

## Toward Anarchist Incentive Systems

Anarchist approaches to incentives cultivate internal drives rather than
external rewards: joy in creative work, satisfaction from helping
others, pride in craft, curiosity and learning. Community bonds provide
motivation without coercion through mutual respect, reciprocity within
gift economies, and collective problem-solving. When people participate
in decisions that affect them, they are motivated to implement those
decisions — hence the anarchist emphasis on direct democracy,
transparent decision-making, and the right of exit from associations.
When basic needs are met unconditionally through commons-based resource
sharing, people are free to pursue higher motivations rather than
scrambling for survival.

Cultural transformation matters alongside structural change: education
oriented toward critical thinking rather than obedience, art and media
that celebrate cooperation, and traditions that support solidarity. Le
Guin imagines this even at the level of language, with Pravic — the
language of Anarres — encoding mutual aid in its grammar.
