# Democratic Confederalism

## Overview

**Democratic Confederalism** is a political theory and practical
programme developed by
<a href="Kurdistan_Workers&#39;_Party" class="wikilink"
title="Kurdistan Workers&#39; Party">Kurdistan Workers' Party</a> (PKK)
leader <a href="Abdullah_Öcalan" class="wikilink"
title="Abdullah Öcalan">Abdullah Öcalan</a>, first formally declared in
2005. It proposes a stateless form of social organisation structured as
a <a href="confederation" class="wikilink"
title="confederation">confederation</a>, built on the principles of
grassroots autonomy, communal economy, radical feminism, ecology, and
cultural pluralism. Rather than seeking an independent Kurdish
nation-state, it advocates the construction of self-governing democratic
institutions from the commune upward — operating alongside, and
ultimately superseding, the authority of existing states.

The theory draws most directly from
<a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a>'s
<a href="Libertarian_Municipalism" class="wikilink"
title="Libertarian Municipalism">Libertarian Municipalism</a> and
<a href="Communalism" class="wikilink"
title="Communalism">Communalism</a>, synthesising these with Öcalan's
analysis of Middle Eastern history, the Kurdish national question, and
the global crisis of capitalist modernity. It has been put into partial
practice in <a href="Rojava" class="wikilink" title="Rojava">Rojava</a>
(the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria) since 2012,
making it one of the most significant living experiments in
anarchist-adjacent political organisation in the contemporary world.

While it operates within the broad current of
<a href="Libertarian_Socialism" class="wikilink"
title="Libertarian Socialism">Libertarian Socialism</a>, its
relationship to classical
<a href="Anarchism" class="wikilink" title="Anarchism">Anarchism</a> is
contested. Öcalan neither identifies as an anarchist nor explicitly
rejects the term; his framework preserves space for co-existence with
existing states and retains organised armed self-defence — positions
that generate genuine tension with much of the anarchist tradition.

## Origins

### The Kurdish Question and the Limits of the Nation-State

The PKK was founded in the 1970s within the context of Cold War
geopolitics and the global wave of national liberation movements. Its
original ideological basis was Marxist-Leninist, directed toward the
creation of an independent Kurdish nation-state through revolutionary
armed struggle.[^1]

By the 1990s, however, Öcalan had grown increasingly critical of this
framework. The Kurdish question, he came to argue, was not merely a
problem of ethnicity or national self-determination soluble through
state-building. A Kurdish nation-state constructed within the logic of
capitalist modernity would simply reproduce the same structures of
exploitation, homogenisation and hierarchy that had oppressed Kurdish
society in the first place.[^2] As he put it: *the call for a separate
nation-state results from the interests of the ruling class or the
interests of the bourgeoisie but does not reflect the interests of the
people.*[^3]

The nation-state, in Öcalan's analysis, is the political form most
suited to capitalism — a centralising, monopolising, homogenising force
that wages cultural and physical genocide against diversity, reduces
citizenship to a form of state slavery, and structurally depends on the
exploitation of women as its deepest internal colony.[^4] Nationalism
functions as the quasi-religion of this system, and positivist science
and institutionalised sexism as its further ideological props.

### Öcalan's Intellectual Turn

Öcalan was captured by Turkish intelligence in 1999 and has been held in
near-total isolation on the prison island of İmralı ever since. It was
during this imprisonment — working through hundreds of books, including
Turkish translations of Western philosophical and political texts — that
he developed the theoretical framework that became Democratic
Confederalism.[^5]

The decisive influence was <a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a>. Öcalan read *The Ecology of
Freedom* and other works in Turkish translation, and reportedly
described himself as a *good student* of Bookchin's work, recommending
his books to Kurdish mayors across the region.[^6] In April 2004, a
message was sent to Bookchin — then elderly and ill — informing him of
Öcalan's engagement with his ideas. The two corresponded briefly before
Bookchin's death in 2006.

Other significant influences included
<a href="Fernand_Braudel" class="wikilink"
title="Fernand Braudel">Fernand Braudel</a>,
<a href="Immanuel_Wallerstein" class="wikilink"
title="Immanuel Wallerstein">Immanuel Wallerstein</a>,
<a href="Maria_Mies" class="wikilink" title="Maria Mies">Maria Mies</a>,
and <a href="Michel_Foucault" class="wikilink"
title="Michel Foucault">Michel Foucault</a>, as well as deep engagement
with Sumerian mythology and the history of Neolithic societies in the
Fertile Crescent — from which Öcalan drew the claim that democratic,
communal forms of social organisation long predate the state and
represent society's natural mode of self-organisation.[^7]

The Declaration of Democratic Confederalism in Kurdistan was issued in
March 2005, followed by the book *Democratic Confederalism* and, later,
the four-volume *Manifesto of Democratic Civilization*.[^8]

## Core Principles

### The Commune as Foundation

Democratic Confederalism organises social life from the bottom up, with
the **commune** as its fundamental unit. Every village, neighbourhood
and street is envisaged as a self-governing commune in which free
individuals discuss, decide, and act collectively on the full range of
social questions — economic, cultural, ecological and political. No
citizen should exist outside some communal structure.[^9]

Communes federate upward into neighbourhood assemblies, city assemblies
and regional congresses, with delegates subject to recall and higher
bodies serving only to coordinate and implement the will of lower ones.
Decision-making power rests with the local level; centralisation is a
tool of coordination, not of authority.

Gender parity is structurally mandated throughout: all institutions
operate with co-spokespersons, women's communes and assemblies exist at
every level with autonomous authority, and the *jineology* (women's
science) framework developed within the movement treats women's
liberation not as a secondary concern but as the foundational condition
for any genuine social freedom.[^10]

### Against the State

The state, in Öcalan's account following Bookchin, is not a neutral
administrative instrument. It is the institutionalisation of hierarchy
and class power — older than capitalism, but capitalism's most essential
political vehicle. The nation-state in particular is *the cage of
natural society*,[^11] a machine of cultural homogenisation,
bureaucratic domination, and organised military violence.

Democratic Confederalism does not seek to capture or reform the state.
Its goal is the construction of parallel, self-governing social
institutions — assemblies, cooperatives, academies, self-defence units —
that over time make the state's functions redundant and its authority
illegitimate. As Öcalan writes, the state *will be overcome when
democratic confederalism has proved its problem-solving
capacities.*[^12]

This is a long-term, prefigurative strategy rather than an
insurrectionary one. Peaceful coexistence with existing states is
admitted as a tactical reality, provided the state does not intervene in
central matters of self-governance or pursue cultural assimilation — but
this coexistence is explicitly not acceptance of state legitimacy.

### Ecology and Economy

Ecological sustainability is a structural principle, not an add-on.
Following Bookchin's social ecology, Democratic Confederalism
understands the domination of nature as continuous with the domination
of people: both flow from hierarchical social relations. An ecological
society requires the dismantling of those relations.

Economically, the model rejects both private capitalism and state
capitalism. A communal economy grounded in use-value — oriented toward
meeting social needs rather than accumulating profit — is to be
constructed through cooperatives operating under democratic control. The
financial system is tolerated only to the extent that it serves
productive activity; the practice of making money from money is rejected
outright.[^13]

### Self-Defence

Democratic Confederalism maintains that communities without self-defence
lose their identity, their capacity for collective self-determination,
and their political existence. Self-defence is understood broadly —
encompassing the preservation of cultural identity, political
consciousness, and democratisation processes, as well as military
capacity.

Armed self-defence forces operate under direct democratic oversight,
with their composition and duties determined by civilian institutions.
The military monopoly of the nation-state is explicitly rejected: armed
forces serve society's defence, not the state's power.[^14]

## Relationship to Libertarian Municipalism and Communalism

Democratic Confederalism is, in its theoretical architecture, an
application and adaptation of Bookchin's programme to the specific
conditions of the Kurdish liberation struggle and the Middle East more
broadly.

<a href="Libertarian_Municipalism" class="wikilink"
title="Libertarian Municipalism">Libertarian Municipalism</a> —
Bookchin's proposal for the gradual building of a network of autonomous
municipal assemblies that would constitute a counter-power to the
centralised state — provides the basic institutional model. The stress
on face-to-face democracy in assemblies, the confederation of
municipalities, the commune as the locus of political life, and the
ecological economy all come directly from this tradition.[^15]

<a href="Communalism" class="wikilink"
title="Communalism">Communalism</a> — Bookchin's later, more explicitly
political articulation of this project, which insisted on direct
engagement with politics and the construction of a dual power capable of
challenging capital and state — is the framework Öcalan most closely
follows. The emphasis on building genuinely counter-institutional
structures, not merely cultural or economic alternatives, and the
willingness to operate in tension with existing political forms,
reflects this inheritance.

Where Democratic Confederalism diverges from or extends Bookchin is in
its embeddedness within an actually existing national liberation
movement, its foregrounding of feminist theory as a founding political
principle (rather than an associated concern), its engagement with
Islamic and Middle Eastern cultural heritage, and its development of
*jineology* as an autonomous intellectual tradition. Bookchin's
municipalism was largely theorised from a North American and European
context; Öcalan's adaptation required a reckoning with colonialism,
genocide, and statelessness as lived conditions rather than analytical
categories.

## Tensions with Traditional Anarchism

The relationship between Democratic Confederalism and
<a href="Anarchism" class="wikilink" title="Anarchism">Anarchism</a> has
generated genuine and productive debate within anti-authoritarian
circles. The framework shares anarchism's rejection of the state, its
commitment to prefigurative politics, its anti-capitalism, its
ecological and feminist dimensions, and its insistence on organising
from below. Many anarchists, particularly those influenced by Bookchin
or engaged with anti-colonial struggles, regard it as a serious and
inspiring development within the broad libertarian socialist tradition.

However, several tensions are real and should be named honestly.

### The Question of the State

Classical anarchism holds that the state cannot be reformed, co-opted,
or gradually displaced — it must be abolished. Öcalan's formulation that
democratic confederalism is *not at war with any nation-state* and that
*peaceful coexistence is possible as long as the nation-state does not
interfere with central matters of self-administration*[^16] sits
uneasily with this tradition. Critics within anarchism argue that such
accommodation risks legitimising state structures and blunting the
transformative thrust of the project — that making peace with the
Turkish, Syrian, Iraqi or Iranian states, even tactically, is to make
peace with genocide, colonial domination, and capitalist exploitation.

Defenders respond that this is a pragmatic recognition of the conditions
facing a stateless people subject to physical extermination, not a
theoretical concession to statism. The long-term trajectory of
Democratic Confederalism remains the withering away of the state;
co-existence is a survival strategy, not a goal.

### Nationalism and the National Question

Democratic Confederalism explicitly positions itself as
anti-nationalist, and Öcalan's critique of the nation-state is
thoroughgoing. Yet it emerged from and remains embedded in a national
liberation movement. The framework is addressed to *the Kurdish people*
and seeks to solve *the Kurdish question*. Critics, including some
scholars, have argued that this constitutes a form of *Turkeyification*
(*Türkiyelileşme*) — that by accepting Turkey's constitutional order and
rejecting federalism or official Kurdish language status, the DEM
Party's practice integrates Kurds into the Turkish state rather than
building genuine autonomy.[^17]

More broadly, some anarchists are cautious about any framework that
begins from the assertion of a national or ethnic identity, even where
that identity is defined in explicitly non-essentialist, pluralist
terms. The tension between the universalism of anarchist politics and
the particular demands of oppressed peoples has never been fully
resolved within the tradition, and Democratic Confederalism brings it
sharply into focus.

### Armed Organisation

The PKK and its affiliated armed wings — the YPG, YPJ, and others —
represent a form of organised military force that, however
democratically structured in principle, has no close precedent in
classical anarchism's vision of popular militia. The question of whether
maintaining an organised, hierarchical armed force — even under communal
oversight — reproduces state-like dynamics is contested. Anarchist
opinion ranges from enthusiastic solidarity with the Rojava experiment
to principled unease about the relationship between the democratic
confederalist institutions and the armed organisations that defend them.

### Öcalan's Own Position

Öcalan does not identify as an anarchist. He has engaged critically with
anarchism as a tradition, acknowledging its importance while arguing
that it has historically lacked the capacity to build lasting social
institutions. His framework retains elements — particularly the
willingness to operate in relation to existing state structures — that
place it in a different theoretical register from most anarchism. This
should be acknowledged rather than papered over: Democratic
Confederalism is best understood as an anarchist-adjacent or libertarian
socialist tendency with deep affinities to, but real distinctions from,
the anarchist tradition proper.

## Rojava: Practice and Limits

The most sustained attempt to implement Democratic Confederalism has
been the revolution in Rojava, beginning in 2012 as Syrian state
authority collapsed amid civil war. The
<a href="Democratic_Union_Party" class="wikilink"
title="Democratic Union Party">Democratic Union Party</a> (PYD) declared
the autonomy of three cantons — Cizire, Kobanê, and Afrin — which
eventually formed the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria
(AANES).

Within this territory, communes, assemblies, and cooperatives were
established across Kurdish, Arab, Assyrian, and Yazidi communities.
Women's institutions with autonomous authority operate at every level.
Cooperative agriculture and ecological land management have been
implemented. The co-presidency system mandates equal gender
representation in all executive roles.[^18]

The experiment has faced enormous pressures: Turkish military invasion,
US and international entanglement, the ongoing Syrian conflict, economic
embargo, and the structural difficulty of building new institutions in
the midst of war. Commentators sympathetic to the project acknowledge
that the gap between the ideals of Democratic Confederalism and actual
practice within AANES remains significant, and that military necessity
has at times distorted the democratic character of the project.[^19]

Nevertheless, Rojava represents the most ambitious living experiment in
libertarian socialist self-organisation of the early twenty-first
century, and its trajectory is of direct concern to the international
anarchist movement.

## Criticism

Beyond the tensions with anarchism discussed above, Democratic
Confederalism has attracted criticism from multiple directions.

Kurdish nationalists and some scholars of Kurdish politics argue that by
abandoning the goal of an independent state, Öcalan has foreclosed the
most realistic path to genuine Kurdish self-determination. The
acceptance of existing borders and constitutional orders — however
critically — is seen as legitimising the very states responsible for the
suppression and massacre of Kurdish people. Soleimani and Shojai's
concept of *Turkeyification* captures this concern: the argument that
Öcalan's framework, as implemented through legal Kurdish political
parties in Turkey, functions as a vehicle for integration rather than
liberation.[^20]

From the left more broadly, questions have been raised about the
relationship between the communalist institutions and the PKK as a
centralised political-military organisation. Democratic Confederalism
theorises a clear distinction between the democratic confederal
structures (which are societal and autonomous) and the political
movement (which advocates for them). In practice, this distinction is
not always observed, and the degree to which Öcalan's personal authority
functions as an ideological anchor for the movement sits in tension with
its anti-hierarchical principles.
