# Anarchism in Bristol and the West Country

<a href="Bristol" class="wikilink" title="Bristol">Bristol</a> and the
wider West Country have hosted an anarchist current for the best part of
two centuries, drawing on older traditions of West Country radicalism
that fed into the
<a href="Socialism" class="wikilink" title="socialist">socialist</a> and
labour movements of the late nineteenth century. Although
<a href="England" class="wikilink" title="English">English</a> anarchism
has often been overshadowed in the literature by more dramatic episodes
elsewhere — the <a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a> above all — a
continuous local tradition can be traced through the romantic radicals
of the late eighteenth century, the strike wave and the Bristol
Socialist Society of the 1880s and 1890s, the syndicalist agitation of
the years before the
<a href="First_World_War" class="wikilink" title="First World War">First
World War</a>, the
<a href="Whiteway_Colony" class="wikilink" title="Whiteway">Whiteway</a>
<a href="Tolstoyan_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="Tolstoyans">Tolstoyans</a> in Gloucestershire, and the
<a href="Spanish_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a>-era solidarity networks
of the 1930s. From the 1980s a new wave of
<a href="anarcho-punk" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-punk">anarcho-punk</a>, anti-roads protest and squatted
social centres reconnected the city with the older current, eventually
producing institutions such as
<a href="BASE_Bristol" class="wikilink" title="BASE">BASE</a> which
remain active today.

Much of the recovery of this history is owed to Stephen E. Hunt's 2010
<a href="Bristol_Radical_History_Group" class="wikilink"
title="Bristol Radical History Group">Bristol Radical History Group</a>
pamphlet *Anarchism in Bristol and the West Country to 1950*,[^1] and to
Samson Bryher's earlier *Account of the Labour and Socialist Movement in
Bristol* (1929),[^2] which remains a foundational source on the period.

## History

### Forerunners: West Country radicalism

The earliest claim on an anarchist genealogy in the region rests on the
Bristol
<a href="Member_of_Parliament" class="wikilink" title="MP">MP</a>
<a href="Edmund_Burke" class="wikilink" title="Edmund Burke">Edmund
Burke</a>, whose 1756 tract *A Vindication of Natural Society; or, A
View of the Miseries and Evils Arising to Mankind from Every Species of
Artificial Society* argued that the movement from natural society to
political society had produced "the dungeons, whips, chains, racks,
gibbets, with which every society is abundantly stored", supporting "a
dozen or two in pride and madness, and millions in abject
servitude".[^3] Burke was born in Dublin and only later became member
for Bristol, and the tract was almost certainly written as a satire of
the <a href="Deism" class="wikilink" title="deistic">deistic</a>
arguments of
<a href="Henry_St_John,_1st_Viscount_Bolingbroke" class="wikilink"
title="Bolingbroke">Bolingbroke</a> — yet it was read by
<a href="William_Godwin" class="wikilink" title="William Godwin">William
Godwin</a> as a serious case against the state, and helped to inspire
*<a href="An_Enquiry_Concerning_Political_Justice" class="wikilink"
title="An Enquiry Concerning Political Justice">An Enquiry Concerning
Political Justice</a>* (1793), the first detailed theoretical statement
of anarchism in the modern sense.[^4]

Godwin himself visited the West Country to call on
<a href="Percy_Bysshe_Shelley" class="wikilink"
title="Percy Bysshe Shelley">Percy Bysshe Shelley</a>, and his ideas
were taken up locally by
<a href="Samuel_Taylor_Coleridge" class="wikilink"
title="Samuel Taylor Coleridge">Samuel Taylor Coleridge</a> and
<a href="Robert_Southey" class="wikilink" title="Robert Southey">Robert
Southey</a>, who in their radical youth lectured on Godwinian themes at
the Corn Market and on College Green in Bristol.
<a href="Mary_Wollstonecraft" class="wikilink"
title="Mary Wollstonecraft">Mary Wollstonecraft</a>, later Godwin's
partner and author of
*<a href="A_Vindication_of_the_Rights_of_Woman" class="wikilink"
title="A Vindication of the Rights of Woman">A Vindication of the Rights
of Woman</a>*, spent time in Bristol, and her daughter
<a href="Mary_Shelley" class="wikilink" title="Mary Shelley">Mary
Shelley</a> grew up in this same milieu. The portrait painter
<a href="Thomas_Lawrence_(painter)" class="wikilink"
title="Sir Thomas Lawrence">Sir Thomas Lawrence</a>, whose chalk sketch
of Godwin is one of the best-known images of him, was a son of the
innkeeper of the Bear at Devizes and worked extensively in Bath.[^5]

Anarchism in the positive sense — the word reclaimed by
<a href="Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon" class="wikilink"
title="Pierre-Joseph Proudhon">Pierre-Joseph Proudhon</a> in the 1840s
under the slogan "anarchy is order"[^6] — did not arrive as a distinct
current until much later. By then the West Country had developed a rich
seam of plebeian radicalism, from the
<a href="Henry_Hunt_(politician)" class="wikilink"
title="&quot;Orator&quot; Hunt">"Orator" Hunt</a> of mass-platform
agitation to the Bristol
<a href="Chartism" class="wikilink" title="Chartists">Chartists</a>, the
<a href="freethought" class="wikilink"
title="freethinkers">freethinkers</a> and the temperance reformers, all
of whom contributed to the soil in which an explicitly anarchist
sensibility would grow.

### The Bristol Socialist Society and Edward Carpenter

The decisive shift came in the 1880s, when anarchism emerged as a
recognisable strand within <a href="British_socialism" class="wikilink"
title="British socialism">British socialism</a>. Three currents
coexisted nationally:
<a href="William_Morris" class="wikilink" title="William Morris">William
Morris</a>'s libertarian-leaning
<a href="Socialist_League_(UK,_1885)" class="wikilink"
title="Socialist League">Socialist League</a>; the gradualist
<a href="Fabian_Society" class="wikilink" title="Fabian Society">Fabian
Society</a> (which expelled its anarchists in 1886); and the more
orthodox <a href="Marxism" class="wikilink" title="Marxism">Marxism</a>
of <a href="H._M._Hyndman" class="wikilink" title="H. M. Hyndman">H. M.
Hyndman</a>'s <a href="Social_Democratic_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Social Democratic Federation">Social Democratic Federation</a>.[^7][^8]
In Bristol these factions overlapped within a single local organisation,
the <a href="Bristol_Socialist_Society" class="wikilink"
title="Bristol Socialist Society">Bristol Socialist Society</a>, founded
in 1885 as a branch of the SDF but operating independently from 1886
until at least 1914.[^9]

The Sheffield-based writer <a href="Edward_Carpenter" class="wikilink"
title="Edward Carpenter">Edward Carpenter</a> became the pivotal link
between the metropolitan socialist scene and the Bristol circle.
Carpenter favoured workers' control over nationalisation and looked
forward to "a free communal society as the finest socialist objective",
and he was a frequent contributor to
<a href="Freedom_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom"><em>Freedom</em></a>, the anarchist paper started by
<a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Kropotkin">Peter
Kropotkin</a> and <a href="Charlotte_Wilson" class="wikilink"
title="Charlotte Wilson">Charlotte Wilson</a> in 1886.[^10] Carpenter
met the Bristol brothers Robert and John Sharland — part of a musical,
working-class family central to the Bristol Socialist Society — and from
the mid-1880s onwards travelled regularly to the city, hopping between
London, Sheffield and Bristol for the rest of his life. In an obituary
written for Carpenter in 1929, Robert Sharland recalled that "his
teaching savoured more of anarchist communism, and that is akin to the
ideal of many of us".[^11][^12]

Carpenter was a cheerful target of
<a href="George_Orwell" class="wikilink" title="George Orwell">George
Orwell</a>'s well-known jibe in
*<a href="The_Road_to_Wigan_Pier" class="wikilink"
title="The Road to Wigan Pier">The Road to Wigan Pier</a>* that
socialism drew "every fruit-juice drinker, nudist, sandal-wearer,
sex-maniac, Quaker, nature-cure quack, pacifist, and feminist in
England".[^13]
<a href="E._F._Schumacher" class="wikilink" title="E. F. Schumacher">E.
F. Schumacher</a>'s later defence of cranks as "tiny devices that create
great revolutions" might serve as the proper riposte.

Through Carpenter the Bristol comrades met or hosted most of the leading
figures of the period.
<a href="William_Morris" class="wikilink" title="William Morris">William
Morris</a> himself came down to speak on "Art and Labour" at the Bristol
City Museum lecture theatre in 1885, outlining a socialist society of
"universal co-operation in place of competition or universal war".[^14]
Kropotkin spoke at St James' Hall in Cumberland Street, near Portland
Square, in 1889 and again the following year; he later cited the
<a href="Radstock" class="wikilink" title="Radstock">Radstock</a>
miners' gift of cabbages and potatoes to 400 striking Bristol miners as
an example of working-class solidarity in
*<a href="Mutual_Aid:_A_Factor_of_Evolution" class="wikilink"
title="Mutual Aid">Mutual Aid</a>*.[^15] The Bristol Socialist Sunday
Society regularly drew audiences of up to 1,700 working people to its
Old Market meetings, and continued to operate well into the 1920s.[^16]

### Helena Born, Miriam Daniell and the 1889–90 strike wave

Two women central to the Bristol scene of the late 1880s were
<a href="Helena_Born" class="wikilink" title="Helena Born">Helena
Born</a> and
<a href="Miriam_Daniell" class="wikilink" title="Miriam Daniell">Miriam
Daniell</a>. Born into comfortable Clifton families and originally
active in the Bristol Women's Liberal Association, they broke with their
class background, moved into a small house at 9 Louisa Street in St
Philip's to live in solidarity with their working-class neighbours, and
threw themselves into industrial organising.[^17][^18] Daniell became
treasurer of the Bristol Strike Committee during the
<a href="Bristol_strike_wave_of_1889–90" class="wikilink"
title="labour revolt of 1889–90">labour revolt of 1889–90</a>, which
paralleled the great
<a href="London_Dock_Strike_of_1889" class="wikilink"
title="London dock strike">London dock strike</a>; Born served as its
secretary, and the two together were honorary secretaries of the Bristol
Tailoresses' branch of the National Union of Gasworkers and General
Labourers.[^19][^20]

Alongside Born and Daniell the strike committee included a remarkable
group of activists — the Sharland brothers (Robert, Will, John and Tom),
<a href="Robert_Allan_Nicol" class="wikilink"
title="Robert Allan Nicol">Robert Allan Nicol</a>,
<a href="Hugh_Holmes_Gore" class="wikilink"
title="Hugh Holmes Gore">Hugh Holmes Gore</a>,
<a href="Enid_Stacy" class="wikilink" title="Enid Stacy">Enid Stacy</a>,
<a href="Katherine_St_John_Conway" class="wikilink"
title="Katherine St John Conway">Katherine St John Conway</a>, the
novelist
<a href="Gertrude_Dix" class="wikilink" title="Gertrude Dix">Gertrude
Dix</a>, and many others.[^21] The Bristol struggles were supported by
visiting organisers from London, including
<a href="Ben_Tillett" class="wikilink" title="Ben Tillett">Ben
Tillett</a> (himself a Bristol-born docker),
<a href="Tom_Mann" class="wikilink" title="Tom Mann">Tom Mann</a> and
<a href="Will_Thorne" class="wikilink" title="Will Thorne">Will
Thorne</a>.

In 1890 Born, Daniell and Nicol — by then in a "free union" relationship
— emigrated to <a href="Cambridge,_Massachusetts" class="wikilink"
title="Cambridge, Massachusetts">Cambridge, Massachusetts</a>, partly to
escape local hostility to Daniell's unmarried pregnancy and frustration
with the limits of British industrial organising. There they fell in
with the Boston anarchist milieu around
<a href="Benjamin_Tucker" class="wikilink"
title="Benjamin Tucker">Benjamin Tucker</a>'s journal
*<a href="Liberty_(1881–1908)" class="wikilink"
title="Liberty">Liberty</a>*, taught themselves typesetting, and
continued to publish poetry, polemic and proto-feminist
commentary.[^22][^23] Daniell died in California in 1894; Born returned
to Massachusetts, formed a partnership with the Belfast-born
basket-maker William Bailie, and died of cancer in 1901. Her biographer
recorded that "to the end of her life Helena accepted the name of
socialist taking the term in its widest sense, but socialism as it
worked through customary channels had less and less of her sympathy, and
after years she grew into the principles of philosophical
anarchism".[^24]

Gertrude Dix, a Bristol novelist who moved in the same circles, drew
directly on the local socialist and anarchist scene for her 1900 novel
*The Image Breakers*, set in a thinly fictionalised "Burster" and
containing a strike scene closely modelled on the 1892 dispute at
Sanders' sweet factory in Redcliffe.[^25]

Hugh Holmes Gore, a Clifton solicitor and Anglo-Catholic convert to
socialism, linked the Christian socialist milieu with the Bristol
Socialist Society and became known as the "people's solicitor". In 1893
he gained a national reputation defending the
<a href="Walsall_Anarchists" class="wikilink"
title="Walsall Anarchists">Walsall Anarchists</a> — a group of mostly
foreign-born anarchists prosecuted on the basis of a bomb plot
orchestrated by the police agent Auguste Coulon. Edward Carpenter, who
had known one of the accused (Fred Charles) in Sheffield, raised a
defence fund through *Freedom* and engaged Gore as solicitor.[^26][^27]
Gore disappeared from public life in 1898 in circumstances his
contemporaries hinted at as a sexual scandal, at a time when
homosexuality was criminalised.[^28]

### Songs, the Clarion and the "larger socialism"

Carpenter spoke of a "larger socialism" reaching beyond industrial
struggle to art, music, literature and what he called the texture of a
new way of life. With the Sharlands he produced *Chants of Labour*
(1888), the first English-language collection of socialist songs, with
cover art by the Bristol artist Annie Cannon.
<a href="Oscar_Wilde" class="wikilink" title="Oscar Wilde">Oscar
Wilde</a> reviewed it warmly in his essay "The Poets and the People",
observing that "should the revolution ever break out in England we
should have no inarticulate roar, but rather pleasant glees and graceful
part-songs".[^29]

The same impulse fed into the
<a href="Clarion_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Clarion"><em>Clarion</em></a> movement of the 1890s, which sought
"to promote socialism by being social". Although founded in
<a href="Manchester" class="wikilink" title="Manchester">Manchester</a>
by <a href="Robert_Blatchford" class="wikilink"
title="Robert Blatchford">Robert Blatchford</a>, the Clarion's network
of cycling clubs, rambling clubs, drama clubs and handicraft groups had
strong roots in Bristol. The Bristol Clarion Cycling Club drew its
objects from "Mutual Aid, Good Fellowship, and the Propagation of
Socialism as advocated by *The Clarion*", carrying the influence of
Morris and Kropotkin into a mass cultural movement. Bryher recalled that
the cyclists "frequently 100 or more members and friends with their
wives and children attended" countryside outings on which "it was the
practice ... to quote such lines as *The paradise of the rich is a hell
of the poor* and *Socialism means peace, competition means war* on all
its posters and handbills, and these were cut off for the purpose
vandalism perhaps, but the object was to save human bodies and minds
that were being sacrificed daily to the gods of Mammon".[^30] Women's
Clarion vans travelled out from Bristol, one of them being attacked by
police at <a href="Thornbury,_Gloucestershire" class="wikilink"
title="Thornbury">Thornbury</a> amid mass arrests. The national Clarion
Cycling Club remains in existence, with a branch in Dorset though no
longer in Bristol itself.[^31]

### Whiteway Colony

In 1898 a group of
<a href="Tolstoyans" class="wikilink" title="Tolstoyans">Tolstoyans</a>
who had broken away from the Purleigh community in Essex purchased forty
acres of Cotswold land near
<a href="Miserden" class="wikilink" title="Miserden">Miserden</a>,
north-east of Stroud, and founded the
<a href="Whiteway_Colony" class="wikilink"
title="Whiteway Colony">Whiteway Colony</a>. The colonists ceremonially
burned the title deeds of the land on the end of a pitchfork to
symbolise the rejection of private ownership, an act later contested in
court that confirmed nobody held legal title to the site.[^32][^33]
Vegetarianism was obligatory and nudism optional; founding members
<a href="Nellie_Shaw" class="wikilink" title="Nellie Shaw">Nellie
Shaw</a> and Francis Sedlák, along with the Quaker journalist Samuel
Veale Bracher, set the tone.[^34] Early members Aylmer and Louise Maude
were friends of
<a href="Leo_Tolstoy" class="wikilink" title="Leo Tolstoy">Leo
Tolstoy</a> and translators of his work.

Whiteway hosted Russian, Czech and Dutch immigrant anarchists,
<a href="conscientious_objectors" class="wikilink"
title="conscientious objectors">conscientious objectors</a> of two world
wars and refugees from the Spanish Civil War, alongside cooperative
ventures including Protheroe's Bakery, the Cotswold Co-operative
Handicraft Guild and a co-operative gardening group.[^35] Between 1928
and 1932 the colony became a temporary base for
<a href="Freedom_Press" class="wikilink" title="Freedom Press">Freedom
Press</a> itself: long-serving *Freedom* editor Tom Keell and his
partner
<a href="Lilian_Wolfe" class="wikilink" title="Lilian Wolfe">Lilian
Wolfe</a> produced fifteen issues of the paper from Whiteway during one
of the British movement's leanest periods.[^36][^37] Wolfe was also
involved with the Sunshine Stores, the colony's wholefood outlet in the
centre of <a href="Stroud" class="wikilink" title="Stroud">Stroud</a> —
a building still in use, where Dennis Gould would in later decades sell
anarchist pamphlets from a stall outside. The colony continues today,
although its explicit anarchist character has been diluted; communal
facilities remain in common use and governance is still by a residents'
meeting.[^38]

### George Barrett and the syndicalist years

The years before the
<a href="First_World_War" class="wikilink" title="First World War">First
World War</a> saw a wave of <a href="syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="syndicalist">syndicalist</a> new unionism affect the West
Country. The outstanding figure to emerge from Bristol in this period
was <a href="George_Barrett_(anarchist)" class="wikilink"
title="George Barrett">George Barrett</a> — born George Powell Ballard
in <a href="Ledbury" class="wikilink" title="Ledbury">Ledbury</a> in
1888. Initially a member of the Bristol Socialist Society, Barrett's
opposition to parliamentarianism turned him towards anarchism, and in
February 1908 *Freedom* reported under the headline "Anarchism in
Bristol" that Ballard had "the heavy responsibility of having disturbed
the otherwise peaceful routine of that highly successful society" by
lecturing on "Anarchy and Socialism".[^39] He married Edith Oxley,
daughter of a Bristol socialist, before moving to London and then to
Glasgow.

Although Barrett left Bristol he retained close ties with West Country
comrades, and considered the anarchist groups of Bristol and South Wales
to be among the most active in Britain.[^40] His pamphlet *The Last War*
(1915), published by the Bristol-based Workers' Freedom Group at 19 The
Haymarket, attacked the war as a conflict fought to settle the quarrels
of capital while real war "is fought to take over the mines, railways,
factories, and fields"; it sold 10,000 copies before being suppressed by
the government.[^41][^42] Barrett died of tuberculosis in 1917 at the
age of twenty-nine. *The Anarchist Revolution* (1915) and the
posthumously published *Objections to Anarchism* (1921) remained widely
read;
<a href="Colin_Ward" class="wikilink" title="Colin Ward">Colin Ward</a>
later recalled the latter as "one of the first, and best, anarchist
pamphlets I ever read".[^43] Barrett's reflection that "millions are on
the verge of starvation, thousands hundreds of thousands are spending
their lives in producing instruments for the destruction of human life,
and millions are wasting their existence in a dull tragedy of monotony"
remains as biting today as it was a century ago.[^44]

### Emma Goldman and the Spanish Civil War

<a href="Emma_Goldman" class="wikilink" title="Emma Goldman">Emma
Goldman</a> visited the West Country twice. In 1925, exiled from both
the
<a href="United_States" class="wikilink" title="United States">United
States</a> and
<a href="Soviet_Russia" class="wikilink" title="Soviet Russia">Soviet
Russia</a>, she spoke at the
<a href="YMCA" class="wikilink" title="YMCA">YMCA</a> in Bristol on
"Labour under the Dictatorship in Russia" and on "Heroic Women of the
Russian Revolution" at the Folk House in Park Street, while staying in
<a href="Redland,_Bristol" class="wikilink" title="Redland">Redland</a>.
She found Bristol audiences thin and the climate dispiriting — "the fogs
and wet remained faithful and wandered through my system at their own
sweet will" — and was soon laid up with chills before moving on to
friends in <a href="Nice" class="wikilink" title="Nice">Nice</a>.[^45] A
highlight of the trip was a visit to the aged Edward Carpenter, "the
great libertarian", whom she persuaded to write an introduction to her
companion <a href="Alexander_Berkman" class="wikilink"
title="Alexander Berkman">Alexander Berkman</a>'s prison memoirs.
Through her friend the novelist
<a href="John_Cowper_Powys" class="wikilink"
title="John Cowper Powys">John Cowper Powys</a> — long associated with
the West Country and Dorset — she found a circle of literary
sympathisers; Powys praised her as one of "the great names of history
along with Kropotkin, Bakunin and Tolstoy".[^46]

Goldman returned in 1937 on a speaking tour for the
<a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a>, agitating in support
of the Spanish anarchists of the
<a href="CNT-FAI" class="wikilink" title="CNT-FAI">CNT-FAI</a> alongside
the Irish anarchist
<a href="Ethel_Mannin" class="wikilink" title="Ethel Mannin">Ethel
Mannin</a> on behalf of the
<a href="International_Anti-Fascist_Solidarity" class="wikilink"
title="International Anti-Fascist Solidarity">International Anti-Fascist
Solidarity</a> campaign. By her own account she travelled around Britain
at this point "followed by a brace of detectives from Scotland
Yard".[^47] South Bristol — particularly
<a href="Bedminster" class="wikilink" title="Bedminster">Bedminster</a>
— was already a centre of working-class organisation that sent
volunteers to fight with the
<a href="International_Brigades" class="wikilink"
title="International Brigades">International Brigades</a> in Spain.[^48]

### The Second World War and after

During the <a href="Second_World_War" class="wikilink"
title="Second World War">Second World War</a> *Freedom* renamed itself
*War Commentary* and continued an anti-militarist line. In 1945 three of
its editors — <a href="Vernon_Richards" class="wikilink"
title="Vernon Richards">Vernon Richards</a>,
<a href="Philip_Sansom" class="wikilink" title="Philip Sansom">Philip
Sansom</a> and John Hewetson — were imprisoned for allegedly spreading
disaffection among the armed forces. The
<a href="Freedom_Defence_Committee" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom Defence Committee">Freedom Defence Committee</a>
established a Bristol branch in support, and the Freedom Press bookshop
relocated briefly to Cheltenham Road in Bristol, only a few streets from
the venues of the city's later anarchist book fairs.[^49][^50]

The post-war decades were leaner for explicit anarchism in the city,
although the underlying cultural networks persisted. By the 1980s a
fresh wave of radicalisation arrived through
<a href="anarcho-punk" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-punk">anarcho-punk</a> —
<a href="Crass" class="wikilink" title="Crass">Crass</a>,
<a href="Poison_Girls" class="wikilink" title="Poison Girls">Poison
Girls</a>, <a href="Flux_of_Pink_Indians" class="wikilink"
title="Flux of Pink Indians">Flux of Pink Indians</a>,
<a href="Conflict_(band)" class="wikilink" title="Conflict">Conflict</a>,
and the local band <a href="Culture_Shock_(band)" class="wikilink"
title="Culture Shock">Culture Shock</a> — alongside the writings of
<a href="Colin_Ward" class="wikilink" title="Colin Ward">Colin Ward</a>
(*<a href="Anarchy_in_Action" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchy in Action">Anarchy in Action</a>*),
<a href="George_Orwell" class="wikilink" title="George Orwell">George
Orwell</a>'s *<a href="Homage_to_Catalonia" class="wikilink"
title="Homage to Catalonia">Homage to Catalonia</a>* and
<a href="George_Woodcock" class="wikilink"
title="George Woodcock">George Woodcock</a>'s *Anarchism: A
History*.[^51] *Freedom* and the magazine *Green Line* circulated in the
West Country counterculture. The defeat of the 1984–85
<a href="UK_miners&#39;_strike_(1984–1985)" class="wikilink"
title="miners&#39; strike">miners' strike</a>, the suppression of the
last <a href="Stonehenge_Free_Festival" class="wikilink"
title="Stonehenge Free Festival">Stonehenge Free Festival</a> in 1984,
the <a href="anti-nuclear_movement" class="wikilink"
title="anti-nuclear movement">anti-nuclear movement</a> and the
<a href="hunt_sabotage" class="wikilink" title="hunt sabotage">hunt
sabotage</a> and <a href="animal_liberation" class="wikilink"
title="animal liberation">animal liberation</a> networks all contributed
to a renewed grassroots militancy in the region.

The 1990s carried this further. The anti-roads movement, with its
set-piece confrontations at
<a href="Solsbury_Hill" class="wikilink" title="Solsbury Hill">Solsbury
Hill</a> outside
<a href="Bath,_Somerset" class="wikilink" title="Bath">Bath</a> and the
Batheaston bypass, drew anarchists into
<a href="direct_action" class="wikilink" title="direct action">direct
action</a> and prefigurative protest camps; international solidarity
campaigns, <a href="Reclaim_the_Streets" class="wikilink"
title="Reclaim the Streets">Reclaim the Streets</a>, the
<a href="Zapatista" class="wikilink" title="Zapatista">Zapatista</a>
support networks and the early
<a href="anti-globalisation_movement" class="wikilink"
title="anti-globalisation movement">anti-globalisation movement</a> gave
Bristol a distinctive radical infrastructure of cooperative cafes,
infoshops and squatted social centres.[^52] The most enduring of these
is <a href="BASE_Bristol" class="wikilink" title="BASE">BASE</a>
(originally Kebele) in
<a href="Easton,_Bristol" class="wikilink" title="Easton">Easton</a>,
opened by squatters in 1995 at 14 Robertson Road and still operating
thirty years later as a volunteer-run social centre, library and
cafe.[^53] The <a href="Bristol_Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Bristol Anarchist Federation">Bristol Anarchist Federation</a>
formed in the wake of the 2009 Bristol Co-Mutiny as a local affiliate of
the national <a href="Anarchist_Federation_(Britain)" class="wikilink"
title="Anarchist Federation">Anarchist Federation</a> founded in
1986.[^54]

A representative institution of this newer wave was
**<a href="Hydra_Books" class="wikilink" title="Hydra Books">Hydra
Books</a>**, a workers' co-operative bookshop, café and meeting space
that grew directly out of the Bristol Radical History Group and related
networks. Hydra opened at 34 Old Market Street in November 2011,
stocking radical politics, history, feminism, animal rights and
environmental titles alongside Zapatista coffee, and hosting meetings of
the Bristol <a href="Industrial_Workers_of_the_World" class="wikilink"
title="IWW">IWW</a>, the
<a href="Bristol_Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Bristol Anarchist Federation">Bristol Anarchist Federation</a>
and many other groups.[^55][^56] The Old Market premises was lost to
redevelopment for luxury flats in June 2019, and Hydra reopened in a
"nomadic" form downstairs at Cafe Kino on Stokes Croft.[^57] On May Day
2024, radical workers in Bristol reoccupied the empty Old Market
shopfront, reopening Hydra's shutters for the first time in four years
in an act explicitly framed as resistance to gentrification.[^58]

## Timeline

- **1756** — Edmund Burke publishes *A Vindication of Natural Society*
  (later Bristol MP)
- **1790s** — William Godwin visits Bristol; Coleridge and Southey
  lecture on Godwinian themes at the Corn Market and College Green
- **1885** — Bristol Socialist Society founded; William Morris speaks on
  "Art and Labour" at Bristol City Museum
- **1885 onwards** — Edward Carpenter begins regular visits to Bristol,
  meeting the Sharland brothers
- **1888** — *Chants of Labour*, co-produced with the Sharlands,
  published; reviewed by Oscar Wilde
- **1889** — Peter Kropotkin speaks at St James' Hall, Cumberland Street
- **1889–90** — Bristol labour revolt; Helena Born and Miriam Daniell on
  the strike committee; Ben Tillett, Tom Mann and Will Thorne visit in
  support
- **1890** — Kropotkin returns to Bristol; Born, Daniell and Nicol
  emigrate to Massachusetts
- **1893** — Hugh Holmes Gore defends the Walsall Anarchists, with funds
  raised by Carpenter
- **1898** — Whiteway Colony founded near Miserden by Tolstoyan
  breakaways from Purleigh
- **1908** — George Barrett (Ballard) lectures on "Anarchy and
  Socialism", disturbing the Bristol Socialist Society
- **1913** — Barrett speaks in Bristol; the Workers' Freedom Group
  operates from 19 The Haymarket
- **1915** — Barrett's *The Last War* published in Bristol; suppressed
  after 10,000 copies sold
- **1925** — Emma Goldman speaks at the Bristol YMCA and the Folk House,
  Park Street; visits Carpenter
- **1928–32** — Tom Keell and Lilian Wolfe produce *Freedom* from
  Whiteway Colony
- **1937** — Goldman returns on a Spanish Revolution speaking tour with
  Ethel Mannin
- **1944–45** — Freedom Press bookshop relocates to Cheltenham Road,
  Bristol; Bristol branch of Freedom Defence Committee formed after the
  *War Commentary* trial
- **1995** - BASE (originally Kebele) Anarchist social centre in Easton,
  opened by squatters
- **2009** - Bristol Anarchist Federation is formed
- **2011** - Hydra Anarchist bookstore opens

## Characteristics

### Positives

What is striking about the Bristol and West Country tradition is its
continuity across very different idioms — from Godwinian dissent through
Carpenterian "larger socialism" to the Tolstoyan land experiments and
the squatted social centres of the late twentieth century. Unlike the
more sectarian metropolitan scenes, local organisations such as the
Bristol Socialist Society tended to combine anarchist,
libertarian-socialist and parliamentary tendencies in a single working
alliance, with Carpenter and the Sharlands moving easily between groups.
The same spirit of "unity and diversity" carried into the Clarion
movement, into Whiteway's open-membership communalism, and into the
broad coalitions of the 1990s anti-roads and anti-globalisation years.

The region also produced or hosted a remarkable concentration of women
radicals — Helena Born, Miriam Daniell, Enid Stacy, Katherine St John
Conway, Gertrude Dix, Nellie Shaw, Lilian Wolfe, Emma Goldman and Ethel
Mannin — for whom anarchism, feminism and the search for new ways of
living were inseparable.[^59] The Whiteway Colony's century-plus
survival is itself a rare instance in
<a href="Britain" class="wikilink" title="Britain">Britain</a> of an
anarchist-founded community persisting into the present.

### Negatives

Steve Hunt has acknowledged that whether one can speak of a continuous
local *movement* as distinct from a tradition is open to question. The
1920s, when *Freedom* was reduced to a small newsletter run from a
Cotswold colony, represented a long ebb; the post-war decades were
similarly thin. Even Bristol's own anarchist history is fragile: at the
third <a href="Bristol_Anarchist_Bookfair" class="wikilink"
title="Bristol Anarchist Bookfair">Bristol Anarchist Bookfair</a> in
2010 the organisers initially repeated the local belief that "this is
the first anarchist book fair to happen in Bristol for fifteen years",
before realising that nobody present could actually recall the supposed
earlier bookfair — an instance of how easily even recent radical memory
is lost.[^60]

Police harassment has been a recurrent feature. The 1893 Walsall trial
was a textbook case of
<a href="entrapment" class="wikilink" title="entrapment">entrapment</a>
by an <a href="agent_provocateur" class="wikilink"
title="agent provocateur">agent provocateur</a>; Goldman's 1937 tour was
shadowed by Scotland Yard; the
<a href="Whiteway_Colony" class="wikilink"
title="Whiteway Colony">Whiteway Colony</a> was infiltrated by the
<a href="Home_Office" class="wikilink" title="Home Office">Home
Office</a> in the 1920s as a suspected nest of revolutionaries; the 1945
*War Commentary* editors were jailed; and from the early 2000s the
<a href="Undercover_policing_relationships_scandal" class="wikilink"
title="undercover policing">undercover policing</a> of British
anti-capitalist movements extended into Bristol's social centres, with
<a href="BASE_Bristol" class="wikilink" title="BASE">BASE</a> volunteers
surveilled by "spycops" and, more recently, named in operations
connected to the prosecution of
<a href="Toby_Shone" class="wikilink" title="Toby Shone">Toby Shone</a>.[^61]
The history also reflects the broader marginalisation of West Country
and English anarchism within the wider literature: as Hunt observes,
English anarchism has never been as well documented as the more dramatic
continental episodes, and even John Quail's *Slow Burning Fuse* (1978),
the standard history, has long been out of print.[^62][^63]
