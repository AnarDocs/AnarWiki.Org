## Overview

Whiteway Colony is a residential community in the
<a href="Cotswolds" class="wikilink" title="Cotswolds">Cotswolds</a>,
near <a href="Stroud" class="wikilink" title="Stroud">Stroud</a> in
Gloucestershire, founded in 1898 by
<a href="Tolstoyan" class="wikilink" title="Tolstoyan">Tolstoyans</a>
who sought to live free of <a href="private_property" class="wikilink"
title="private property">private property</a>, wage labour and state
authority. Over more than a century the colony has hosted
<a href="anarchist" class="wikilink" title="anarchist">anarchists</a>,
<a href="conscientious_objector" class="wikilink"
title="conscientious objector">conscientious objectors</a>, refugees
from the <a href="Spanish_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a> and a succession of
co-operative ventures, and although much of its original communalism has
loosened over time, the land remains held in common and its affairs are
still managed by a general meeting of residents.[^1][^2]

## History

The colony was established in 1898 by Samuel Veale Bracher, a
<a href="Quaker" class="wikilink" title="Quaker">Quaker</a> journalist,
together with a small group of fellow Tolstoyans drawn from an earlier
and short-lived community at Purleigh in Essex. Bracher purchased
forty-one acres of Cotswold land along with seeds, tools and provisions,
and the founding settlers marked their break with the existing order by
burning the property deeds on the end of a pitchfork, refusing to
recognise the legitimacy of titles to the earth.[^3][^4]
<a href="Aylmer_Maude" class="wikilink" title="Aylmer Maude">Aylmer
Maude</a>, Tolstoy's English translator and friend, led the founding
board of trustees, and recalled that the story which had drawn the
settlers together was Tolstoy's *Walk in the Light While There is
Light*.[^5]

The earliest period at Whiteway was a strict experiment in voluntary
poverty and shared life. The most committed colonists rejected the use
of money altogether, rose with the sun to save matches, made fire by
striking bricks, ate raw wheat from the hollow of the hand and went
without bread, salt or sugar.[^6] Provisions were pooled and labour
shared. This austere phase did not last. Bracher and several of the
other founders left within a few years, frustrated by what they regarded
as the idleness of some of their companions, and the community moved
away from full communism of goods toward what its members called
individual living, in which each household worked its own plot and built
its own dwelling.[^7][^8]

The land itself, however, was never re-enclosed. A court ruling in 1955
confirmed that no individual held legal title to the site, and the soil
has remained the common possession of the colony ever since.[^9] Houses,
by contrast, came to be privately built and, eventually, privately sold;
because the land beneath them could not be mortgaged, the early
dwellings were of necessity self-built, ranging from William Sinclair's
hand-made brick and thatch hut to timber clapboard cottages. Piped water
did not reach the colony until 1949 and electricity not until Christmas
1954.[^10][^11]

Over the decades the colony absorbed successive waves of newcomers.
Immigrant anarchists arrived from across Europe; conscientious objectors
took refuge there during both world wars; refugees from the
<a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a> settled among the
existing households; and for a period the anarchist newspaper
*<a href="Freedom_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom">Freedom</a>* was produced at Whiteway by
<a href="Thomas_Keell" class="wikilink" title="Thomas Keell">Thomas
Keell</a>.[^12][^13] A communal hall was raised by the residents in 1924
to house meetings, social gatherings and the Whiteway Modern School, and
a swimming pool was built in 1969.[^14]

The British state did not view the experiment kindly. Home Office papers
released to the Public Record Office in 1999 revealed that officials in
the 1920s had regarded Whiteway as a security risk and had paid a
married couple four hundred pounds to infiltrate the colony in search of
evidence of "promiscuous fornication". The agents reported back that
such behaviour did indeed occur but could produce no proof, and the Home
Office's hope of stirring up popular agitation came to nothing, since
local people regarded the colonists as cranks rather than as threats.
One official complained in 1925 that the inhabitants had no manners and
that their customs were beastly.[^15]

## Characteristics

Whiteway today sits on the same forty acres it occupied in 1898, though
the open fields once known as the Dry Ground and the Wet Ground have
given way to a patchwork of gardens, allotments and mature woodland
holding more than sixty homes and around a hundred and twenty
colonists.[^16] The lanes wind without regard for the motor car, the
houses sit at irregular angles to one another, and the architecture
ranges from early twentieth-century self-build to modern brick
bungalows. Communal facilities — the hall, the playing field, the
swimming pool, the lanes themselves — are still maintained collectively
and administered through a general meeting of residents that has
continued without interruption since the colony's founding.[^17]

Many of the experimental practices of the early years, once considered
shocking, have since either faded or been quietly absorbed by the wider
culture. The colony's early enthusiasms included vegetarianism, rational
dress, <a href="naturism" class="wikilink" title="naturism">naturism</a>
and <a href="free_love" class="wikilink" title="free love">free love</a>
unions, and although Whiteway is no longer a
<a href="free_love" class="wikilink" title="free love">free love</a>
commune in any organised sense, its tolerance of varied domestic
arrangements is now unremarkable. Among the co-operative ventures that
have flourished at one time or another are Protheroe's Bakery (recently
reopened), the Cotswold Co-operative Handicraft Guild, the Co-operative
Gardening Group, Lillian Woolf's wholefood shop, the Whiteway Youth Club
and the Whiteway Wanderers football team, alongside numerous
craftworkers, artists and smallholders.[^18]

### Positives

The colony's longevity is itself a substantive achievement: it has
outlasted nearly every other anarchist settlement of its generation, and
has done so without abandoning the common ownership of the land or the
practice of governing common affairs through the assembly of those
affected by them. Several generations of colonists, including
descendants of the original settlers, have grown up in a place where the
lanes belong to no one, where building one's own home is unremarkable,
and where decisions about the shared life of the community are taken in
common. The continuity of <a href="Freedom_(newspaper)" class="wikilink"
title="Freedom">Freedom</a> press work, the sheltering of Spanish
refugees, and the steady stream of conscientious objectors who passed
through during both world wars all attest to a tradition of practical
solidarity with the wider anarchist and pacifist movement.[^19][^20]

### Negatives

The colony has often been read, including by
<a href="Mohandas_Gandhi" class="wikilink"
title="Mohandas Gandhi">Mohandas Gandhi</a> who visited in 1909, as a
Tolstoyan experiment that failed in its more rigorous aspirations,
having moved from full communality toward individual living within its
first generation.[^21] Critics within the anarchist tradition have noted
that once houses became privately owned and saleable at market value,
the barrier to entry rose sharply: a penniless anarchist could no longer
simply turn up at Whiteway and join, as was possible in 1898.[^22] The
colony historian Joy Thacker, herself sympathetic to the place, has
observed the same shift from intense communality toward increasing
individuality across its century of life. Whether this represents a
failure of the original vision or a sustainable accommodation that has
allowed the colony to endure where stricter communities collapsed is a
question the residents themselves continue to debate.
