# Communal Parenting (Alloparenting)

## Overview

Alloparenting — from the Greek *allos*, meaning "other" — refers to the
care of children by individuals other than their biological parents. It
is the predominant mode of child-rearing across most of human prehistory
and remains widespread in non-Western, non-capitalist societies today.
The nuclear family, with its rigid boundary between parents and everyone
else, is a historical anomaly: a relatively recent arrangement tied to
property, inheritance, and the enclosure of social life under capitalism
and the state.

Where alloparenting flourishes — among grandparents, aunts, uncles,
siblings, friends, and neighbours sharing responsibility for children —
the burden on any individual is reduced, children develop broader webs
of attachment, and care itself becomes a collective rather than
privatised act. This is not a radical proposal. It is how most humans
have raised children for most of human existence.

<a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a>
put the principle plainly: children "do not constitute anyone's
property: they are neither the property of the parents nor even of
society. They belong only to their own future freedom."[^1]
<a href="Emma_Goldman" class="wikilink" title="Emma Goldman">Emma
Goldman</a> drew the logical consequence — child-rearing should aim to
develop "a well-rounded individuality" rather than "a patient work
slave, professional automaton, tax-paying citizen, or righteous
moralist."[^2] Alloparenting is one of the structural conditions that
makes this possible: it disperses the power that any single parent holds
over a child, and embeds children in a web of relationships that no one
adult controls.

## Origins

The biological groundwork for alloparenting was laid by the
primatologist and anthropologist
<a href="Sarah_Blaffer_Hrdy" class="wikilink"
title="Sarah Blaffer Hrdy">Sarah Blaffer Hrdy</a>, whose work *Mothers
and Others* (2009) argued that *Homo sapiens* evolved as a cooperative
breeding species. Unlike great apes, human infants are born profoundly
helpless and remain dependent for years — a developmental pattern that
only makes sense, Hrdy argues, if it co-evolved with reliable
alloparental support. Human mothers, unlike chimpanzee mothers,
routinely allow others to hold, feed, and care for infants. This is not
a cultural preference; it is species-typical behaviour with deep
evolutionary roots.[^3]

The term itself was coined by the biologist Edward O. Wilson in 1975, in
his book *Sociobiology*, as a neutral term to cover what had previously
been described with the gender- and relation-specific language of
"aunting" behaviour.[^4]
<a href="Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Kropotkin">Kropotkin</a>'s
*<a href="Mutual_Aid:_A_Factor_of_Evolution" class="wikilink"
title="Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution">Mutual Aid: A Factor of
Evolution</a>* (1902), though not focused on child-rearing specifically,
provided the broader theoretical frame: cooperation, not competition, is
the organising principle of social life among humans and many other
species. The isolated, self-reliant nuclear family is not the natural
unit of human society — it is a product of enclosure, dispossession, and
the deliberate destruction of communal bonds.

<a href="David_Graeber" class="wikilink" title="David Graeber">David
Graeber</a> and
<a href="David_Wengrow" class="wikilink" title="David Wengrow">David
Wengrow</a>, in *<a href="The_Dawn_of_Everything" class="wikilink"
title="The Dawn of Everything">The Dawn of Everything</a>* (2021),
document the enormous variety of kinship and child-rearing arrangements
across human societies, pushing back against any single model of the
family as natural or universal. Many of the societies they examine
distribute the responsibilities of child care across households, clans,
and communities in ways that bear no resemblance to the Western nuclear
ideal.

Anthropological fieldwork across hunter-gatherer societies — among the
<a href="!Kung_San" class="wikilink" title="!Kung San">!Kung San</a>,
the <a href="Aka_people" class="wikilink" title="Aka">Aka</a> of Central
Africa, the <a href="Hadza" class="wikilink" title="Hadza">Hadza</a> of
Tanzania, and the
<a href="Efé_people" class="wikilink" title="Efé">Efé</a> of the Ituri
Forest in the Democratic Republic of Congo — consistently shows that
infants are held, carried, and tended by multiple adults and older
children throughout the day.[^5] The figure of the isolated mother
solely responsible for her child is simply absent from these societies.

<a href="Silvia_Federici" class="wikilink"
title="Silvia Federici">Silvia Federici</a>, in
*<a href="Caliban_and_the_Witch" class="wikilink"
title="Caliban and the Witch">Caliban and the Witch</a>* (2004), traces
how the enclosures of the early modern period destroyed communal
arrangements and forced care work inward, into the private family, where
it became invisible, unwaged, and feminised. Alloparenting, in this
reading, is not merely a pleasant alternative: reclaiming it is part of
dismantling the social structures that reproduce gendered subordination.

## Tendencies

### Grandmothering

One of the most studied forms of alloparenting is the role of
grandmothers. The "grandmother hypothesis," developed by anthropologist
<a href="Kristen_Hawkes" class="wikilink" title="Kristen Hawkes">Kristen
Hawkes</a> and colleagues, proposes that post-menopausal women in human
populations contributed so substantially to the survival of
grandchildren that this shaped human longevity itself. Grandmothers who
provisioned and cared for grandchildren freed younger mothers to have
more children sooner, driving both longer lifespans and stronger social
bonds across generations.[^6]

### Sibling and peer care

In many societies, older children take substantial responsibility for
younger ones. This is not exploitation — in contexts where it is freely
chosen and age-appropriate, sibling caretaking builds competence,
empathy, and cross-generational solidarity. Among the
<a href="Aka_people" class="wikilink" title="Aka">Aka</a> of the Congo
Basin, fathers are also notable alloparents, carrying and tending
infants at rates unusual by global standards, demonstrating that the
gendered division of care is far from inevitable.

### Allonursing

A specific form of alloparenting, allonursing involves a female
providing milk to offspring not her own. It is rarer than other
alloparental practices but documented across several species and some
human societies. In addition to nourishing the infant, it broadens the
immune compounds a child receives and gives the nurser early maternal
experience.[^7]

### Intentional communities

Contemporary <a href="intentional_communities" class="wikilink"
title="intentional communities">intentional communities</a>,
<a href="kibbutzim" class="wikilink" title="kibbutzim">kibbutzim</a>,
and anarchist collective households have consciously attempted to
distribute child-rearing responsibility beyond the nuclear unit. Early
kibbutz experiments went furthest, with children housed communally and
raised collectively — an experiment that produced its own tensions and
was partly reversed under social pressure, but demonstrated both the
possibility and the genuine challenges of departing from the nuclear
norm.

The philosopher Anca Gheaus has argued that parents hold an unjustified
"monopoly of care" over children, and that some non-parental care should
be mandatory as a matter of children's rights. Where care is distributed
more widely, children are exposed to a broader range of viewpoints, bad
parenting is more readily visible and subject to challenge, and children
develop the capacity for trust and relationship beyond the immediate
family.[^8] This is not an argument for the bureaucratic surveillance of
families by the state — it is an argument for the kind of embedded,
mutual accountability that characterised child-rearing before the
nuclear family was imposed as the norm.

The
<a href="Mujeres_Libres" class="wikilink" title="Mujeres Libres">Mujeres
Libres</a> of the <a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a> understood
child-rearing as integral to the revolutionary project itself. As
<a href="Martha_Ackelsberg" class="wikilink"
title="Martha Ackelsberg">Martha Ackelsberg</a> summarises their
position: "Authoritarian schools (or families), based upon fear,
prepared people to be submissive to an authoritarian government.
Different schools and families would be necessary to prepare people to
live in a society without domination."[^9]

### Family abolition

Alloparenting has found a more structural political articulation in the
tradition of family abolition — a demand with roots in
nineteenth-century socialist feminism and revived in recent years by
writers including
<a href="Sophie_Lewis" class="wikilink" title="Sophie Lewis">Sophie
Lewis</a> and <a href="M.E._O&#39;Brien" class="wikilink"
title="M.E. O&#39;Brien">M.E. O'Brien</a>.[^10] Family abolition does
not mean the destruction of kinship relationships — just as opposing
wage labour is not the same as opposing work. It means the abolition of
the Family as a coercive institution: the property relations that
sustain it, the legal codes that enforce it, and the atomisation it
imposes on care.

As Lee Shevek puts it, the nuclear family is not merely a private
arrangement but a structural block of hierarchy — one the state has a
considerable interest in maintaining, because "the supremacy granted to
parents ensures that they have something at stake in teaching children
to accept and defer to authority at a young age."[^11] The family
teaches hierarchy by living it. Children internalise deference because
deference is what their first years of life demand of them.

Lewis argues that what family abolition actually proposes is an
*expansion* of care — the removal of its privatisation and scarcity, so
that the love families promise becomes available beyond the enclosure of
blood relation. O'Brien, approaching the question from a communisation
perspective shaped by anarchist politics, traces how the family's
function has shifted across the history of capitalism while always
serving to discipline reproductive labour and enforce conformity.[^12]
Both writers point to protest encampments, collective kitchens, and
mutual aid networks as living experiments in the communisation of care —
practical demonstrations that the functions the family performs, badly,
can be performed better by broader communities of accountable
relationship.

Crucially, the survivor's perspective on this question reframes what
alloparenting is actually for. A person who has experienced familial
abuse does not need the village to supplement the family — they need it
to replace it, or to provide the means of escape from it. The statistics
are unambiguous: the family home is the primary site of child abuse, not
its refuge from it.[^13] In Europe, studies consistently find that
between a quarter and forty percent of children experience some form of
abuse or neglect within the family. The ideology that presents the
family as the natural haven of love and safety is precisely what makes
abuse within it so difficult to name, challenge, or escape.

Alloparenting — real, embedded, communal care — dissolves the conditions
that make abuse possible: the isolation of the child as the private
property of its parents, the sanctity of "family business" that
discourages outside intervention, and the absence of adults in a child's
life who are not under the authority of the abuser. When a child has
meaningful relationships with multiple adults in a community of genuine
mutual accountability, the monopoly any one adult holds over that
child's life is broken.

### Children's autonomy

Alloparenting, at its best, is not simply the redistribution of adult
authority over children — it is a structural condition for reducing that
authority. When a child has multiple trusted adults in their life, no
single adult holds a monopoly over their development. This matters
because anarchists have consistently argued that how children are raised
shapes whether they are capable of living in freedom.
<a href="Wilhelm_Reich" class="wikilink" title="Wilhelm Reich">Wilhelm
Reich</a> and
<a href="A.S._Neill" class="wikilink" title="A.S. Neill">A.S. Neill</a>
both argued that authoritarian child-rearing — not nature — produces the
psychological structures of submission and deference to authority.
Alloparenting does not automatically solve this, but the dispersion of
care creates more space for children's individuality to develop on its
own terms.

One sharp consequence follows: parents have no moral right to cut
children off from people they love. When families move, divorce, or fall
out with neighbours, children can lose access to alloparents they have
formed deep bonds with — and are given no say in the matter. This
arbitrary severance is a form of harm that rarely gets named as such.

## Criticism

The case for alloparenting can be romanticised. Not all communal
child-rearing arrangements are liberatory — children in some historical
societies have been subject to harsh collective discipline, rigid
socialisation, or the control of elders acting in authoritarian ways.
The Canadian residential school system, which forcibly removed
Indigenous children from their families and communities over more than a
century, was a murderous demonstration of what state-imposed "communal"
child-rearing actually looks like when stripped of consent and
self-determination.[^14] The question is not simply whether care is
shared, but under what conditions, with what degree of consent and
self-determination for children and carers alike.

Feminist critics have noted that alloparenting in practice often means
the redistribution of childcare labour among women — grandmothers,
aunts, female neighbours — rather than its genuinely collective
assumption. Without a structural challenge to the gendered division of
care, "the village raising the child" can simply extend the unpaid
burden further across a female network, leaving the underlying
patriarchal arrangement intact. O'Brien's argument that true
communisation of care requires the abolition of gendered spheres of
labour, not their redistribution, speaks directly to this.[^15]

There is also the question of attachment. Proposals for communal
child-rearing have sometimes been criticised from within anarchist and
feminist traditions for being adult-centric — designed to free parents
(and particularly mothers) to participate in paid labour, rather than
built around the developmental needs of children themselves. Children
need not their biological parents specifically, but they do need stable,
secure attachment relationships with adults who are genuinely present to
them. Bureaucratic rotations of caregivers, or networks assembled
primarily around adult political commitments, can fail to provide this.
The aim is more adults, more reliably present — not a managed programme
of exposure.

Finally, proposals for family abolition and communal care face the
difficulty that the relationships capable of sustaining genuine mutual
obligation take time to build. As one survivor of familial abuse has
written, such networks are "always fragile, always in reconfiguration" —
capable of sustaining people when they need them, but requiring ongoing
maintenance and trust that capitalist conditions of overwork, mobility,
and atomisation constantly undermine.[^16] The village has to be built,
and building it under present conditions is hard. That difficulty is not
an argument against trying — it is an argument for understanding
alloparenting as a political project, not simply a lifestyle choice.
