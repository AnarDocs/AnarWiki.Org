## Overview

Written sometime between 1552 and 1553, when La Boétie was around 22 and
studying at university, the *Discours de la servitude volontaire*
(*Discourse on Voluntary Servitude*) circulated in manuscript for
decades after its author's death. His closest friend
<a href="Michel_de_Montaigne" class="wikilink"
title="Michel de Montaigne">Michel de Montaigne</a> came into possession
of it but declined to publish it, and it only entered wider circulation
in 1576 among exiled
<a href="Huguenots" class="wikilink" title="Huguenots">Huguenots</a> in
the <a href="County_of_Holland" class="wikilink"
title="County of Holland">County of Holland</a>.[^1]

## Summary

The *Discourse* opens with a deceptively simple question: why do people
submit to authority? La Boétie's answer is equally stark — they choose
to. <a href="Liberty" class="wikilink" title="Liberty">Liberty</a>, he
argued, belongs to human nature, but
<a href="slavery" class="wikilink" title="slavery">slavery</a> has no
foundation in natural law; it persists through habit and nothing more.
Were people to live by their natural rights alone, they would defer to
their own parents and their own reason, and to no one else.[^2]

La Boétie identified three varieties of tyrant — those elected by the
people, those who seize power by force, and those who inherit it by
succession — and condemned all three. Although he considered the elected
tyrant marginally the most defensible, he held that all three
arrangements share the same fundamental problem: they require the ruled
to surrender their freedom.[^3]

The deeper argument is that tyranny is not primarily maintained through
violence but through the calculated self-interest of those who profit
from their own subordination. The ruled become complicit in their own
domination. The remedy La Boétie proposed was
<a href="civil_disobedience" class="wikilink"
title="civil disobedience">civil disobedience</a>: people need not
overthrow tyrants by force, because rulers hold power only on loan. To
withdraw consent is enough.[^4]

## Legacy

The *Discourse* was largely forgotten until the
<a href="Age_of_Enlightenment" class="wikilink"
title="Age of Enlightenment">Age of Enlightenment</a>, when
<a href="Jean-Jacques_Rousseau" class="wikilink"
title="Jean-Jacques Rousseau">Jean-Jacques Rousseau</a> read it, and it
was republished during the <a href="French_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="French Revolution">French Revolution</a> for the first time in
two centuries. Its influence spread unevenly but persistently across
political traditions that had little else in common.[^5]

Among
<a href="anarchism" class="wikilink" title="anarchists">anarchists</a>,
the *Discourse* has long been recognised as one of the most significant
precursors to anarchist thought.
<a href="Gustav_Landauer" class="wikilink"
title="Gustav Landauer">Gustav Landauer</a>,
<a href="Bart_de_Ligt" class="wikilink" title="Bart de Ligt">Bart de
Ligt</a>, <a href="Max_Nettlau" class="wikilink" title="Max Nettlau">Max
Nettlau</a>,
<a href="Rudolf_Rocker" class="wikilink" title="Rudolf Rocker">Rudolf
Rocker</a>, and
<a href="Nicolas_Walter" class="wikilink" title="Nicolas Walter">Nicolas
Walter</a> all treated it as foundational, drawn to its depiction of a
world without the state and its insistence that political power derives
entirely from popular acquiescence.[^6]
<a href="Ralph_Waldo_Emerson" class="wikilink"
title="Ralph Waldo Emerson">Ralph Waldo Emerson</a> dedicated a poem to
La Boétie, and
<a href="Leo_Tolstoy" class="wikilink" title="Leo Tolstoy">Leo
Tolstoy</a> translated it into Russian — a measure of how far its
pacifist implications reached beyond France.
