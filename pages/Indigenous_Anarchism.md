# Indigenous Anarchism

## Overview

Indigenous Anarchism names a set of ideas, practices, and tensions at
the intersection of anarchist politics and Indigenous ways of being. As
a concept it resists easy definition — and deliberately so. While
<a href="European_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="European anarchism">European anarchism</a> developed as a formal
tradition through the writings of Proudhon, Bakunin, and Kropotkin,
Indigenous peoples across the world have practiced forms of
non-hierarchical, place-based, mutually sustaining social life for
millennia, predating and in many respects exceeding what that tradition
articulates. The
<a href="Indigenous_Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
title="Indigenous Anarchist Federation">Indigenous Anarchist
Federation</a> has argued plainly that anarchism as practiced by many
North American tribes predates European anarchism by thousands of years,
destroyed only relatively recently by the forces of colonisation,
capitalism, and white supremacy.[^1]

The tension at the heart of Indigenous Anarchism is whether it should
exist as a named theory at all. Klee Benally, writing in *Black Seed:
Not On Any Map*, argued forcefully against fixing Indigenous Anarchism
into a doctrine: "Indigenous autonomy needs no theoretical foundation to
justify itself."[^2] Aragorn!, whose 2005 essay *Locating an Indigenous
Anarchism* opened much of this conversation, similarly resisted any
programmatic conclusion. The refusal to be pinned down — to remain, in
Benally's phrase, "unknowable" — is not a weakness in the tradition but
one of its defining commitments.

## Origins

### First Principles

Aragorn! identified three first principles for an Indigenous Anarchism,
distinct from the classical anarchist trio of direct action, mutual aid,
and voluntary cooperation. These were: everything is alive; the
ascendance of memory; and sharing is living.[^3]

The principle that everything is alive refuses the category of the
object. Nothing is dead matter to be used; everything encountered has
complexity, dynamism, and worth. As Aragorn! put it, if everything is
alive, then sociology, politics, and statistics — as anti-life
disciplines — all have to be destroyed.

The ascendance of memory is a counter to a world built on institutional
forgetting. Those with power to speak — teachers, media, institutions —
devote little to honesty, and their memory comes from the same
forgetfulness as everyone else's. Against this, an Indigenous Anarchism
proposes spending far greater time remembering, sharing memory with
those we love and those who pass by us.

The third principle, sharing as living, refuses the reduction of social
life to exchange or contract. Benally glossed it thus: "There is no
authority above yourself," an anarchist would say; "There is no
authority but nature," an Indigenous Anarchist would offer.[^4]

To these, Benally added a fourth defining characteristic: Indigenous
Anarchism is not an identity.

### An Anarchism of Place

Central to Aragorn!'s account was the concept of an anarchism of place —
that is, a politics rooted in the specific land one actually inhabits,
rather than in abstract universal principles. This distinguishes it
sharply from the universalising tendency of much European radical
thought. "An indigenous anarchism is an anarchism of place," Aragorn!
wrote; location is "the differentiation that is crushed by the mortar of
urbanisation and pestle of mass culture into the paste of modern
alienation."[^5]

This matters beyond the philosophical. Benally, writing from a Diné
perspective, noted that for his people, "where and who we are mean the
same thing," an understanding cultivated through generations of
mutuality with the land. The cosmologies of Indigenous peoples — among
Diné, expressed through the six sacred mountains and ceremonies
maintaining covenant with nature, through concepts like *Nahasdzáán dóó
Yádiłhił Bitsąądęę Beenahaz'áanii* (the natural order of mother earth
and father sky) — are not metaphors for political positions. They are
living relations, and are incompatible with any form of centralised
governance.[^6]

The K'é Infoshop in Window Rock, Arizona — an autonomous anti-capitalist
space established in 2017 on Navajo Nation land — offers a practical
expression of this. Members describe *k'é*, the Navajo kinship concept,
as "our theory of everything... how we're connected to everything — but
specifically how that kinship is reciprocated and maintained."[^7]
Brandon Benallie, a co-founder, explained that before he encountered the
language of anarchism, his grandfather had simply taught him: "We are
all given unique gifts and abilities, and we must do what we can to
provide for others with our unique gifts and abilities." That, he said,
"was just k'é."[^8]

## Tendencies

### Relation to European Anarchism

The classical anarchist principles did not, in fact, originate in
Europe. Benallie argued that anarchism and communism as European
traditions derived ideas from Franciscan missionaries who studied
Indigenous societies in the Americas from the 1500s onwards, and from
the accounts of those missionaries read by Engels, Marx, and
Bakunin.[^9] Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* drew
heavily on observations of non-European peoples. The debt, as Benally
noted dryly, runs in one direction: "They drew from our blood and we
kept bleeding. In their distillation they separated out our matriarchy,
our queerness, and that which made us whole."[^10]

When anarchism speaks, Benally wrote, "we locate an affinity in our
hostility towards those who have imposed themselves upon us." But
hostility towards anarchist identity coexists with some affinity for
anarchism itself — particularly for its uncompromising rejection of the
state. Taiaiake Alfred, in his 2005 book *Wasàse*, coined the term
"anarcho-Indigenism" to name "a rejection of alliances with legalized
systems of oppression, non-participation in the institutions that
structure the colonial relationship, and a belief in bringing about
change through direct action, physical resistance, and confrontations
with state power."[^11]

What anarchism has going for it, Benally suggested, is a certain
dynamism: unlike Marxism or revolutionary socialism, it has "the unique
character of resisting urges towards intransigence" and has been
"developed and redeveloped as a dynamic position that strengthens with
its contortions."[^12] Anarchism is among the few anti-political
propositions that can be configured through Indigenous teachings and
remain intact.

### Anti-Civilisation

Several Indigenous anarchist writers have engaged with
<a href="anti-civilisation" class="wikilink"
title="anti-civilisation">anti-civilisation</a> positions. Writing on
*raddle.me*, ziq argued that civilisation as such — defined as a culture
revolving around cities that must import resources to survive — depends
structurally on the exploitation of Indigenous peoples and lands. The
process of "civilising" Indigenous peoples requires displacement, forced
labour, cultural and linguistic suppression, and the destruction of the
ecosystems that sustained non-sedentary life for millennia.[^13]

This framing draws a sharp distinction between tools — made locally,
from available materials, without constructing systems of authority —
and technology, which "depends on the ability to mount immense
operations of extraction, production, distribution and consumption" and
therefore "demands coercive authority and hierarchy."[^14] The Fifth
Estate's 1981 formulation was cited approvingly: technology "is not a
simple tool which can be used in any way we like. It is a form of social
organization, a set of social relations. It has its own laws."[^15]

### Prefiguration and Integral Living

Kahala Johnson and Kathy E. Ferguson, in their chapter *Anarchism and
Indigeneity* in the *Palgrave Handbook of Anarchism* (2019), drew
connections between anarchist prefigurative politics and Indigenous
sovereignty practices.[^16] Prefiguration — building the new society
inside the shell of the old — resonates with what Noelani
Goodyear-Ka'ōpua describes as Indigenous sovereignty not as a plan made
in advance but as "enacted on the ground through political
organising."[^17]

Both traditions also share a concept of integral living — the refusal of
the separation between mental and manual labour, between people and
land, between self and community. In Hawaiian pedagogical movements like
Hālau Kū Mána, students learn to read stars for navigation, cultivate
taro, draw water through irrigation ditches, and chant — practices
integrating head and hands in ways that parallel the Modern Schools of
<a href="Francisco_Ferrer" class="wikilink"
title="Francisco Ferrer">Francisco Ferrer</a>, while rooted in
specifically Hawaiian relations to the land.[^18]

The concept of *kuleana* — a Hawaiian notion intertwining authority and
responsibility — offers one point of connection with anarchist thinking
about mutual, shifting, voluntary authority. Goodyear-Ka'ōpua suggested
that Kanaka Maoli might "consider blending this voluntary and mutual
authority with older Hawaiian practices of governance and
decision-making about our natural resources," so that those with the
most intimate knowledge of particular resources would have greater say
in decisions about those activities.[^19]

### Temporality

Johnson and Ferguson also examined the question of time. Both anarchists
and Indigenous peoples are bracketed by hegemonic historical narratives
as untimely: Indigenous people as nostalgic for a pristine but lost
past, anarchists as optimistic for an impossible future. Yet as Seneca
scholar Mishuana Goeman observed, Native peoples are "becoming and
belonging in movement rather than as stable and unchanging
identities."[^20]

Indigenous time and anarchist time both emerge in a counter-history that
does not develop primarily through inclusion in the temporal registers
of states and capital. They do not dissolve into a single alternative,
but inhabit what Mark Rifkin calls "discrepant temporalities" — plural,
non-normative durations grounded in distinct life-worlds.[^21]

## Criticism

### The Colonial Blind Spot

The most substantial challenge to any synthesis of European anarchism
and Indigenous politics is the settler colonial history of the anarchist
tradition itself. Benally documented that Voltairine de Cleyre, one of
the most prominent early anarchist thinkers in North America, celebrated
the violence of Bacon's Rebellion in her 1912 essay *Direct Action* as
an exemplary unmediated act — without noting that this same rebellion
involved the massacre of Occaneechi warriors by the very militia it
celebrates, and that settler militias like Bacon's later transformed
into the first police forces in "America."[^22]

More recently, Cindy Milstein's 2010 book *Anarchism and Its
Aspirations* called for "a second 'American Revolution'" while
acknowledging the injustices of the founding of the United States as a
"sideshow." For Benally, this represents not an oversight but a
structural tendency: "American anarchist history and contemporary
analysis is devoid of meaningful anti-colonial analysis and
action."[^23]

Settler colonialism is, by definition, involuntary association.
Colonisers who are anarchists still maintain an implicit position of
dominance over Indigenous peoples and lands — which is, as Benally
wrote, "unmistakably contrary to anti-authoritarianism."[^24] This has
been apparent in primitivist, green anarchist, and rewilding tendencies
wrought with cultural appropriation, fetishism, and erasure.

### Against Theory

Benally's deepest challenge is not to the politics of anarchism but to
the project of theorising Indigenous Anarchism at all. Theory, he
argued, proposes to map who and what we are "into the awareness we
reject; to make us known and formulate a position through the
cartography of settler knowledge." The calcification of an Indigenous
anarchist theory would lead to the same merchandising and doctrinal
rigidity that has rendered other political theories into "banal
dramaturgy."[^25]

The anonymous Diné account of the 2019 Indigenous Anarchist Convergence
in occupied Kinłani offered a ground-level version of this concern: a
movement that essentialises indigeneity globally becomes one whose
absence others will speak into, and whose monolithic form the state can
more easily identify and repress.[^26]

Aragorn! had anticipated this in *A Non-European Anarchism*: "The
formation of a non-European anarchism is untenable. The term bespeaks a
general movement when the goal is an infinite series of disparate
movements."[^27]

### Race and Self-Determination

Aragorn! also noted that a significant proportion of anarchists hold
that race does not matter — that it is at best a tool used to divide
people. Outside the merits of these arguments, he argued, they violate
the principles of self-determination and radical decentralisation: they
expect reality to conform to a subjective understanding rather than
engaging with people as they actually are.[^28]

The question of whether anarchists can apply the principle of
self-determination to the fact that real people identify within racial
and cultural categories — and that this identification has consequences
— is one the tradition has not yet resolved. As long as the most
appropriate critique remains the arbiter of anarchism, Aragorn! argued,
anarchism will continue to be an isolated sect far removed from any
particularly anarchistic events that happen in the world.[^29]

## Practice

The practical expressions of Indigenous Anarchism are various and resist
reduction to a single model.

- The
  <a href="K&#39;é_Infoshop" class="wikilink" title="K&#39;é Infoshop">K'é
  Infoshop</a> in Window Rock, Arizona, established 2017, operates as an
  autonomous, anti-colonial, anti-capitalist community space on Navajo
  Nation land, with activities including mutual aid, food distribution,
  an infoshop, and solidarity meals.[^30]

<!-- -->

- The <a href="Táala_Hooghan_Infoshop" class="wikilink"
  title="Táala Hooghan Infoshop">Táala Hooghan Infoshop</a> in occupied
  Kinłani (Flagstaff, Arizona), established 2007, hosted the 2013 "Fire
  at the Mountain" anti-colonial anarchist book fair and the 2019
  Indigenous Anarchist Convergence.[^31]

<!-- -->

- The <a href="Indigenous_Anarchist_Federation" class="wikilink"
  title="Indigenous Anarchist Federation">Indigenous Anarchist
  Federation</a> (Federación Anarquista Indígena), established 2019,
  works to unite Indigenous anarchist struggles in North America,
  arguing that anarchism in the continent must reflect the Indigenous
  traditions displaced by colonialism and capitalism, and must speak to
  the material realities of people both on reservations and in
  diaspora.[^32]

<!-- -->

- The Diné, O'odham, Anarchist Bloc, which intervened in a 2010 march
  against the fascist sheriff Joe Arpaio in occupied Akimel O'odham
  Pi-Posh land (Phoenix, Arizona), was attacked by police and denounced
  as "outside agitators" by non-profit migrant justice groups — despite
  being composed of elders and youth Indigenous to the area.[^33]
