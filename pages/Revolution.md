# Revolution

## Overview

Revolution — the fundamental dismantling of hierarchical power and its
replacement with free, self-organised social relations — has been a
central concern of anarchist theory and practice since the movement's
emergence in the nineteenth century. Anarchists do not agree on what
revolution looks like, when it will come, or precisely how it will be
achieved. What they share is a conviction that the existing order,
sustained by structural violence, cannot be reformed into liberation: it
must be broken.

This distinguishes the anarchist conception of revolution sharply from
both liberal reformism and Marxist-Leninist approaches. Liberals seek to
humanise the state; Leninists seek to capture it. Anarchists reject the
premise that state power can ever serve as a vehicle for emancipation.
As <a href="Luigi_Fabbri" class="wikilink" title="Luigi Fabbri">Luigi
Fabbri</a> argued, libertarian violence — the violence of those who no
longer wish to obey — ceases to be revolutionary the moment it
crystallises into a new government.[^1] The means of revolution must
prefigure its ends.

## Origins

The word "revolution" in its political sense refers to a general
movement in which a people or class, breaking with legality and
overturning existing institutions, overthroughs the prevailing political
and social regime and establishes new social arrangements.[^2]
Anarchists inherited this concept from the broader tradition of radical
thought emerging from the French Revolution, but immediately began to
transform it. Where Jacobins and later Blanquists conceived of
revolution as seizure of state power by a disciplined vanguard, and
Marxists added the notion of a transitional workers' state, anarchists
argued from the beginning that any such seizure would reproduce the
fundamental problem: the domination of some people by others.

<a href="Mikhail_Bakunin" class="wikilink"
title="Mikhail Bakunin">Mikhail Bakunin</a>'s split with
<a href="Karl_Marx" class="wikilink" title="Karl Marx">Karl Marx</a> in
the <a href="First_International" class="wikilink"
title="First International">First International</a> in 1872 crystallised
this disagreement. Bakunin insisted that a revolutionary party standing
*outside* the people — however well-intentioned — would inevitably
become their new masters. The revolution could only be made by those who
had to live in its aftermath, acting on their own terms and through
their own organisations.[^3]

<a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Kropotkin">Peter
Kropotkin</a> developed this into a more systematic theory, arguing that
revolution was not a single rupture but a process — that the task was to
hasten and clarify the conditions for popular insurrection while
building the organisations that would allow the masses to act
independently of any leadership.[^4] He was explicit that no revolution
was possible if the need for it was not felt among the people
themselves.[^5]

## The Anarchist Critique of Authoritarian Revolution

The experience of the Russian Revolution proved formative for anarchist
thinking about what revolution must not become. Anarchists participated
enthusiastically in the February and October revolutions of 1917, only
to find themselves systematically suppressed once the Bolsheviks
consolidated power. The <a href="Makhnovshchina" class="wikilink"
title="Makhnovshchina">Makhnovshchina</a> in Ukraine was destroyed by
the Red Army in 1921; the <a href="Kronstadt_rebellion" class="wikilink"
title="Kronstadt rebellion">Kronstadt rebellion</a> was drowned in
blood. For anarchists, this was not an accident of circumstance but the
logical consequence of the Leninist theory of the
<a href="vanguard_party" class="wikilink"
title="vanguard party">vanguard party</a> and the
<a href="dictatorship_of_the_proletariat" class="wikilink"
title="dictatorship of the proletariat">dictatorship of the
proletariat</a>.

Fabbri's analysis, written in 1921, identified the structural problem:
any dictatorial power, however "provisional" in intent, tends by its
nature to become stable and self-reinforcing.[^6] Government, once
formed, acts to preserve itself — which means suppressing the further
development of the revolution. The Bolsheviks did not betray their
principles; they followed them. The vanguard's seizure of the soviets in
the name of the workers was precisely what their theory prescribed. The
result, as
<a href="Nestor_Makhno" class="wikilink" title="Nestor Makhno">Nestor
Makhno</a> wrote from bitter experience, was that workers found
themselves under a new regime as oppressive as the old, with the added
obscenity that it claimed to act in their name.[^7]

Jeff Shantz identifies the deeper structural cause: where people lack
prior experience of self-organisation, they become dependent on whoever
offers to coordinate change on their behalf.[^8] Vanguard leadership is
not simply imposed on populations; it becomes a default position where
people feel unprepared to organise and construct viable alternatives.
This is why the building of what Shantz calls "anarchist transfer
cultures" — self-organised institutions that model the new society
within the shell of the old — is not merely desirable but strategically
indispensable.[^9]

## Revolution as Process, Not Event

A central revision in anarchist thinking since the classical period has
been a move away from the idea of revolution as a single, definitive
rupture — the storming of the Winter Palace, the general strike that
brings everything to a halt — toward an understanding of revolution as
an ongoing process of social transformation.

Howard Ehrlich, quoted by Shantz, expressed the point simply:
"Revolution is a process, and even the eradication of coercive
institutions will not automatically create a liberatory society. We
create that society by building new institutions, by changing the
character of our social relationships, by changing ourselves."[^10] The
insurrectionary moment, when it comes, will be preceded and shaped by
the slow, often unglamorous work of building capacities, relationships,
and alternative structures. Without this groundwork, sudden upheaval
produces only chaos or a new hierarchy.

<a href="David_Graeber" class="wikilink" title="David Graeber">David
Graeber</a> offered a complementary formulation: rather than asking
"what is revolution?", it becomes more useful to ask "what is
revolutionary action?" His answer: any collective action that rejects
and confronts some form of power or domination, and in doing so
reconstitutes social relations — even within the collectivity — in that
light.[^11] By this definition, revolutionary action does not require
seizing state power. Attempts to create autonomous communities in the
face of power are, almost by definition, revolutionary acts.

Graeber developed this further in "Revolution in Reverse", observing
that contemporary mass mobilisations effectively invert the classic
insurrectionary sequence. Rather than a dramatic confrontation with
state power leading to popular festivity and the creation of new
institutions, activists in the tradition of the
<a href="global_justice_movement" class="wikilink"
title="global justice movement">global justice movement</a> began by
creating new forms of collective decision-making — assemblies, spokes
councils, consensus processes — and used these to organise the actions
themselves, with confrontations with the state following from, rather
than preceding, the construction of new social relations.[^12]

## Means and Ends

The insistence that means and ends must be consistent is one of the most
distinctive features of anarchist revolutionary theory, and one of its
most practically significant. If the goal is a free, non-hierarchical
society, then organising toward it through hierarchical and coercive
means will not produce that goal — it will produce a new hierarchy. This
is not merely a moral scruple; it is an empirical observation drawn from
the history of every authoritarian "revolution" of the twentieth
century.

George Barrett put the point with characteristic directness in 1920: you
cannot put new wine into old bottles. The institutions of the present
society, based on subjection, will not hold the spirit of liberty that
should compose the new society.[^13] The implication is that
revolutionary transformation requires the prior development of
organisations genuinely modelled on the principles of freedom — not as a
rehearsal, but as the substance of the new society already in formation.

Fabbri distinguished between libertarian violence — used by those who no
longer wish to obey, directed at dismantling domination — and
authoritarian violence, which ceases to be revolutionary the moment it
attempts to consolidate power over others.[^14] This distinction matters
practically: popular, decentralised revolutionary action is, in Fabbri's
view, more radical and harder to defeat than centralised state terror,
which must always hesitate out of concern for its own survival and
reputation.[^15]

## Prefiguration and Transfer Cultures

Alongside insurrectionary and process-based conceptions of revolution,
anarchism has developed the concept of
<a href="prefigurative_politics" class="wikilink"
title="prefigurative politics">prefigurative politics</a>: the
deliberate construction, in the present, of social relations and
institutions that model the future society. Prefigurative practice does
not wait for the revolution to build the new world; it begins building
it now, both as preparation and as expression of the values at stake.

Shantz, drawing on Howard Ehrlich, uses the concept of the "anarchist
transfer culture" — the agglomeration of ideas and practices that guides
people from the society here to the society there, providing real
alternatives to capitalist and state structures and building the
organisational capacities needed for any meaningful transformation.[^16]
Free schools, community gardens, worker co-operatives, squats,
alternative media, and rank-and-file union networks all constitute
elements of such a culture, insofar as they embody non-hierarchical
relations and prefigure a world organised around need rather than
profit.

The feminist movement, as Graeber observed, has been one of the most
important drivers of this development within contemporary anarchism. The
current emphasis on consensus process and assembly-based organisation in
radical movements traces back directly to feminist organising in the
1960s and 1970s, when the search for non-hierarchical forms of
decision-making produced the affinity group structure, the spokes
council model, and a range of facilitation methods that remain
foundational to anarchist practice today.[^17]

Prefigurative politics is not without its critics, including within
anarchism. Murray Bookchin warned that it risks collapsing into
subcultural self-expression — "lifestyle anarchism" — that leaves
capitalist structures untouched. The challenge, as Shantz acknowledges,
is to connect the construction of alternative institutions to active
contestation of dominant ones: building the new society while fighting
the old one, not retreating from it.[^18]

## Other Paths to Social Transformation

Revolution is not the only path anarchists have theorised toward the
dismantling of hierarchical power. Several related but distinct
approaches have developed:

**<a href="Direct_action" class="wikilink" title="Direct action">Direct
action</a>** operates on the principle that the people affected by an
issue are the only ones capable of addressing it, and that meaningful
change comes from those at the bottom of social hierarchies acting in
their own interests without mediation.
<a href="George_Barrett" class="wikilink" title="George Barrett">George
Barrett</a> traced the logic: if transport workers distribute wealth, it
is transport workers — not politicians — to whom one must turn to change
the distribution of wealth.[^19]

**<a href="Mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="Mutual aid">Mutual aid</a>**
— the practice of communities meeting their own needs through voluntary
cooperation rather than charity or the state — was theorised by
Kropotkin as an evolutionary force and remains a core element of
anarchist practice. It builds community resilience and social bonds that
strengthen the conditions for broader transformation.

**<a href="Anarcho-syndicalism" class="wikilink"
title="Anarcho-syndicalism">Anarcho-syndicalism</a>** pursued the
industrial union as the dual vehicle of present-day resistance and
future self-management, arguing that workers organised along industrial
lines could both contest capitalism now and provide the organisational
skeleton of a free economy after the revolution.

**<a href="Propaganda_of_the_deed" class="wikilink"
title="Propaganda of the deed">Propaganda of the deed</a>** — associated
with the insurrectionary tendency in late nineteenth and early twentieth
century anarchism — held that exemplary acts of resistance, including
political violence, could communicate revolutionary possibilities more
effectively than any pamphlet, inspiring others to act and demonstrating
the vulnerability of power. The strategy was most associated with
figures such as <a href="Errico_Malatesta" class="wikilink"
title="Errico Malatesta">Errico Malatesta</a> and
<a href="Johann_Most" class="wikilink" title="Johann Most">Johann
Most</a>, though Malatesta later distanced himself from individual
assassination as tactically counterproductive. CrimethInc, writing in a
contemporary idiom, echoes the underlying logic without the violence:
"Don't just chant that another world is possible; manifest it, so
everyone who might believe in it can."[^20] The propaganda of the deed
tradition sits in permanent tension with prefigurative approaches — one
seeks to shatter the existing order through demonstration, the other to
grow the alternative from within it.

**Prefigurative institution-building** — as discussed above — constructs
the new society within the old, creating frameworks of self-management,
free association, and mutual support that can both sustain communities
in the present and expand to replace hierarchical institutions over
time.

These approaches are not mutually exclusive. Most anarchist practice
combines elements of all of them, with the emphasis varying according to
context and strategic judgment.

## Historical Examples

Anarchists have participated in, and in some cases led, a number of
revolutionary upheavals. Each has been shaped by, and has in turn
shaped, anarchist thinking about what revolution can and cannot achieve:

- **<a href="Paris_Commune" class="wikilink" title="Paris Commune">Paris
  Commune</a> (1871)** — The first major workers' government in history,
  established after the fall of the Second Empire. Anarchists including
  <a href="Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a> and
  <a href="Élisée_Reclus" class="wikilink" title="Élisée Reclus">Élisée
  Reclus</a> participated or expressed solidarity. The Commune
  established workers' self-management, abolished standing armies in
  favour of a citizens' militia, and separated church from state, but
  was crushed by the French Army after 72 days.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Makhnovshchina" class="wikilink"
  title="Makhnovshchina">Makhnovshchina</a> (1918–1921)** — The
  <a href="Free_Territory_of_Ukraine" class="wikilink"
  title="Free Territory of Ukraine">Free Territory of Ukraine</a>,
  organised under the military leadership of
  <a href="Nestor_Makhno" class="wikilink" title="Nestor Makhno">Nestor
  Makhno</a>, established an anarchist society across a large region of
  southern Ukraine during the Russian Civil War. It convened free
  soviets, collectivised production, and maintained a volunteer army
  operating without officers' privilege. It was ultimately destroyed by
  the Red Army.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Kronstadt_rebellion" class="wikilink"
  title="Kronstadt rebellion">Kronstadt rebellion</a> (1921)** — Sailors
  and workers at the Kronstadt fortress revolted against Bolshevik
  authoritarianism, demanding free soviets, free speech, and an end to
  one-party rule. The rebellion was suppressed by Trotsky's forces.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Revolutionary_Catalonia" class="wikilink"
  title="Revolutionary Catalonia">Revolutionary Catalonia</a>
  (1936–1939)** — During the
  <a href="Spanish_Civil_War" class="wikilink"
  title="Spanish Civil War">Spanish Civil War</a>, the
  <a href="CNT" class="wikilink" title="CNT">CNT</a>-<a href="FAI" class="wikilink" title="FAI">FAI</a>
  led a social revolution across Catalonia, Aragon, and parts of
  Andalusia. Factories were collectivised under worker committees,
  agricultural land was reorganised as libertarian communes, and in
  anarchist strongholds the figure of the economy under workers' control
  reached 75%.[^21] The revolution was ultimately crushed by a
  combination of Francoist military advance and Stalinist
  counter-revolution within the Republican zone.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Strandzha_Commune" class="wikilink"
  title="Strandzha Commune">Strandzha Commune</a> (1903)** — A
  short-lived anarchist commune established in Bulgaria during the
  <a href="Ilinden–Preobrazhenie_Uprising" class="wikilink"
  title="Ilinden–Preobrazhenie Uprising">Ilinden–Preobrazhenie
  Uprising</a>, organised by Mihail Gerdzhikov and the Internal
  Macedonian Adrianople Revolutionary Organization. Defeated by Ottoman
  forces after approximately one month.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Shinmin_Autonomous_Region" class="wikilink"
  title="Shinmin Autonomous Region">Shinmin Autonomous Region</a>
  (1929–1931)** — Established by the
  <a href="Korean_Anarchist_Communist_Federation" class="wikilink"
  title="Korean Anarchist Communist Federation">Korean Anarchist Communist
  Federation</a> in Manchuria, organising over two million Korean
  immigrants in decentralised village, district, and area councils for
  agriculture, education, and finance. Destroyed by Japanese military
  occupation.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Zapatista" class="wikilink" title="Zapatista">Zapatista</a>
  communities (1994–present)** — Though not identifying primarily as
  anarchist, the <a href="EZLN" class="wikilink" title="EZLN">EZLN</a>'s
  rebellion in Chiapas and its subsequent construction of autonomous
  communities organised through assemblies and without state structures
  has been widely analysed as prefigurative and influenced by
  libertarian socialist principles.

<!-- -->

- **<a href="Rojava" class="wikilink" title="Rojava">Rojava</a>
  (2012–present)** — The
  <a href="Autonomous_Administration_of_North_and_East_Syria"
  class="wikilink"
  title="Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria">Autonomous
  Administration of North and East Syria</a> emerged from the Syrian
  civil war with an explicitly anti-statist, feminist, and ecologically
  oriented political programme drawing on
  <a href="Democratic_Confederalism" class="wikilink"
  title="Democratic Confederalism">Democratic Confederalism</a>, itself
  theorised by <a href="Abdullah_Öcalan" class="wikilink"
  title="Abdullah Öcalan">Abdullah Öcalan</a> in dialogue with
  <a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
  title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a>'s
  <a href="Libertarian_Municipalism" class="wikilink"
  title="Libertarian Municipalism">Libertarian Municipalism</a>. Its
  relationship to anarchism is contested — see
  <a href="Democratic_Confederalism" class="wikilink"
  title="Democratic Confederalism">Democratic Confederalism</a>.

## Criticism

The anarchist concept of revolution faces a number of serious
challenges, both internal and external.

Critics from the Marxist tradition argue that prefigurative and
process-based approaches underestimate the power of the capitalist
state, which will use military force to destroy any serious challenge to
its authority. The <a href="Spanish_Revolution" class="wikilink"
title="Spanish Revolution">Spanish Revolution</a> was defeated in part
by Franco's armies and in part by Stalinist counter-revolution — but
anarchists themselves acknowledge that any libertarian revolution will
face violent suppression and must be capable of defending itself.[^22]

Within anarchism, there is ongoing debate about the relationship between
insurrectionary and gradualist approaches, and about the risk that
prefigurative institution-building becomes depoliticised — comfortable
enough within the existing system that it ceases to challenge it.
Bookchin's critique of "lifestyle anarchism" touched on this: it is not
enough to build alternative institutions if those institutions coexist
peacefully with the dominant order rather than contesting it.[^23]

A distinct and contested position within anarchism is
**accelerationism** in its left variants — the idea that deepening the
contradictions of capitalism or the state, whether through militant
confrontation or strategic disruption, will hasten the conditions for
revolutionary transformation. Unlike prefigurative approaches,
accelerationist strategies are explicitly oriented toward crisis rather
than construction. Critics within anarchism argue that this conflates
destabilisation with liberation: a collapsing system does not
automatically produce a free one, and historical examples — from Weimar
Germany to contemporary failed states — suggest that the primary
beneficiaries of accelerated crisis are often authoritarian movements
rather than libertarian ones. The means-ends argument applies here with
particular force.
