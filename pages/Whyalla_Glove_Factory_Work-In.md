The **Whyalla Glove Factory Work-In** was an episode of
<a href="Workers&#39;_Self-Management" class="wikilink"
title="workers&#39; control">workers' control</a> in
<a href="Whyalla" class="wikilink" title="Whyalla">Whyalla</a>,
<a href="Commonwealth_of_Australia" class="wikilink"
title="South Australia">South Australia</a> from
<a href="Timeline_of_Anarchism_in_Oceania" class="wikilink"
title="1972 to 1973">1972 to 1973</a>.

## Background

Twenty women machinists faced redundancy when James North and Sons — a
British company that had expanded significantly during World War Two —
decided to close the factory in late 1972. The women challenged the
company's prerogative to withdraw its operations at will, and ten of
them occupied the factory. The
<a href="Miscellaneous_Workers_Union" class="wikilink"
title="Miscellaneous Workers Union">Miscellaneous Workers Union</a>
(MWU), which represented the women, had been moving leftward since the
early 1960s, part of a broader radicalisation of Australian unions
during the period. The MWU was committed to militant strategies,
including converting sit-ins into workers' cooperatives. A former
Seaman's Union official observed of the unions of this period: "They
were becoming far more militant and serious about challenging the bosses
and winning, and therefore becoming more disliked… by employers."[^1]

## Events

When the women machinists streamed into the manager's office at the
commencement of their sit-in, the manager, J.E. Larven, was clearly
shocked. Those involved attest that Larven realised he had lost control
of the situation.[^2] Peter Duncan, a Labor MP, attended the occupation
on 20 November 1972 in his role as counsel for the MWU. He told the
South Australian Parliament that Larven "completely lost control of
himself and started punching people in all directions…. \[He\] was a
boxing instructor and he evidently decided to use his prowess on the
employees."[^3] Barry Cavanagh, MWU South Australian state secretary at
the time — whose wife's nose was broken by Larven — recalls:

> While the manager, Larven, was going berserk, outside the factory the
> workers streamed inside…. Larven eventually went and locked himself in
> his office and made frantic phone calls…. Then the Ship Painters and
> Dockers arrived with their secretary who had a particularly strong
> record of militancy and mobilized members to demonstrate solidarity
> with workers during their sit-in…. Larven had no more authority. In a
> show of solidarity and material support, all of a sudden a procession
> of Ship Painters and Dockers streamed into the factory through the
> window, later followed by mattresses, guitars, food, T.V. sets, and
> other amenities.[^4]

The next morning, hundreds of unionists and onlookers had gathered in
front of the building. When Larven arrived, he found the path blocked.
Police informed him they could escort him through, but could not
guarantee his safety if anything went wrong. Larven got back in his car
and went home.[^5] Around a hundred members of the Federated Ship
Painters and Dockers Union went out on strike in sympathy, while an ad
hoc committee of unionists at the factory announced they would attempt
to close down all industry in Whyalla.[^6] The sit-in lasted five days.
During this time the South Australian government ordered a consignment
of gloves, guaranteeing a month's work and giving the women time to
establish the Whyalla Cooperative.[^7]

## Media and Solidarity

The mainstream South Australian press was predictably hostile, with the
Adelaide *Advertiser* focusing on the militancy of the Ship Painters
union and running captions such as "Whyalla Unionists Invade Factory."
The normally conservative local *Whyalla News*, however, ran a strongly
supportive editorial, noting that "even the most implacable opponents of
direct action on the industrial front can hardly quarrel with the motive
behind the latest show of protest."[^8]

## The Whyalla Cooperative

The cooperative ran successfully under workers' control for nine months
before being sold to private interests — Spencer Gulf Clothing — who
guaranteed continued employment.[^9]

### Organisation and Work Practices

The women elected a committee of three to represent them collectively.
All decisions affecting the cooperative had to be ratified by the
workers, and unanimous agreement was the norm. Day-to-day operations
were discussed in meetings between the committee and the appointed
manager, Jim Gettings — a recently retired foreman brought in for his
knowledge of the machinery. The women paid Gettings six times what they
themselves earned on average.[^10] Gettings held no normal managerial
prerogatives. His occasional attempts to claim the power to hire and
fire without collective approval were consistently rejected, as were
bids for greater disciplinary authority. In practice, he often helped
with cutting, after which a worker would complete the sewing — a far cry
from the rigid division of labour under James North.[^11] Nancy Baines
was elected "supervisor" among the workers, but the role bore little
resemblance to its name. As she explained: "You can't 'supervise' people
who are their own bosses, because you can't give them orders." Her role
became that of organiser and quality controller — delivering materials,
inspecting work for flaws, and helping out when operators ran into
difficulty.[^12] Workers who were seen not to be pulling their weight
were not reprimanded by management but spoken to collectively, with
"encouragement rather than abuse or threats."[^13] Production was also
diversified from gloves into surgical gowns, with task allocation
determined by whoever wanted to learn a new process — and rotation used
when more than one worker expressed interest.[^14]

### Morale and Worker Experience

Productivity improved under cooperative conditions. Gettings himself
conceded there was greater "conscientiousness" in the cooperative; when
private ownership was resumed, he noted, "They didn't care. They played
up — always going to the toilet, arguing, complaining."[^15] The women
described the atmosphere during the cooperative as cordial and casual,
with morale high. Whenever a production run was completed and a truck
arrived to take goods to Adelaide, the workers would hold a small party
to celebrate.[^16] Workers' own accounts confirm the transformative
nature of the experience. One stated: "In James North I took pride in
the gloves which I made but nobody else's…. In the cooperative I took
pride in the whole organisation."[^17] Another worker, Jacqui Blakeley,
found the experience had spoiled her for wage labour altogether. When
Spencer Gulf Clothing took over, she refused to continue: "I don't want
a bridge between my wages and the product. The company is the middle-man
and you can't see any profit unless it is a cooperative."[^18]

### Gender and Limits of Autonomy

Gender dynamics complicated the cooperative's practice of
self-management. The decision to appoint a male manager — even one with
no genuine authority — meant a form of patriarchal hierarchy persisted,
however constrained. The women paid Gettings six times their own average
wage for what amounted to a technical and organisational support role.
The cultural context mattered too: accounts suggest the cooperative
eventually wound down partly because husbands were uneasy with the extra
time and commitment their wives were devoting to the factory rather than
domestic duties.[^19] The contrast with the cooperative's own
egalitarian norms was telling. When Gettings was named manager of the
new private company after reprivatisation, his manner changed
dramatically — he became authoritarian and demanded to be called "Mr.
Gettings." None of the women took any notice, and they continued to call
him Jim.[^20]
