# Tlaxcala

## Overview

**Tlaxcala** (Nahuatl: *Tlaxcallān*, "place of maize tortillas") was a
pre-Columbian republic in central
<a href="Mexico" class="wikilink" title="Mexico">Mexico</a>, remarkable
for its non-hierarchical political structure at a time when the
surrounding
<a href="Aztec_Empire" class="wikilink" title="Aztec Empire">Aztec
Empire</a> was organised along strictly authoritarian lines. Completely
encircled by Aztec territory, the Tlaxcalteca maintained their autonomy
for generations — resisting absorption through both military resistance
and the internal cohesion of their republican institutions.

The absence of any palace or central temple in the archaeological record
suggests a society that deliberately distributed rather than
concentrated power.[^1]
<a href="Hernán_Cortés" class="wikilink" title="Hernán Cortés">Hernán
Cortés</a> himself, writing to the Spanish crown, observed that "the
order of government so far observed among the people resembles very much
the republics of Venice, Genoa, and Pisa for there is no supreme
overlord."[^2]

## History

The Tlaxcalteca arrived in central Mexico during the Late Postclassic
period, initially settling near
<a href="Texcoco" class="wikilink" title="Texcoco">Texcoco</a> in the
Valley of Mexico. Driven eastward, they eventually established the city
of Tepeticpac under the leadership of Culhuatecuhtli Quanex. Over
subsequent generations the Tlaxcalan state expanded, with the founding
of Ocotelolco and Tizatlán, followed by Quiahuiztlan — the four
altepemeh that would form the confederation.[^3]

Throughout this period, Tlaxcala was ringed by Aztec territory and
subject to sustained military and economic pressure. Under Moctezuma I,
the Aztec Triple Alliance instituted a policy of ritual "Flower Wars"
against Tlaxcala — nominally to obtain prisoners for sacrifice —
alongside a trade embargo intended to weaken the recalcitrant
polity.[^4] Despite this, the confederation held.

When Spanish forces under Cortés arrived in 1519, the Tlaxcalteca
initially fought them. According to conquistador Bernal Díaz del
Castillo, the Spanish would likely not have survived had the Tlaxcalan
elders Xicotencatl the Elder and Maxixcatzin not persuaded the war
leader Xicotencatl the Younger to ally with the newcomers rather than
destroy them.[^5] Xicotencatl the Younger, who later reversed course and
attempted to withdraw from the alliance, was condemned by the Tlaxcalan
ruling council and hanged by Cortés in April 1521 during the siege of
Tenochtitlan.

The alliance with Spain ultimately meant Tlaxcala's survival as a
distinct polity — though survival within a colonial order exacted its
own costs. The confederation was formally divided into four *señoríos*
by the Spanish corregidor Gómez de Santillán in 1545, and colonial-era
noble houses constructed fictitious genealogies to claim hereditary
rights over each.[^6] The republican institutions that had defined
Tlaxcala were progressively dismantled and replaced by the hierarchies
of New Spain.

## Organisation

Tlaxcala was governed by a parliament of between 50 and 200 elected
representatives, drawn from both noble (*pilli*) and commoner
(*macehualli*) classes. Officials gained their positions through service
to the community — primarily in warfare — rather than through
inheritance alone.[^7]

Decisions were made by consensus within this body. Entry into the
parliament was not simply granted — new members underwent a demanding
public induction involving exposure, fasting, sleep deprivation, and
bloodletting, a process designed to be visibly self-effacing and to
demonstrate subordination to the collective rather than elevation above
it.[^8] The archaeological record corroborates this: excavations at
Tizatlán and elsewhere in the Tlaxcalan altepemeh show an unusual degree
of equality in residential structures, with no evidence of a palace or
monumental administrative centre of the kind found in contemporary Aztec
cities.[^9]

This was not merely a proto-democratic curiosity but a functioning
anti-authoritarian system that lasted for generations under active
external pressure. The Tlaxcalan confederation held together without a
sovereign, without a standing elite monopoly on violence, and without
the monumental architecture that typically signals concentrated power.

## Culture

Three languages were spoken across Tlaxcala: Nahuatl (the majority
tongue and administrative language), Otomi (spoken by a substantial
minority, with further Otomi migration following Aztec conquests of
neighbouring regions), and Pinome — likely a form of Popoloca — spoken
by a smaller group.[^10] A dialect of Otomi is still spoken in the
region today.

The patron deity of the confederation was
<a href="Camaxtli" class="wikilink" title="Camaxtli">Camaxtli</a>, a god
of hunting and war associated with the Chichimec tradition from which
the Tlaxcalteca partly descended.

## Economy

The Aztec trade embargo imposed on Tlaxcala cut the confederation off
from lowland goods — cotton, cacao, and other tropical commodities —
that were otherwise accessible through Aztec-controlled markets.
Archaeological analysis suggests that rather than being crippled by this
isolation, the Tlaxcalteca adapted, developing internal exchange
networks and relying on local production. Some researchers have
questioned how complete the blockade actually was, pointing to evidence
of continued interregional exchange even during periods of nominal
embargo.[^11]
