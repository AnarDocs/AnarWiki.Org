# Teotihuacan

## Overview

<a href="Teotihuacan" class="wikilink"
title="Teotihuacan">Teotihuacan</a> was an ancient city in the Valley of
Mexico, active from around 100 BCE to 600 CE, with an estimated
population of around 100,000 people at its height. At its zenith it was
among the largest cities in the ancient world, covering roughly 18
square kilometres and containing some 2,000 buildings.

Although Teotihuacan began as a stratified society, major changes
occurred around 300 CE. The Temple of the Feathered Serpent was
desecrated and partially destroyed. Residents permanently ended new
pyramid construction and the ritual sacrifice of city residents.
Resources were redirected toward the construction of comfortable stone
apartment complexes for the vast majority of the population. The city's
artwork from this later period portrayed people at roughly the same
scale and level, with no depictions of rulers. According to
<a href="David_Graeber" class="wikilink" title="David Graeber">David
Graeber</a> and
<a href="David_Wengrow" class="wikilink" title="David Wengrow">David
Wengrow</a>, "The general consensus among those who know the site best
is that Teotihuacan was, in fact, a city organized along some sort of
self-consciously egalitarian lines."[^1]

## Culture

Art historian <a href="Esther_Pasztory" class="wikilink"
title="Esther Pasztory">Esther Pasztory</a> argues that Teotihuacan art
appears to have consciously avoided the styles of more hierarchical
Mesoamerican societies such as the Maya and Zapotec. Where Maya and
Zapotec art used curves and flowing forms associated with aristocratic
display, Teotihuacan favoured angular, block-like compositions. The
absence of ceremonial ball courts — a fixture of aristocratic spectacle
elsewhere in Mesoamerica — further suggests a deliberate rejection of
hierarchical cultural forms.[^2]

Teotihuacan was a genuinely multiethnic city. Migrants arrived from
across Mesoamerica, including substantial communities from the Oaxaca
region (the Tlailotlacan neighbourhood) and the Gulf Coast (the Barrio
de Los Comerciantes). Isotope analysis of skeletal remains has confirmed
that much of the population consisted of immigrants or their immediate
descendants, with evidence of sustained contact between Teotihuacan and
surrounding homelands across generations.[^3]

Artwork across the city depicted institutionalised roles and deities
rather than individual rulers. Murals portrayed priests, jaguar figures,
storm deities, and an anonymous goddess offering maize, precious stones,
and water. Rulers who might have wished to be immortalised in art are
conspicuously absent.[^4]

## Organisation

The city was organised around large apartment compounds distributed
across its area. These served not only as residences but as the primary
units of economic and social organisation — neighbourhood centres
housing craftspeople of various specialties, whose collective output
drove the city's productive life.[^5] Evidence from skeletal wear
patterns indicates that residents engaged in sustained craft production:
fibre-working, sewing, painting, and the carrying of heavy materials.
Female skeletons show patterns consistent with long periods of sewing or
painting.

The apartment compounds varied in quality, ranging from spacious and
well-appointed residences to more cramped quarters.[^6] Some scholars
have identified three broad residential strata — high elites,
intermediate elites, and labourers — though the city as a whole does not
appear to have been divided into discrete zoning districts, and elite
compounds were distributed across the urban area rather than
concentrated in a single quarter.[^7]

What is striking in the archaeological record is the near-total absence
of the markers of authoritarian rule found in contemporaneous
Mesoamerican cities. There are no royal palaces, no ceremonial ball
courts, no depictions of conquest, and no humiliated captives. Many
scholars have concluded that Teotihuacan was governed through some form
of collective arrangement, though its precise character remains
debated.[^8] Some researchers have proposed that after around 250–300
CE, political authority shifted from the Temple of the Feathered Serpent
complex toward a more decentralised structure centred on the Avenue of
the Dead.[^9]

## Economy

Teotihuacan held a near-monopoly on obsidian production in Mesoamerica,
with raw material drawn primarily from the Pachuca mines. Workshops
produced tools, blades, figurines, arrowheads, and ornaments that were
traded as far as Monte Albán, Tikal, and various Maya states. The state
closely monitored the movement and production of obsidian, restricting
manufacture to regional workshops.[^10]

The city also exported fine textiles and ceramics, and its craftspeople
developed a sophisticated internal economy organised through
neighbourhood centres. A large marketplace — the Great Compound — sat
across the Avenue of the Dead from the Citadel complex.

## Collapse

Teotihuacan's internal tensions appear to have been a primary driver of
its eventual disintegration. The burning of the city around 550 CE was
concentrated on elite housing compounds along the Avenue of the Dead
rather than the city as a whole, suggesting an internal uprising rather
than foreign invasion.[^11] The city's elite structures bore extensive
burn marks; the sculptures inside palatial structures such as Xalla were
deliberately shattered.

The collapse coincided with climatic disruption in 535–536 CE, possibly
caused by volcanic activity, which contributed to agricultural failures
and famine. Skeletal evidence from the 6th century shows a rise in
juvenile malnutrition. Drought, famine, and social conflict were likely
mutually reinforcing rather than separate causes.[^12]

Although the sacrifice of city residents had ended around 300 CE, the
sacrifice of war captives continued in the neighbourhood of Teopancazco,
and social stratification persisted throughout the city's later history.
Teotihuacan was not a fully egalitarian society, but the direction of
its development — away from monumental hierarchy and toward collective
urban life — remains historically significant.[^13]
