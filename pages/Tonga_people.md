# Tonga People

The **Tonga** (also known as the **Batonga** or **Plateau Tonga**) are a
<a href="Bantu_peoples" class="wikilink" title="Bantu">Bantu</a> ethnic
group of southern Zambia and northern Zimbabwe, widely regarded as among
Zambia's earliest Bantu inhabitants. They are related to the **Batoka**,
part of the
<a href="Tokaleya" class="wikilink" title="Tokaleya">Tokaleya</a>
people, but are distinct from the
<a href="Tonga_people_of_Malawi" class="wikilink"
title="Tonga people of Malawi">Tonga people of Malawi</a>, who belong to
the
<a href="Tumbuka_people" class="wikilink" title="Tumbuka people">Tumbuka
people</a> group. In southern Zambia, the Tonga are patrons of the
<a href="Kafue_Twa" class="wikilink" title="Kafue Twa">Kafue Twa</a>.

Harold Barclay draws on the Tonga as a key example of a functioning
stateless society in his anthropological survey of anarchy, and their
social structure has attracted sustained attention from anarchist and
anthropological writers. They govern themselves without chiefs or
centralised authority, maintaining order through an intricate web of
kinship obligations, joking relationships, and neighbourhood ties.

## Overview

The Tonga are thought to be Zambia's original Bantu inhabitants,
believed to have arrived from the Great Lakes region around present-day
Kenya and Uganda around 1100 CE.[^1] They share common origins with the
Lenje, Totela, Ila, and Sala peoples. The name **Toka** — used in early
colonial records — is likely a variant transcription of Tonga, while the
**We**, recorded on early maps along the Zambezi River, were also Tonga
people who had acquired a separate name due to their geographical
remoteness.

The Tonga number well over 150,000 on the plateau alone,[^2] with an
estimated 1.38 million Tonga-language speakers in Zambia and 137,000 in
Zimbabwe.[^3] In Zimbabwe they are concentrated in the Binga District,
the Kariba area, and parts of Matabeleland, where the language is called
*tchitonga*.[^4]

## Social Organisation

Harold Barclay draws on the Tonga as a key example of a functioning
stateless society. Their social order rests not on coercive authority
but on overlapping and mutually restraining networks of obligation.

Each Tonga belongs to a **matrilineal clan**, whose members are
scattered across the land. These clans are not corporate groups — living
members never assemble as a group, and they have no leaders. Their
practical functions are to regulate marriage (one cannot marry within
the clan) and to establish joking relationships with several other
clans. As Barclay observes, the joking relationship prescribes that an
individual does not become hostile toward their joking relative,
promoting an "easy-going, light-hearted association" that diffuses
potential conflict.[^5]

Within the clan, each Tonga also belongs to a more localised
**matrilineal group**, which has corporate characteristics: members are
jointly responsible for providing bridewealth, defending one another in
feuds, taking collective vengeance for injuries, and fulfilling mutual
ritual obligations. Inheritance is governed by this group as well.

Beyond kin ties, the Tonga are embedded in **neighbourhood clusters** —
typically four to eight villages — which control land use and the
exploitation of hunting, fishing, and other resources. Neighbourhoods
obligate residents to mutual aid across a wide range of activities and
constitute a local religious congregation, gathering for mourning, rain
prayers, harvest celebrations, and ritual purification after homicides.
An age-grouping system further strengthens ties within a neighbourhood
across generations.

Additional social bonds are formed through **cattle loans**, which
establish new ties while distributing risk across households, and
**brotherhood pacts**, which guarantee peaceful movement and trade
between contracting neighbourhoods.

Villages and neighbourhoods have headmen, and the neighbourhood headman
also serves as priest of the local shrine. But these positions carry no
coercive authority. Barclay notes: "Leadership positions are precarious
in the sense that leaders can readily be abandoned by their followers.
Headmen act as advisors, mediators and coordinators. They might
intervene in a dispute, but... they have no authority to enforce their
views."[^6]

The Tonga never act as a single consolidated unit — no mechanism exists
for such a mobilisation. As Barclay summarises, the central mechanism of
social control is the fact that any given individual belongs to several
different groups, each part of a network of further obligations, so that
any harmful action against one person is restrained by their connections
to others across multiple groups. This "fine mesh of counterbalancing
obligations" renders centralised authority redundant.[^7]

### Conflict Resolution

Feuds between clans and cult neighbourhoods are settled through
agreement to pay damages. The Tonga stress personal restraint as a
social norm. Elizabeth Colson observed that they "attempt to sidestep
issues, are reluctant to allow their fellows to drag them into a
dispute, and try to vanish from the scene if those in their vicinity
seem intent on pursuing a quarrel." Bystanders and supporters apply
gentle pressure, remove potential weapons, and urge de-escalation — not
taking sides, but cooling conflict.[^8]

## Economy

The Tonga are shifting cultivators and cattle-keepers. On the plateau
they raise corn, millet, and sorghum alongside considerable cattle
herds, while Valley Tonga communities along the Zambezi also rely on
fishing.[^9] Population density on the plateau exceeds 60 people per
square mile.[^10] Because of poor soil and the slash-and-burn
cultivation technique, village locations shift regularly, and
individuals move between settlements with relative freedom.

Villages were historically led in their founding by a figure known as
the **Ulaanyika**, whose authority beyond that founding role was
minimal. Of greater social importance was the **Sikatongo**
(rain-maker), who maintained a rain-shrine and organised ceremonies
appealing to ancestors for rainfall — reflecting the centrality of rain
variability to Tonga life.[^11]

## Culture

The annual **Lwiindi** festival is a thanksgiving ceremony held in early
July to acknowledge good rains and harvest, and to maintain relations
with ancestral spirits.

Tobacco and the tobacco pipe hold ceremonial importance among the Tonga.
The 19th-century traveller Emil Holub noted of the Tonga near Victoria
Falls (whom he called the Matoka): "The tobacco pipe plays a very
important role in the life of the Matoka... While they are smoking all
quarrel and strife is suspended and when they invite strangers and
unknown visitors to smoke with them this is a sign of
friendliness."[^12]

Holub also documented a distinctive drum — struck not with a stick but
by rubbing a moistened fibre along an internal rod, producing what he
described as a "deep droning double tone" called the *Namarva* or
*Wupu-Wupu*.[^13]

## Gender

It is a common misconception that matrilineal descent implies gender
equality. The Tonga are matrilineal but not matriarchal. As Barclay
observes: "Reckoning descent through females is not rule by females. Of
the latter there is no record and for matrilineal societies, such as the
Tonga, we find still that men are dominant and have rights and
privileges denied the women."[^14]

Matriliny does afford women more leverage than in patrilineal systems,
since property and status pass through female lines. But Barclay
identifies an inherent tension in matrilineal societies between
inheritance through females and social control by males — a conflict
that historically exerts pressure toward patriliny. Among the Tonga this
is visible in the practice of **virilocal residence**[^15] and in the
considerable influence afforded to the father's kin group, deviating
from a stricter matrilineal model.[^16]

## History

### Origins and Early Settlement

The Tonga are believed to have migrated from the Great Lakes region
around 1100 CE, arriving before many of the great kingdoms of southern
Africa emerged.[^17] Early accounts describe them living in large
villages, moving frequently as soils were exhausted by shifting
cultivation. Inter-village cooperation in field preparation was common
despite the absence of centralised authority.

The Tonga were divided between those living in the Zambezi Valley
(**Valley Tonga**) and those on the higher plateau above. Although
sharing language and broad cultural forms, the two groups had distinct
lifeways and traded occasionally. Valley Tonga on both banks of the
Zambezi regarded those across the river as their near neighbours rather
than those upstream or downstream; those on the southern bank were also
called **Nyai**.[^18]

### Raiding and Disruption (1820s–1899)

The 19th century brought devastating disruption. From the 1820s onward,
a raider known as **Pingola** — of uncertain origin — attacked Tonga
settlements, slaughtering cattle herds that were central to bridewealth,
ritual, and sustenance.[^19]

Shortly after, the **Kololo** — a people displaced northward from
southern Africa by the Zulu wars — settled near present-day Kalomo and
raided Tonga cattle over several years before moving westward. The
**Matabele** (Ndebele), established around present-day Bulawayo under
Mzilikazi and later Lobengula, conducted raids across the Zambezi
repeatedly until the 1890s. In 1855, when David Livingstone encountered
Tonga communities, he found them living in small scattered hamlets — a
contraction from the large villages that elders recalled — and noted the
absence of cattle, replaced only by chickens, sheep, and goats.[^20]

Refugees fleeing Matabele raids south of the Zambezi also arrived in
large numbers, all of whom the Tonga absorbed and sheltered.

The slave trade added a further dimension of violence. Chikunda raiders
operating from river islands captured enslaved people for transport
downriver to Portuguese buyers. One of the most notorious, **Kanyemba**,
maintained his own armed force and trading posts extending far into the
interior; Frederick Selous encountered him in 1877.[^21]

### Slavery Among the Precolonial Tonga

It should be noted that a form of slavery or pawnship existed among the
Tonga before and during the colonial period. Tim Matthews writes that it
"probably existed among the Tonga as it did among many other Central
African peoples long before the stimulus of external trade." People were
enslaved for serious offences or impoverishment due to famine, and
slaves could be given to settle a blood debt. Matthews notes, however,
that "Tonga slaves were in fact simply absorbed into their owner's
lineages and homesteads, where their labors paralleled everyone else's"
— suggesting that the institution, while real, did not produce the sharp
economic stratification associated with plantation slavery.[^22]

### Colonial Period

British administration, established in 1899 under the British South
Africa Company, brought a different set of impositions. Census-taking
for tax purposes required the administrators to assign chiefs — a
category the Tonga did not use. The only figure with something
approaching wider recognition was **Chief Monze**, famed as a Sikatongo
(rain-maker). British officials travelled from hamlet to hamlet
appointing chiefs and ordering the consolidation of small settlements
into taxable villages of at least ten taxpayers.[^23]

Men were recruited or coerced as migrant labour for mines and farms in
Southern Rhodesia. When the railway was constructed through Tongaland in
1905, Tonga men were forced to work on it without pay, after which much
of the adjacent land was allocated to white-owned farms.[^24]

### The Kariba Displacement

The most catastrophic event in modern Tonga history was the construction
of the
<a href="Kariba_Dam" class="wikilink" title="Kariba Dam">Kariba Dam</a>
in 1957, which created what was then the largest reservoir in the world.
Approximately 50,000 Valley Tonga were forcibly displaced from their
homes along the Zambezi to higher ground on the dry plateau. Their
shrines and burial sites were submerged as the waters rose. The land
allocated to them in exchange lacked access to a perennial river — a
serious blow to communities whose agriculture, fishing, and spiritual
practices were oriented around the Zambezi.[^25]

## Anarchist Analysis

The Tonga represent a practical demonstration of what Barclay calls a
society that has turned "chiefs, as well as centralised authority and
integration, into redundancies."[^26] Their social order shows that
large populations across a considerable territory can maintain peace,
resolve disputes, organise production, and sustain complex cultural life
without a state apparatus.

The mechanisms they use — cross-cutting kinship obligations, joking
relationships, neighbourhood mutual aid, cattle loans, brotherhood pacts
— do not require any single authority to function. Each individual's
embeddedness in multiple overlapping groups means that potential
aggressors face restraint from several directions simultaneously. This
is not a society free of conflict, but a practical system for managing
conflict without hierarchy.

At the same time, the Tonga example raises questions that anarchists
must take seriously: the persistence of gender inequality under a
formally non-hierarchical system, and the existence of slavery as an
institution, both complicate any straightforward celebration of Tonga
society as anarchist in the full sense. A genuine anarchism must address
domination in all its forms — not only the state.

## Languages

The <a href="Tonga_language_(Zambia)" class="wikilink"
title="Tonga language">Tonga language</a> is spoken by approximately
1.38 million people in Zambia and 137,000 in Zimbabwe.[^27] It is
unrelated to the Tonga dialect of
<a href="Tumbuka_language" class="wikilink"
title="Chitumbuka">Chitumbuka</a> spoken in Malawi, which belongs to a
different branch of the Bantu language family. In Zimbabwe the language
is known as *tchitonga*.[^28]
