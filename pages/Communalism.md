# Communalism

## Overview

Communalism is a political theory developed by
<a href="Murray_Bookchin" class="wikilink"
title="Murray Bookchin">Murray Bookchin</a> during the 1990s, proposing
a confederation of directly democratic municipal assemblies as the
foundation for a post-capitalist society. Bookchin presented it as the
institutional and political expression of his broader philosophy of
<a href="social_ecology" class="wikilink" title="social ecology">social
ecology</a>, and eventually as a distinct ideology separate from
anarchism — a break he made explicit in 1999.

The theory sits in contested territory within libertarian socialist
thought. Its proponents regard it as a practical framework for building
dual power against the state, grounded in face-to-face democracy and
confederal organisation. Its critics — including many anarchists — argue
that its reliance on majority rule, municipal elections, and binding
collective decisions places it outside the anarchist tradition, and that
Bookchin's late-career attacks on anarchism were both historically
inaccurate and politically damaging.

## Origins

Bookchin began developing what he called
<a href="libertarian_municipalism" class="wikilink"
title="libertarian municipalism">libertarian municipalism</a> from the
1980s onward, rooted in his concept of social ecology — the argument
that ecological destruction and social hierarchy are inseparable, and
that both must be confronted together. His vision drew on the
participatory institutions of the
<a href="Paris_Commune" class="wikilink" title="Paris Commune">Paris
Commune</a> of 1871 and, more controversially, the democratic *sections*
of the French Revolution of 1789–94, which he regarded as the closest
historical approximation to his ideal of popular self-governance through
neighbourhood assemblies.[^1]

Bookchin was critical of what he saw as a retreat in contemporary
anarchism toward personal autonomy, lifestyle politics, and
anti-rationalism — a tendency he labelled
"<a href="lifestyle_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="lifestyle anarchism">lifestyle anarchism</a>." Against this he
counterposed what he called "social anarchism," and eventually
communalism, which he defined using the *American Heritage Dictionary*
as "a theory or system of government in which virtually autonomous local
communities are loosely bound in a federation."[^2] He acknowledged the
dictionary's use of the word "government" but argued the term could be
stripped of its statist connotations.

The naming of the tendency as Communalism — capitalised to distinguish
it from looser uses of the word — was itself deliberate. Bookchin argued
the term had not been historically tainted by association with either
Stalinist communism or bourgeois liberalism, and that it could carry the
weight of what he called the "Commune of communes": a confederation of
self-governing municipalities replacing both the nation-state and
capitalist social relations.[^3]

In 1999, Bookchin publicly broke with anarchism entirely, subsequently
writing increasingly hostile critiques of the movement he had spent
decades working within. He argued that anarchism was "not a social
theory" but rather an expression of liberal individualism, ill-suited to
modern urban and industrial conditions.[^4] This break was marked by
significant historical revisionism, with Bookchin now describing a
movement he had previously championed in largely negative terms.

## Core Principles

Communalism rests on several interlocking ideas. The basic unit of
political life is the municipality — the town, city neighbourhood, or
village — governed through face-to-face citizens' assemblies in which
all residents participate directly. These assemblies make policy
decisions by majority vote, with mandated and recallable delegates (not
representatives) coordinating between municipalities through a
confederation. Administration is carried out by these confederal bodies,
but sovereignty remains with the local assemblies.

Crucially for Bookchin, communalism extends democratic control to the
economy. Rather than either private ownership or workers'
self-management of individual enterprises, it proposes the
*municipalisation* of production — bringing the means of life under the
authority of the local assembly, which would determine how they are used
for the benefit of the whole community.[^5] This distinguishes
communalism from both market socialism and from the collectivist
tradition within anarchism, which tends to favour workplace control by
the workers themselves.

Bookchin was explicit that communalism required engagement with existing
political structures, including standing candidates in municipal
elections. He argued this did not constitute participation in the state,
drawing an analogy with anarchosyndicalists organising within capitalist
workplaces without thereby endorsing capitalism.[^6] The goal was to use
electoral presence to build and legitimise popular assemblies, which
would then develop into a genuine dual power against the state.

## Relationship to Anarchism and Libertarian Socialism

### Points of Convergence

Bookchin's communalism shares significant ground with the broader
anarchist and libertarian socialist tradition, and many of its core
institutional proposals — confederalism, mandated delegates, recall,
face-to-face decision-making — have deep roots in that tradition.
<a href="Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Kropotkin">Kropotkin</a>
discussed "communalism" in *Words of a Rebel*, describing the coming
revolution as both communalist and communist in character. The sections
of the French Revolution that Bookchin cites were also analysed at
length by Kropotkin in *The Great French Revolution*, where he concluded
that the principles of anarchism dated from 1789 and arose not from
theory but from the practice of popular self-organisation.[^7]

<a href="Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a>'s vision
of social revolution also centred on a confederation of insurgent
communes, with delegates "invested with binding mandates and accountable
and revocable at all times" — a formulation strikingly close to
Bookchin's own.[^8] The argument that anarchists have always recognised
the need for popular assemblies and confederal structures is
well-supported historically, which makes Bookchin's late claim that
anarchism is inherently hostile to organised social life difficult to
sustain.

For libertarian socialists who do not share the more absolute
anti-statist commitments of communist anarchism, communalism offers a
coherent framework for transitional politics: building
counter-institutions, challenging the state from within existing
municipal structures, and developing popular political capacity. The
emphasis on ecology and the integration of social and environmental
questions also represents a genuine contribution to socialist thought
that retains value independently of the communalism/anarchism dispute.

### Points of Divergence

The central disagreement turns on majority rule and enforceability.
Bookchin defended majority voting as the only equitable means of
collective decision-making for large groups, and was explicit that
communalism required binding decisions — including the striking
provision that member communities of a confederation "may withdraw only
with the approval of the confederation as a whole."[^9] For most
anarchists, this crosses a clear line: the right of free association
necessarily includes the right to disassociate, and a confederation that
cannot be left is not meaningfully voluntary.

More fundamentally, anarchism rejects the enforcement of
collectively-made decisions on dissenters as itself a form of hierarchy
— regardless of whether those decisions were reached through democratic
process. As Uri Gordon argues in *Anarchy Alive!*, democratic discourse
assumes that political process produces collectively binding decisions,
and it is precisely this binding character that distinguishes democracy
from anarchist process, in which outcomes are matters of consultation
and arrangement rather than enforceable mandates.[^10] On this view,
communalism is not a more organised or rigorous anarchism but a
qualitatively different political paradigm.

The distinction becomes clearer when communalism is set against the
federalist tradition that most
<a href="anarcho-communism" class="wikilink"
title="anarcho-communists">anarcho-communists</a> do accept. Thinkers
from <a href="Proudhon" class="wikilink" title="Proudhon">Proudhon</a>
and <a href="Bakunin" class="wikilink" title="Bakunin">Bakunin</a>
onward envisioned free federation as a relationship between voluntary
associations — groups that come together because their members choose
to, and that retain the unconditional right to withdraw. Delegates to
federal bodies carry imperative mandates from their base, can be
recalled at any time, and have no authority beyond what has been
specifically delegated to them. Crucially, the federation has no
coercive power over its constituent parts: a minority that disagrees
with a decision is free to act otherwise, to argue for reversal, or to
leave. This is federation as coordination rather than governance.
Bookchin's confederalism looks superficially similar but differs at
precisely this point — member communities cannot withdraw without the
approval of the confederation as a whole, delegates exercise something
closer to allocated power than a strict mandate, and majority decisions
carry a binding character that dissenters cannot simply refuse.[^11]
Where anarchist federation is a horizontal network held together by
agreement, communalist confederation is a polity held together by
democratic authority — a meaningful difference, even if both reject the
centralised nation-state.

The municipal elections question also generates significant anarchist
criticism. Iain McKay notes that the reformist nature of electoral
engagement is likely to produce exactly the "morass of compromises"
Bookchin himself warned against — the history of left parties at local
and national level offering ample evidence that participation in
electoral politics tends to absorb rather than radicalise
movements.[^12] Bookchin's own account is contradictory here: he states
that communalism does not seek to "legislate" a new society into
existence, but also describes communalist elected representatives using
their office to legislate popular assemblies into being.[^13]

The question of workers' control is a further point of friction.
Bookchin's municipalisation model removes economic decision-making from
workers in specific enterprises and lodges it in the broader community
assembly. While this avoids the "collective capitalism" problem he
rightly identified in some self-managed enterprises — where collectives
competed with each other rather than socialising production — it also
removes direct control from those doing the work. The Spanish anarchists
of the CNT were aware of the tension between collectivisation and full
socialisation and consistently argued for the latter; Bookchin's
solution moves in a different direction that many anarchists find less
satisfying.[^14]

### Bookchin's Break and Its Legacy

Bookchin's late-career attacks on anarchism were widely regarded as
historically inaccurate and politically destructive. His
characterisation of anarchism as "the most extreme formulation of
liberalism's ideology of unfettered autonomy" contradicts not only the
classical anarchist tradition but his own earlier writings, in which he
explicitly defended anarchism against similar charges from
Marxists.[^15] Bakunin and Kropotkin were consistently critical of
bourgeois individualism; the tradition Bookchin now identified as
"authentic" anarchism — consensus-based, lifestyle-oriented,
anti-rationalist — was a minority current, not the movement's historical
core.

The schism Bookchin manufactured between "lifestyle anarchism" and
social anarchism, while addressing real tensions, did significant damage
by providing ideological ammunition for the marginalisation of
individualist and anti-civilisation currents within broader anarchist
spaces. As ziq argues, individualist anarchists are not incapable of
both living anarchically in the present and organising for systemic
change — the dichotomy Bookchin erected was largely false.\<[^16] Many
who followed Bookchin's communalist path subsequently abandoned
anarchism altogether, a trajectory that says something about the
long-term political direction his framework pointed.

## Communalism, Libertarian Municipalism, and Democratic Confederalism

These three terms are closely related and often used interchangeably,
but have distinct emphases.

**<a href="Libertarian_Municipalism" class="wikilink"
title="Libertarian Municipalism">Libertarian Municipalism</a>** is the
earlier name Bookchin used for essentially the same politics — the
strategy of building popular assemblies through municipal engagement,
confederating them, and developing them as a dual power to the state.
Communalism became his preferred term after his break with anarchism,
partly to distinguish the project from what he now regarded as
anarchism's theoretical inadequacy.

**<a href="Democratic_Confederalism" class="wikilink"
title="Democratic Confederalism">Democratic Confederalism</a>** is the
term used by <a href="Abdullah_Öcalan" class="wikilink"
title="Abdullah Öcalan">Abdullah Öcalan</a> for the political framework
he developed while imprisoned from 1999 onward, explicitly drawing on
Bookchin's libertarian municipalism. It is the theoretical basis for the
structures that have been implemented in
<a href="Rojava" class="wikilink" title="Rojava">Rojava</a> (northern
Syria) since 2012. Democratic Confederalism shares communalism's core
commitments — municipal assemblies, confederal organisation, ecology,
and the rejection of the nation-state as a political horizon — but
places greater emphasis on gender liberation as a foundational principle
(<a href="jineology" class="wikilink" title="jineology">jineology</a>)
and operates within the specific context of Kurdish political struggle
and the Middle East.[^17]

The Rojava experience is significant for evaluating communalist politics
in practice. It demonstrates that the broad framework can be implemented
and can produce genuinely different social relations — particularly
regarding women's autonomy and grassroots organisation — under
conditions of extreme adversity. It also demonstrates the limits of any
such project when surrounded by hostile state and non-state forces, and
the compromises that military necessity and realpolitik impose on any
movement operating within the existing world order. The relationship
between the institutional forms of Democratic Confederalism and the
PKK's own organisational culture (which retains significant hierarchical
and vanguardist elements) is a subject of ongoing debate within and
beyond the Kurdish liberation movement.

## Criticism

### From Anarchism

The most sustained anarchist critique of communalism — beyond the
majority-rule and enforceability questions discussed above — concerns
Bookchin's positive vision of Western civilisation and the city. His
fondness for classical Athenian democracy as a reference point sits
uneasily with the facts of Athenian slavery, patriarchy, and the
restriction of citizenship to a narrow male elite. Green anarchists and
anti-civilisation tendencies point out that the urban civilisation
Bookchin sought to democratise is itself the primary driver of
ecological destruction, and that no amount of institutional reform at
the municipal level will change the material throughput requirements of
industrial urban life.[^18]

Bookchin's concept of "post-scarcity" — the claim that modern technology
has already produced the material basis for a society without want — is
particularly contested from an ecological perspective. Resources are not
post-scarce; they are being depleted at accelerating rates. The
technologies Bookchin pointed to as liberatory (solar, wind,
cybernation) depend on global supply chains, ecologically destructive
mining, and manufacturing processes whose costs fall disproportionately
on the Global South.[^19] The claim that these technologies become clean
once placed under democratic rather than capitalist control does not
address their material requirements.

### From Libertarian Communism

Miguel G. Gómez of Regeneración Libertaria raises a different set of
criticisms from within the broader libertarian communist tradition.
Communalism tends to speak from a romanticised rural perspective that
does not correspond to the actual conditions of either contemporary
villages or cities. Rural economies today are not self-sufficient
agricultural communities but are integrated into neoliberal capitalism
through tourism, car dependency, and market relations. Communalist
projects — however valuable as experiments in better living — do not in
themselves constitute an alternative to capitalism as a systemic model;
they are alternatives to urban capitalism via a mix of rural capitalism,
cooperativism, and informal exchange.[^20]

More seriously, communalism risks becoming a politics of withdrawal
rather than confrontation. The working-class urban neighbourhoods that
will ultimately determine any balance of power between emancipatory
movements and the capitalist state tend not to feature in communalist
strategy, which focuses on small towns and rural communities. The left's
abandonment of working-class urban spaces has created vacuums now
occupied by evangelical churches, far-right movements, and authoritarian
populism. A politics adequate to this situation requires not a retreat
to the village but engagement with the city, which means developing
organisation, class analysis, and programmatic politics beyond the
local.[^21]

Gómez also notes that communalism lacks a serious theory of how it would
survive under capitalist and fascist pressure. Eco-villages and
liberated zones can be outlawed with a parliamentary vote; the political
battle that determines whether communalist experiments are tolerated or
crushed takes place in the cities and at the level of mass movements,
not in the assemblies of small communities.[^22]

### Internal Tensions

McKay identifies a structural problem in Bookchin's own framework: the
distinction he draws between "government" and "state" — where collective
self-management constitutes the former but not the latter — relies on a
semantic shift that does not resolve the underlying question of
authority. If a confederation's decisions are binding and its member
communities cannot leave without majority approval, then the
confederation exercises authority over its members regardless of what it
is called. Bookchin's attempt to distance communalism from both
anarchism's "anti-rationalism" and Marxism's authoritarianism ends up
reproducing a form of the latter at the confederal level.[^23]
