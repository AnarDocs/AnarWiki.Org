## Overview

Published on 14 February 1793, ***Enquiry Concerning Political Justice
and its Influence on Morals and Happiness*** is widely recognised as the
first modern articulation of
<a href="anarchism" class="wikilink" title="anarchism">anarchism</a>.
<a href="William_Godwin" class="wikilink" title="William Godwin">William
Godwin</a> began thinking through its arguments in 1791, prompted by the
debate between
<a href="Thomas_Paine" class="wikilink" title="Thomas Paine">Thomas
Paine</a>'s
*<a href="Rights_of_Man" class="wikilink" title="Rights of Man">Rights of
Man</a>* and
<a href="Edmund_Burke" class="wikilink" title="Edmund Burke">Edmund
Burke</a>'s
*<a href="Reflections_on_the_Revolution_in_France" class="wikilink"
title="Reflections on the Revolution in France">Reflections on the
Revolution in France</a>* — though unlike the polemicists on either
side, Godwin was less interested in the specific events of the day than
in the underlying philosophical principles. Its length and expense (over
£1) kept it out of mass circulation, which probably also kept Godwin out
of the dock at a time when radicals like Paine faced prosecution.
Nevertheless, the book spread through unauthorised copies, radical
journals, and lectures by
<a href="John_Thelwall" class="wikilink" title="John Thelwall">John
Thelwall</a>, making Godwin a central intellectual figure for the
movement.[^1]

## Summary

*Political Justice* argues that humanity is capable of indefinite moral
and rational improvement — and that the primary obstacle to that
improvement is government itself. Godwin held that government
"insinuates itself into our personal dispositions, and insensibly
communicates its own spirit to our private transactions."[^2] As McCann
explains, "as public opinion develops in accordance with the dictates of
reason, so too should political institutions change until, finally, they
will wither away altogether, leaving the people to organize themselves
into what would be a direct democracy."[^3] Property monopoly, marriage,
and monarchy all appeared in his analysis as restraints on human
progress rather than natural features of social life.

The book opens with eight principles, which together form its
philosophical foundation. Godwin argues that moral and political life
should aim at maximising happiness; that government, despite originating
as a response to injustice, has become a perpetuator of it; that
security purchased through the abridgement of individual freedom is too
high a price; that justice requires impartiality and is universal in
scope; that individuals have both a duty to contribute to the general
good and a right to share in it; that reason operates not by overriding
feeling but by allowing feelings to be compared and balanced; that
knowledge — and therefore social improvement — is in principle
unlimited; and that freedom of inquiry must be protected against
institutions designed to freeze any particular mode of thought in
place.[^4]

Godwin was not a revolutionary in the mode of the
<a href="London_Corresponding_Society" class="wikilink"
title="London Corresponding Society">London Corresponding Society</a>. A
<a href="philosophical_anarchism" class="wikilink"
title="philosophical anarchist">philosophical anarchist</a>, he believed
that change would come through persuasion rather than insurrection —
through "enquiry, communication, discussion" rather than violence.[^5]
This faith in rational public opinion was, however, not without its own
tensions. Godwin simultaneously distrusted public speeches for appealing
to sentiment and the printing press for its capacity to perpetuate dogma
as much as to enlighten — an unease with collective action that sits
awkwardly alongside his vision of a self-organising society.

Three editions appeared in Godwin's lifetime — 1793, 1796, and 1798 —
each reflecting his ongoing revisions. The trajectory was generally away
from pure reason and toward a greater acknowledgement of human feeling.
<a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Kropotkin">Peter
Kropotkin</a>, writing on anarchism for the *Encyclopædia Britannica*,
criticised the revisions sharply, arguing that Godwin had retreated from
his most radical positions on property: the first edition had argued
that goods must go to whoever most needs them, a conclusion Kropotkin
read as communist, and which Godwin substantially softened in the
second.[^6]

## Critiques

The most immediate counterattack came from the Reverend
<a href="Thomas_Malthus" class="wikilink" title="Thomas Malthus">Thomas
Malthus</a>, whose
*<a href="An_Essay_on_the_Principle_of_Population" class="wikilink"
title="An Essay on the Principle of Population">An Essay on the
Principle of Population</a>* (1798) was written largely as a refutation
of Godwin and the <a href="Marquis_de_Condorcet" class="wikilink"
title="Marquis de Condorcet">Marquis de Condorcet</a>. Malthus argued
that population grows geometrically while production grows only
linearly, making disease, famine, and poverty inevitable — and Godwin's
vision of human perfectibility a fantasy. Godwin responded in 1820 with
*Of Population*, disputing Malthus's predictions.[^7]

Kropotkin, by contrast, placed Godwin at the origin of the anarchist
tradition, crediting him as "the first to formulate the political and
economical conceptions of anarchism" — while noting that Godwin himself
never used the word. The critique from within that tradition has focused
on the retreats of the later editions and on Godwin's residual
individualism, which left little account of collective organisation
beyond the gradual spread of rational opinion.
