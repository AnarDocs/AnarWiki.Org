# Positive And Negative Freedom

## Overview

The division between **negative** and **positive** freedoms is a
philosophical distinction made famous by
<a href="Isaiah_Berlin" class="wikilink" title="Isaiah Berlin">Isaiah
Berlin</a> in his 1958 lecture *Two Concepts of Liberty*. Negative
freedom is glossed as freedom *from* interference, while positive
freedom is glossed as freedom *to* do or become something. The
distinction has been used, particularly by
<a href="Liberalism" class="wikilink" title="liberals">liberals</a> and
<a href="Right-Libertarianism" class="wikilink"
title="right-libertarians">right-libertarians</a>, to elevate
non-interference as the only ‘real’ kind of liberty while dismissing
material and social conditions as either irrelevant to freedom or
actively hostile to it.

Anarchists have generally rejected the division as it is conventionally
drawn. Where it is treated as analytically useful, the two halves are
understood as inseparable: freedom from hunger is empty without freedom
to eat, and freedom to associate is empty without freedom from coercion.
More often, anarchists treat the distinction itself as ideological — a
way of naturalising the property relations and state institutions that
constitute much of what passes for ‘negative’ freedom, while framing any
challenge to those relations as an imposition on liberty.

## Origins

The contrast between two senses of liberty long predates Berlin.
Anarchists were arguing about it in the nineteenth century without using
his terms.
<a href="Emma_Goldman" class="wikilink" title="Emma Goldman">Emma
Goldman</a> put the anarchist objection to a purely negative conception
bluntly:

> True liberty... is not the negative thing of being free from
> something, because with such freedom you may starve to death. Real
> freedom, true liberty is positive: it is freedom to something; it is
> the liberty to be, to do; in short, the liberty of actual and active
> opportunity.[^1]

<a href="Peter_Kropotkin" class="wikilink" title="Peter Kropotkin">Peter
Kropotkin</a> made the same point from the other direction in
*<a href="The_Conquest_of_Bread" class="wikilink"
title="The Conquest of Bread">The Conquest of Bread</a>*, rejecting the
formulae of bourgeois and statist socialism alike:

> All is for all! If the man and the woman bear their fair share of
> work, they have a right to their fair share of all that is produced by
> all, and that share is enough to secure them well-being. No more of
> such vague formulas as "The right to work," or "To each the whole
> result of his labour." What we proclaim is The Right to Well-Being:
> Well-Being for All![^2]

<a href="Pierre-Joseph_Proudhon" class="wikilink"
title="Pierre-Joseph Proudhon">Pierre-Joseph Proudhon</a> had earlier
insisted that anarchy is a *positive* condition — not the absence of
order but its realisation. In *On the Solution of the Social Problem*
(1848) he wrote that ‘The Republic is a positive anarchy \[…\] it is the
liberty that is the MOTHER, not the daughter, of order’.[^3] Freedom,
for Proudhon, was constituted in shared institutions rather than secured
by withdrawal from them.

Berlin codified the distinction in *Two Concepts of Liberty* as a Cold
War polemic, contrasting Anglo-American liberalism with Soviet
<a href="Marxism–Leninism" class="wikilink"
title="Marxism–Leninism">Marxism–Leninism</a>. The framing has since
hardened into a standard move in political philosophy, with negative
liberty treated as the safe, default conception and positive liberty as
a slippery slope to authoritarianism. Anarchist writers have been
picking apart this framing ever since.

## Tendencies

### The interpenetration of freedoms

The starkest anarchist critique is simply that the two halves of the
distinction cannot be separated in practice. Each ‘freedom from’
requires a corresponding ‘freedom to’, and each ‘freedom to’ requires a
corresponding ‘freedom from’.

Freedom from hunger requires freedom to access food. Freedom from
homelessness requires freedom to access shelter. Freedom from coercion
requires freedom to meet one’s basic needs without depending on a boss
or landlord. Freedom from exploitation requires the freedom to refuse
work and walk away. Freedom from ignorance requires freedom to learn.
Freedom from isolation requires freedom to associate.

The same holds in reverse. Freedom to learn requires freedom from
censorship and information control. Freedom to create requires freedom
from exploitation. Freedom to associate requires freedom from forced
unity. Freedom to be healthy requires freedom from environmental
destruction. Freedom to contribute requires freedom from the constant
pressure of mere survival. Freedom to develop as a person requires
freedom from oppression.

This is not merely a list of pairings. The point is that any freedom
worth having involves both removing constraints and opening
possibilities, and any attempt to honour one half while ignoring the
other collapses on inspection. A person stranded on a desert island with
no shelter, food or water enjoys an almost total absence of
interference; few would call them free.

### The circularity of ‘non-interference’

Negative freedom presents itself as a baseline that owes nothing to
anyone, but
<a href="Kevin_Carson" class="wikilink" title="Kevin Carson">Kevin
Carson</a> and others have shown that this neutrality is illusory.
Whether a given act counts as ‘interference’ depends entirely on a prior
distribution of property, and property rules are not given by nature. As
Carson puts it, the right-libertarian framing ‘obscures power relations
behind the myth of voluntary exchange and freedom of contract’.[^4]

The modern enclosure of land, the violent dispossession of commoners,
the colonial expropriation of whole continents — these were the
preconditions for the property regime whose defenders now describe its
protection as a purely ‘negative’ matter. Protecting that property
requires courts, police, prisons, surveillance, contract enforcement,
registries and a military. All of that is the positive provision of
services by people, funded by taxation and backed by force. To call this
‘negative’ is to naturalise a vast and ongoing intervention while
treating the demand for housing or healthcare as an alien imposition.

The same point can be made by rephrasing the rights involved.
<a href="James_P._Sterba" class="wikilink" title="James P. Sterba">James
P. Sterba</a> notes that the so-called positive right of the poor to
subsistence can equally be stated as a negative right of
non-interference: ‘the liberty of the poor not to be interfered with in
taking from the surplus possessions of the rich what is necessary to
satisfy their basic needs.’ Whether a given freedom appears ‘negative’
or ‘positive’ turns out to depend on which background distribution one
treats as natural.

### Freedom in, freedom with

<a href="Alex_Prichard" class="wikilink" title="Alex Prichard">Alex
Prichard</a> has argued that anarchists have always operated with a
third conception alongside negative and positive freedom: freedom *in*
or freedom *with*. This is the freedom constituted by participation in
shared institutions — unions, federations, communes, assemblies — that
members shape collectively. On this view freedom is neither a state of
isolation nor a basket of guaranteed goods, but a quality of
relationships sustained by the rules and practices a community gives
itself.

This is consistent with Proudhon’s
<a href="Federalism" class="wikilink" title="federalism">federalism</a>,
Bakunin’s insistence that ‘society is not the product of individuals
comprising it’ but the other way round, and the long history of
anarcho-syndicalist organising. It is also why anarchist communities
have so often produced elaborate procedures and written agreements,
despite the caricature of anarchism as opposed to all structure. As
<a href="Jo_Freeman" class="wikilink" title="Jo Freeman">Jo Freeman</a>’s
critique of structurelessness reminded the women’s movement, the absence
of formal procedure does not abolish power; it just hides it.

<a href="William_Gillis" class="wikilink" title="William Gillis">William
Gillis</a> makes a related argument from a different angle. Negative
freedom understood as mere non-interference ‘lacks a coherent analysis
of how the restriction of options can impact one’s liberty’.[^5] A wall
has the same effect as a chain. Freedom is the expansion of what is
possible, and possibility is not a zero-sum good. ‘Your freedom is my
freedom,’ Gillis writes, ‘because freedom tolerates no divisions,
accepts no adjectives, belongs to no one.’

### The forced-labour objection

A standard objection to ‘positive’ freedoms — that guaranteeing access
to healthcare, housing or food means compelling someone to provide them
— is built on the assumption that resources must be privately owned and
services must be sold. Once those assumptions are dropped, the objection
loses most of its force.

Most medical workers, teachers and carers want to do their work; what
wears them down is the bureaucratic and profit-driven structure that
surrounds it. Doctors are already rushed through quotas, patients are
already saddled with medical debt that resembles indentured servitude,
and millions of people are already forced into exploitative jobs for the
sake of health insurance. The current system does not avoid coercion; it
produces a great deal of it, and then describes the alternative as
coercive.

<a href="Mutual_aid" class="wikilink" title="Mutual aid">Mutual aid</a>
arrangements,
<a href="The_Commons" class="wikilink" title="commons">commons</a>
regimes and federated
<a href="Workers&#39;_self-management" class="wikilink"
title="self-managed">self-managed</a> services demonstrate that the
contribution of labour need not depend on either market discipline or
state command. The Spanish collectives, the Makhnovist agricultural
communes, the medical infrastructure of
<a href="Rojava" class="wikilink" title="Rojava">Rojava</a> and
countless smaller experiments have all shown that people contribute when
the conditions for doing so are not poisoned. The ‘forced labour’
objection ends up defending the existing system of forced labour by
projecting its features onto the alternatives.

### Anarchist scepticism about rights

A separate strand of anarchist thought, drawing on writers like
<a href="Costas_Douzinas" class="wikilink"
title="Costas Douzinas">Costas Douzinas</a>,
<a href="Jacques_Rancière" class="wikilink"
title="Jacques Rancière">Jacques Rancière</a> and
<a href="Wendy_Brown" class="wikilink" title="Wendy Brown">Wendy
Brown</a>, questions whether the language of rights — whether positive
or negative — is the right framework at all. Rights are conferred by
states and codified by laws, which means they presuppose the very
apparatus anarchists oppose. They sublimate political struggle into
legal demand, channel resistance into juridical forms and convert the
‘part of no part’ into petitioners. Where they are defended, it is
largely the negative rights of property and contract; where positive
rights are written into constitutions, their material delivery is
typically thin or tokenistic, with their main function being to
legitimate the state that nominally guarantees them.

From this angle the negative/positive distinction is a quarrel within
rights discourse, and the more interesting question is whether anarchist
freedom should be articulated in those terms at all. The reply common
among constructive anarchists is that freedom is better understood as a
quality of relationships, sustained by institutions of mutual agreement,
rather than as a list of entitlements conferred from above.

## Criticism

The clearest defenders of a sharp negative/positive distinction are
<a href="right-libertarians" class="wikilink"
title="right-libertarians">right-libertarians</a>, who hold that only
negative freedoms are genuine and that positive freedoms collapse into
compelled service.
<a href="Jan_Narveson" class="wikilink" title="Jan Narveson">Jan
Narveson</a> has argued that the distinction is ‘quite robust’ even
after one concedes that protection of negative rights requires
enforcement, on the grounds that enforcement is a separate question from
whether the right exists.[^6] Anarchist replies have generally accepted
that the analytical distinction can be drawn but denied that it tracks
anything morally fundamental, since which freedoms count as negative and
which as positive shifts with whatever property regime one assumes.

A different criticism comes from within the anarchist tradition.
<a href="Uri_Gordon" class="wikilink" title="Uri Gordon">Uri Gordon</a>
has resisted the institutionalist reading offered by Prichard and
others, arguing that durable formal rules tend to ossify into new
hierarchies and that anarchist politics is better understood through
empowerment, autonomy and the cultivation of ‘power-with’ rather than
through written constitutions. Prichard and
<a href="Ruth_Kinna" class="wikilink" title="Ruth Kinna">Ruth Kinna</a>
reply that without institutionalisation, anarchist communities tend to
dissolve when their founding members tire or move on, and that informal
arrangements simply hide power rather than abolishing it. The
disagreement runs through contemporary anarchist debate about
organisation and continues to shape how the question of freedom is
posed.

A final criticism is that the negative/positive frame, even when handled
carefully, distracts from what is actually at stake. As one contributor
on r/Anarchy101 put it, the categories ‘are just analytical categories
most often used to distract from the fact that someone is compromising
your ability to meet your needs for no good reason’[^7]. The substantive
question is who is preventing whom from living, and why; the
philosophical taxonomy is incidental.
